# Writing with Style: Conversations on the Art of Writing
## Summary of Chapter 7: Readability (Third Edition, John R. Trimble)

This document distills every actionable writing principle from the excerpt (pages 58-75) of Trimble's *Writing with Style*. It is intended as a primary reference for an AI editor persona improving prose quality in technical proposals.

---

## 1. The Philosophical Foundation: Why Readability Matters

### 1.1 What Makes Writing Readable

Trimble defines a readable style as "one that invites reading." He requires two things of any author:

1. **Have something fresh to say** -- something that teaches, amuses, or both. If the writer has nothing new, the reader stops.
2. **Don't waste the reader's time** -- get to the point. If the writer idles, the reader leaves.

Beyond these bedrock requirements, Trimble most values **authenticity**. He wants the writer to come across as "a vital, companionable human being, not a stuffed shirt or emotionally unfeeling." He wants prose that *talks* to the reader -- natural, conversational rhythm forged from "homespun English rather than highbrow English," stamped with personality, carrying "the ring of honesty and passionate conviction."

**Key quote (Pascal):** "When we encounter a natural style, we are astonished and delighted; for we expected to see an author, and we find a man."

**Key quote (Frost):** "Sentences are not different enough to hold the attention unless they are dramatic. No ingenuity of varying structure will do. All that can save them is the speaking tone of voice somehow entangled in the words and fastened to the page for the ear of the imagination."

### 1.2 Authenticity Over Perfectionism

Trimble quotes Anne Lamott extensively to warn against perfectionism: "Perfectionism is the voice of the oppressor, the enemy of the people. It will keep you cramped and insane your whole life, and it is the main obstacle between you and a shitty first draft. Perfectionism will ruin your writing, blocking inventiveness and playfulness and life force."

The principle: **Write freely first, edit ruthlessly later.** Clutter is fertile ground -- you can discover treasures under the piles, clean things up, edit things out. Tidiness in a first draft suggests "held breath, of suspended animation, while writing needs to breathe and move."

Trimble also quotes Dorothy Allison on the connection between authenticity, emotional honesty, and powerful writing -- the kind that pushes people into changing their ideas.

### 1.3 Self-Acceptance as the Root of Voice

Trimble argues that literary authenticity comes from either "a religious reverence for truth" or "genuine self-acceptance." A writer who accepts herself will speak her mind without inhibition, won't posture or counterfeit her personality, because she has "no wobbly idealized self to defend."

The fear of rejection, learned early, drives most writers to mimic others and smother their individuality. Overcoming this is the central challenge of developing a voice.

## 2. The Godlike Pose and the Dogma of Formalism

### 2.1 How Fear Creates Bad Writing

When asked to produce serious writing, fear compels most people to appear "wise, rational, authoritative." Since they cannot actually become more rational on demand, they compensate by adopting "the trappings of pure rationality and authority: studied 'objectivity,' impersonality of address, elevated diction, a grave manner, elaborate sentences." Trimble calls this **"the Godlike Pose."**

This is a learned response reinforced by the popular dogma that "only a lofty, formal style is appropriate in serious writing." That dogma not only strengthens the feeling that we must be something we are not, but teaches us *how* to strike the pose.

### 2.2 How the Dogma Originated

Through thousands of precedents -- people imitating other people's trappings of authority -- the trappings became the established style. Decorum (not just the individual's ego) began insisting on stylistic acceptability. Convention reinforced convention, instinct rigidified into convention: "a vicious circle."

### 2.3 The Actual Effects of Formalism (Both Negative)

1. **It ironically promotes bad writing.** Mencken called it "bow-wow language" -- stilted diction, abstract phraseology, frozen sentence rhythms. Most writers will never be at home with it, and their awkwardness shows. It promotes "phoniness and empty conventionality -- the Standard Way of Thinking."
2. **It creates lifelong imitation habits.** "Businessese," "academese," "officialese," "committee prose" -- all forms of imitation-writing, "sterile in its uniformity, opaque in its jargon, and absurd in its pomposity." People learn to write this way not from instruction to write simply but from being taught "that naturalness is unnatural, that informality is unacceptable, and that individuality is unpardonable."

**Key quote (Orwell):** "Modern writing at its worst does not consist in picking out words for their sake of their meaning and inventing images in order to make the meaning clearer. It consists in gumming together long strips of words which have already been set in order by someone else, and making the results presentable by sheer humbug."

### 2.4 Formalism's Shortcomings

The archly conservative formal style, while capable of satisfying precision and conciseness, "tends to lack ease and freshness, since it inhibits variety of diction, simplicity, and anything offbeat. Its self-consciousness is both its virtue and its limitation."

**Critical exception:** Trimble explicitly concedes that formal style IS sometimes appropriate — specifically for "a legal brief, a statement of corporate policy or a scientific paper." This means an editor applying Trimble's principles to technical proposals must calibrate: the goal is to move toward General English where possible, but some sections (methodology descriptions, quantitative claims, risk analyses) may legitimately require formal register. The editor should push for naturalness in framing, narrative, and argumentation while respecting the technical authority that formal prose provides in data-heavy passages.

## 3. The "General English" Style: The Recommended Middle Way

### 3.1 Definition

Trimble advocates for what language expert Porter Perrin calls **"General English"** -- a middle style that is "essentially a happy compromise between formal and informal." It is "by far the most palatable of the written styles, and its area of appropriateness, at least in the real world, is virtually unlimited."

### 3.2 The Four Essentials of Good Prose

Skilled writers working in General English can satisfy all four:

1. **Precision**
2. **Conciseness**
3. **Ease**
4. **Freshness**

Trimble notes that formal style would be hard-pressed to satisfy all four. This is why General English has been "displacing formal English as the prevailing literary style in recent years."

### 3.3 Character of the Style

Somerset Maugham captured it: "Good prose should resemble the conversation of a well-bred man." The best examples are "conversational in tone -- unaffected, idiomatic, straightforward -- but also beautifully wrought. The phrasing is tight and precise; the diction, fresh and apt. Considerable labor has been lavished on these sentences, we can be sure, not a little of it on concealing that very labor. They all seem happy accidents -- precisely the intended effect."

### 3.4 Why General English Is Hard

It requires "a sophisticated control of *tone*, which is the most intangible but perhaps most consequential element in a writer's voice." It involves mingling contraries: "formal and informal diction, objectivity and subjectivity, impersonality and directness." The challenge is getting the right mix -- "as tricky as concocting a good sweet-and-sour sauce" -- and working around the edges of extremes without taking a tumble.

Questions the writer must navigate: When will a colloquialism lend the right note of easy informality vs. cheapen a sentence? When will an unusual word add piquant effect vs. sound pretentious? When will a personal touch be welcome vs. narcissistic? "Guessing right requires a good ear, taste, and tact -- all of them intuitive, and acquired only through considerable reading and writing."

### 3.5 Contractions: A Case Study in Formalism vs. General English

Trimble rejects the common textbook dictum that contractions are "appropriate in informal and colloquial styles but not in a formal style." This postulates only two kinds of style and "implies that only the latter is legitimate for serious writing." In General English, contractions are a normal, useful tool.

### 3.6 Closing Thoughts on Style as a Moral Choice

Trimble frames style as a moral issue. He quotes at length a passage presenting the writer's choice: "Do I want to be authentically *me*, speaking my own thoughts in my own idiom, or am I content to be a pseudo-self, using borrowed thoughts, borrowed language, and a borrowed personality to gain the approval of a few literary traditionalists?"

He urges writers not to be "slaves to blind convention -- or unnecessary pomp." He cites Franklin Roosevelt's "fireside chats" as an example: convention demanded lofty presidential style, but Roosevelt "blithely ignored convention, choosing instead to give what he called 'fireside chats' -- personal, down-to-earth talks laced with colloquialisms and jokes." The result made him one of the most endearing modern presidents.

**Key principle:** "Be guided by what your own eyes and ears tell you, not merely by the so-called authorities." Look at what the best publications actually publish -- *The New Yorker*, *Harper's*, *Forbes*, *The Atlantic Monthly*, *Time*, *The New York Times*, *The Wall Street Journal*, *The Washington Post*, Pulitzer Prize-winning nonfiction. The revolution toward "greater naturalness, vigor, informality, and individuality" is already well underway.

## 4. Tips for Improving Your Readability

### 4.1 The Two Master Tips (Boxed in the Original)

> **1. View your reader as a companionable friend -- someone with a warm sense of humor and a love of simple directness.**
>
> **2. Write like you're actually talking to that friend, but talking with enough leisure to frame your thoughts concisely and interestingly.**

Trimble says if you tack these two tips on your wall and practice them, "your readability quotient should soar."

### 4.2 The 26 Specific Tips

Trimble presents 26 numbered, concrete tips. Every one is captured below.

---

#### Tip 1: Vary Sentence Length -- Use the Short Sentence as a Power Tool

"As a rule of thumb, whenever you've written three longish sentences in a row, make your fourth a short one. And don't fear the super-short sentence. It's arresting. Sometimes just a single word will be plenty long."

**Example (Gregg Hopkins):** "Many American parents think that today's colleges are veritable breeding grounds for premarital sex. Nonsense. Each year, literally tens of students graduate with their virtue still intact."

The last sentence illustrates the "literary knuckleball" -- and an axiom in aesthetics: "The smaller the sign, the greater the pleasure." Hopkins counts on our tendency to read "tens of students" as "tens of thousands." The joke slips through before we realize it, in a double take.

---

#### Tip 2: Use Occasional Contractions

"They'll help you unbend, let your readers relax as well, and free up your writing voice." The most popular contractions involve *am*, *are*, *is*, and *not*:

- I'm / you're / we're / they're
- he's / she's / here's
- won't / wouldn't / shouldn't / don't / doesn't / can't

**Example contrasted:**
- Stiff: "Why should we not have clean air?"
- Natural: "Why shouldn't we have clean air?"

**Critical caveat:** "Contractions, though, are like kisses: bestowed too freely, they lose their effect, in fact seem cheap. Save them for when you want to humanize some sentence." A sentence like "Let us start now because I will not be in town tomorrow" should become "Let's start now because I won't be in town tomorrow" -- but don't overdo it. "Let yourself sound like the very person you'd want to read yourself."

---

#### Tip 3: Prefer "That" to "Which" (Generally)

"Generally, prefer *that* to *which*. The one is conversational; the other, slightly more bookish." Save "which" for after a comma, to introduce a **nonrestrictive clause** (one that adds incidental information and could be cut without damage).

**Trimble's practice rule:** "If I could cut the clause, I'll use 'which' and a comma before it; otherwise, I'll normally use 'that.'" But he hedges: "normally." When in doubt, read the sentence aloud, testing it on the ear. Sometimes "which" sounds better for variety or rhythm. "As Rudolf Flesch has wisely said, 'You have to go by feel, not by rule.'"

**Example -- nonrestrictive (use "which"):** "The bike, which she rode just yesterday, has a flat." (The "which" clause adds incidental info; the sentence already specified the bike.)

**Example -- restrictive (use "that"):** "The bike that she rode just yesterday has a flat." (The "that" clause is essential -- it tells us *which* bike.)

---

#### Tip 4: Say "I" When You Mean "I"

"Don't wrap yourself in pomposities like 'the writer' or 'one' or 'this author' or 'we.'" Reserve "we" and "our" for situations where you're genuinely referring to both yourself and the reader -- i.e., where there really is someone else involved. Reserve "one" for when you mean "a person" (as in "One would have to be a CPA to grasp that"). When referring to the reader alone, address that person as "you," not "the reader." "The printed page already puts enough distance between the two of you. Why add to it?"

When generalizing about readers or people, and including yourself among them, use "we" and "our" -- "They're simple, conversational, and democratic."

---

#### Tip 5: Use Dashes for Emphasis or Humor

"Use dashes to isolate concluding phrases for emphasis or humorous effect." Trimble points to Pauline Kael as a master of the dash and recommends studying her film review (referenced in Chapter 2) for the range of effects achievable.

---

#### Tip 6: Professionals Quote; Amateurs Paraphrase (Use Dialogue)

"Professionals quote, amateurs paraphrase." The powerful truth: "readers love listening to people talk -- love hearing the actual words, not a preemptive digest of them." Use dialogue wherever the context warrants it -- "it's intrinsically dramatic."

**Don't be shy about inventing some.** *Imagined* thoughts -- one of a writer's best resources -- are fair game. Trimble gives the example of imagining a historical figure's internal monologue (Enobarbus deciding to leave Antony for Rome).

**Example (student paper on Updike's "A&P"):** Rather than summarizing what a character says, the student quotes the actual dialogue: "You'll feel this for the rest of your life. . . . You'd better get used to keeping your mouth shut when you don't agree with certain things because that's life, and that's what you have to do."

---

#### Tip 7: The More Abstract the Argument, the More You Need "Word Pictures"

"The more abstract your argument, the more you should lace it with what I call 'word pictures' -- illustrations, analogies, vivid quotations, metaphors, similes." These aid both understanding and memory. "They'll probably remember your illustration or analogy far longer than the abstract idea itself. But if the illustration is a good one, they'll be able to reconstruct the thought fairly easily, so it will have served its purpose twice over."

**Example (Ron Suskind, *A Hope in the Unseen*):** Uses the vivid analogy of the "crab/bucket syndrome" -- when one crab tries to climb from the bucket, the others pull it back down -- to illustrate the social forces dragging ambitious inner-city students toward failure.

**Example (Witold Rybczynski, *Home: A Short History of an Idea*):** Contrasts the Rococo chair (which "invites conversation"), the Victorian chair (which "invites after-dinner naps"), and the Modern chair which is "all business" -- it commands: "Let's get this sitting over with and get back to something useful."

**Example (Stewart Brand, *How Buildings Learn*):** Building historian Orlando Ridout "reads" houses: "The front facade is a self-conscious part of the house, where the owner is trying to make a statement to the world about what he is about."

---

#### Tip 8: Minimize Adjectives

"Try to let nouns -- especially *accurate* nouns -- work alone. This will simplify your style *and* give it more point."

**Key quotes:**
- Voltaire: "The adjective is the enemy of the noun."
- Twain: "As to the Adjective: when in doubt, strike it out."
- Twain (expanded, in a letter to D. W. Bowser): "I notice that you use plain, simple language, short words and brief sentences. That is the way to write English -- it is the modern way and the best way. Stick to it; don't let fluff and flowers and verbosity creep in. When you catch an adjective, kill it. No, I don't mean utterly, but kill most of them -- then the rest will be valuable. They weaken when they are close together. They give strength when they are wide apart. An adjective habit, a wordy, diffuse, flowery habit, once fastened upon a person, is as hard to get rid of as any other vice."

---

#### Tip 9: Minimize Adverbs -- Especially Trite Intensifiers

"Minimize your adverbs, too -- especially trite intensifiers like *very*, *extremely*, *really*, *clearly*, and *terribly*, which show a 90% failure rate."

**Example:** Compare "She was very upset by the news" with "She was shattered by the news." Cutting "very" and finding a stronger word usually *gains* intensity.

**Principle:** "When you then cut the intensifier, your phrasing usually *gains* intensity." Compare "very hungry" with "ravenous."

**Concession:** "The right adverb, fresh and adroitly placed, is one of life's finest small pleasures."

**Example (Stewart Brand):** "James Donnelly of Whole Earth inked every page of the manuscript bright red with line-editing corrections, for which I am whimperingly grateful." In each case, Brand saves his adverb for the next-to-last word, where it won't get overlooked.

**Another Brand example:** "Because of water, houses deteriorate most from the bottom up and the top down. Damage comes from below thanks to what the British call, knowledgeably, 'damp.'"

---

#### Tip 10: Use the Fewest and Simplest Words Possible

"Use the fewest words possible and the simplest words possible." Occasionally a longer word will work best -- it may express the idea concisely, contribute cadence, or gratify the reader with surprise. But: "the more you surrender to the temptation to write fancy, the further you'll stray from your true feelings and the more you'll write in a style designed to impress rather than serve the reader."

**Key quotes:**
- Thoreau: "Simplify, simplify."
- Maugham: "To write simply is as difficult as to be good."
- Hemingway: "Writing plain English is hard work."

**Warning against oratory:** "Oratory can fool us into thinking we're saying something smart, when in fact we may not yet have gotten past a platitude."

---

#### Tip 11: Ensure a Clean Narrative Line

"Be sure that each sentence is somehow connected to the ones immediately before and after it. There's no other way to achieve fluidity, or what I like to call a 'clean narrative line' -- the hallmark, in my opinion, of professional prose."

---

#### Tip 12: Summarize Your Argument Periodically

"In a long essay or report, summarize your argument every now and then so that readers can keep their bearings." These summaries should be "brief transitional paragraphs, maybe three or four sentences long." They serve double duty: they make a welcome change of pace *and* they show the steps in the argument.

---

#### Tip 13: Use Questions -- But Answer Them Promptly

"If you like putting questions to your reader, fine. They can add point to a discussion -- and, like transitional paragraphs, variety to your style. But answer them promptly." If you raise a juicy question at the end of your opening paragraph, the opening sentence of your next paragraph should start answering it explicitly. "You've created an expectation in us (*Hmm, what's her answer?*) that you need to gratify, pronto."

Also: "More often than not, young writers will forget to clue in their reader as to what they are about -- where they're headed and why they're taking that particular route. They assume it's understood. But the reader isn't clairvoyant; she knows only what the writer remembers to tell her. So share your road map with her."

---

#### Tip 14: Use Semicolons to Reduce Choppiness

"Use semicolons to reduce choppiness, particularly when you have several sentences with parallel structures. Also use them for a change of pace."

---

#### Tip 15: Read Your Prose Aloud -- Always

"Read your prose aloud. *Always* read your prose aloud." A checklist of what to listen for:

- Do you sound at ease with your reader?
- Can you read each sentence without stumbling or running out of breath?
- Does the phrasing sound like you talking at top form, or does it sound alien, "like it's coming out of some statue"?
- Does your prose flow along?
- Have you managed to avoid unconscious word repeats, especially at the beginning and end of consecutive sentences?
- Double-check paragraph openings -- "Skilled writers will always double-check those two spots. Their paragraph openings, too. It's easy to fall into a rut there."

---

#### Tip 16: Use Numerals Occasionally Instead of "First" and "Second"

"Instead of always saying 'first' and 'second,' occasionally use the numerals themselves in parentheses." And: "A *pair* of parentheses, please." It's a superstition that numerals have no place in serious writing.

---

#### Tip 17: Numbers -- When to Spell Out, When to Use Figures

Trimble acknowledges this is contested. His own practice, aligned with AP style: **"Spell out whole numbers below 10, use figures for 10 and above."** His mnemonic for the AP rule: *"If it's a one-digit number, make it a word."*

Concessions: Sometimes he'll write out a number like 16 at the beginning of a sentence or use figures for numbers below 10 where the simpler, more graphic form serves better. He admires consistency in style but has "learned to respect instinct; and sometimes 'sixteen' feels better, or looks better."

---

#### Tip 18: Don't Put a Comma After "And" or "But" at the Start of a Sentence

"When you begin a sentence with *And* or *But* (and you most definitely should now and then), don't, for heaven's sake, put a comma after it. You want to quicken your prose with those words, and the comma would just kill any gain."

**The exception:** A comma is needed "only if a parenthetical phrase follows." In that case, another comma goes after that phrase as well: "But, considering the evidence, she's probably right."

---

#### Tip 19: "So" and "Yet" Also Make Great Sentence Starters

"*So* and *Yet* also make great lead-offs, though most amateur writers can't believe it, or refuse to, at least in their own prose." They are short and brisk. Note: *So*, *Yet*, *And*, and *But* are normally spared commas because they're so short. But their polysyllabic kin -- *Consequently* and *However* -- normally do get commas.

**Example (Paul Graham, software developer and essayist):** "Part of what software has to do is explain itself. So to write good software you have to understand how little users understand."

**Example (Mark Bittman, *New York Times* food columnist):** Uses "So" to smoothly transition between a historical description and its consequence.

---

#### Tip 20: Prefer "But" to "However" as a Sentence Starter

"As a sentence starter, prefer *But* to *However*. It has two fewer syllables and takes no comma, so it's a cleaner, punchier transition word -- especially at the head of a paragraph, where it's peerless." *However* works best internally, "positioned right next to the point of emphasis."

---

#### Tip 21: Share Your Wit

"Do you have a good wit? If so, share it -- share your sense of verbal play, your good spirits. Let yourself have fun with your prose." Serious writing need not be solemn. 

**Key quote (F. L. Lucas, *Style*):** "No manual of style that I know has a word to say of good humour; and yet, for me, a lack of it can sometimes blemish all the literary beauties and blandishments ever taught."

---

#### Tip 22: Paragraphing Is Hugely Important

"Paragraphing is hugely important -- as much a matter of good style, and good sense, as practically anything else one can think of in writing."

- **Long paragraphs intimidate** most readers ("*I don't want to go in there!*").
- **Lots of short paragraphs** can suggest "a breezy, Madison Avenue glibness or a refusal to pursue a point home."
- **Cookie-cutter paragraphs** (say, two per page) "can suggest a tired imagination."

**Moral:** "Use variety to keep things alive and vital, as Dr. Seuss advised."

**Lead-off sentence rule:** Make sure your lead-off sentence in each paragraph does double duty as a **topic sentence**. "It should forecast, if not state outright, the new thought you're now developing there."

---

#### Tip 23: Use White Space Creatively for Major Transitions

"Here's a tip on the creative use of white space." When shifting from one large section to another and you're stymied about how to bridge, skip four spaces instead of the usual two between paragraphs. This will:

- Cue readers *visually* that a major new section is at hand.
- Give them a convenient place to pause.
- Spare you from having to manufacture a real transition. "White space substitutes for *meanwhile*, a most convenient pseudo-bridge that pros rely on when all else fails."

---

#### Tip 24: Choose Your Title with Care

"Leave the 'teasing' title to writers who are still putting cuteness before communication. Focus instead on making your own title accurately descriptive, which is challenge enough. If possible, try to give it zing as well." The title is "our introduction to you as well as to your paper. Remember, a pedestrian title is about as welcoming as a burnt-out motel sign."

---

#### Tip 25: If a Paragraph Sounds Labored, Talk It Out

"If you've written a paragraph that sounds labored, back off and ask yourself, 'How would I *say* this to a friend?' Then go ahead and talk it out loud. Afterward, write down as nearly as you can recall what you said." The talked-out version will usually shame the written one. Why? "When we write, we tend to over-complicate, and our very words get in the way; but when we talk, we instinctively simplify. We need it to be simple just to get it out, it seems."

---

#### Tip 26: Soak in Good Writing

"Take a short break and read some paragraphs of a writer whose style you relish. Try to *soak in* that style; try to feel yourself actually writing those paragraphs as you read them. Then say to yourself, 'OK, now how would [Wonderful Writer] write this?' and let yourself try again." This gives fresh perspective. "That's maybe half the battle right there."

## 5. Cross-Cutting Principles (Synthesized from Across the Chapter)

These principles emerge repeatedly throughout Trimble's chapter and represent his deepest convictions:

### 5.1 Write for the Reader, Not for Yourself
The reader is always the judge. Every choice -- diction, sentence length, paragraph structure, tone -- should be made with the reader's experience in mind. The reader is not clairvoyant; share your road map.

### 5.2 Naturalness Is Not the Enemy of Seriousness
The most damaging myth in writing instruction is that serious writing must be formal. The best contemporary prose sounds conversational yet is beautifully wrought. Labor should be lavished on concealing that very labor.

### 5.3 Voice Comes from Self-Acceptance
Authenticity cannot be faked. Writers who accept themselves write without inhibition, posturing, or counterfeiting. This produces the natural voice that readers find irresistible.

### 5.4 Read Aloud, Always
The ear catches what the eye misses. If prose doesn't sound right spoken aloud, it won't read right on the page.

### 5.5 Simplicity Is Harder Than Complexity
Plain English is hard work. Oratory and elevated diction are easy to produce and often mask shallow thinking. Simplicity requires genuine understanding of your subject and genuine respect for your reader.

### 5.6 Rhythm and Variety Are Essential
Vary sentence length. Vary paragraph length. Vary sentence openings. The interplay of long and short creates rhythm; monotony in any dimension creates boredom.

### 5.7 Concreteness Beats Abstraction
Abstract arguments need illustrations, analogies, metaphors, vivid quotations, and dialogue. Readers remember the concrete example long after they've forgotten the abstract principle -- but a good example lets them reconstruct the principle.

### 5.8 Cut the Fat
Minimize adjectives, minimize adverbs (especially trite intensifiers), use the fewest and simplest words possible. When you cut an intensifier and find a stronger word, the phrasing gains power. "Very upset" loses to "shattered."

### 5.9 Every Sentence Must Connect
Professional prose has a "clean narrative line" -- each sentence flows from the one before and into the one after. Fluidity is the hallmark. Orphaned sentences destroy it.

### 5.10 Style Is a Moral Choice
Every time you write, you choose what kind of person to be. Authenticity, directness, and individuality are not just stylistic preferences -- they are acts of intellectual courage. Hiding behind convention is the safe choice, but it produces lifeless writing.
