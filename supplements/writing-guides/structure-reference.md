# Structure reference

Lookup tables and procedures for document architecture. Consult a heading; do not read front to back.

Source: Schimel, J. (2012). *Writing Science: How to Write Papers That Get Cited and Proposals That Get Funded*. Oxford University Press.

## Story structures: full menu

| Structure | Sequence | Reader it suits | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| OCAR | Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution | Patient; wants evidence laid out before conclusions | Specialist papers, technical reports; the default |
| Telegraphed OCAR | OCAR plus a one-line preview of conclusions right after the challenge ("Here we show…") | Screening fast but willing to read on | Biomedical papers; anywhere readers triage a queue |
| ABDCE | Action, Background, Development, Climax, Ending | Impatient; must be hooked before being informed | Proposals, generalist journals. A and B together do the work of O and C |
| LDR | Lead, Development, Resolution | Impatient but will finish | Proposals, Discussion sections, long paragraphs, public writing |
| LD | Lead, Development | May stop at any point | Methods, Results paragraphs, abstracts, anything that may be truncated |

Three constraints ride on top of these; they are not alternatives to them.

- **Hourglass.** Wide opening, narrow challenge and action, wide resolution. The base must be as wide as the mouth.
- **Spiral.** The resolution returns to the opening's problem with that problem moved. A story that returns unchanged has resolved nothing.
- **IMRaD.** The section format a venue imposes. Fit the story into it; do not mistake it for the story.

Mapping the story onto IMRaD: Opening = first paragraph(s) of the Introduction. Funnel and background = body of the Introduction. Challenge = end of the Introduction. Action = Methods, Results, and most of the Discussion. Resolution = end of the Discussion.

## Opening: diagnosis and repair

**Misdirection.** Symptom: the second paragraph introduces a mechanism, topic, or actor the first paragraph never forecast; the reader cannot tell whether it is a correction or an addition. Fix: forecast in the first sentence. "Plants are a critical control of methane dynamics" becomes "Plants control methane dynamics by two mechanisms. The first is…".

**No direction.** Symptom: the opening explains textbook material the target reader already holds and never points anywhere. Fix: find the sentence where the real story starts and delete everything before it.

**Overpromising.** Opening broader than the resolution delivers. Readers feel cheated. Fix: narrow the opening, or widen what the resolution legitimately claims.

**Underpromising.** Resolution broader than the opening promised. The readers who wanted the story never arrive. Fix: widen the opening.

**Width test.** Read the opening and the resolution with nothing in between. If their breadths differ, change one. Forced to choose, err wide — an oversold opening filters down fast, a narrow one loses readers permanently.

**Two-step opening.** Step one engages an issue the target audience already cares about; step two modulates to the narrower topic the document is actually about. Exactly two steps; more and it stumbles.

**Pawn push vs queen launch.** A pawn push is a modest positioning first sentence whose job is to set up the next one — correct for OCAR. A queen launch opens in the centre of the story — correct for ABDCE and LDR. A pawn push that positions nothing is a platitude; cut it.

**Audience framing.** The same fact opens differently for different readers. For specialists, name the shared problem: "Only 0.01 to 0.1% of microbial cells from marine environments form colonies on standard agar." For generalists, make it strange: "Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, the first observer of bacteria, would be surprised that over 99% of microbes in the sea remained unseen until after Viking Lander set out to seek microbial life on Mars." Naming a work every insider knows in the first sentence acts as a password: it admits that audience and quietly excludes the wider one.

## Introduction funnel

**Step test.** At each step, ask whether the text is building the logical connection to the next step or telling the reader everything you know about the topic. Only the first is a funnel.

**Wall vs hole.** A literature review builds a wall and describes knowledge. An introduction describes the hole in that wall — ignorance. Test: when you state something known, do you use it to mark the boundary of what is known? If not, you are reviewing, not introducing.

**Citation position flag.**

- Data dump: "Smith (2003) found X." A story about Smith.
- Synthesis: "X occurs (Smith 2003)." A story about the world. Default to this.
- Live disagreement: "Although Smith (2003) reported X, Jones (2005) found Y."
- Challenge to consensus: "While most reports suggest X (Smith 2003, Xu 2004), Jones (2005) found the opposite, arguing…"
- Two camps: "There is still uncertainty about X, with some reports suggesting Y (Smith 2003, Xu 2004) and others Z (Arif 2005, Masukawa 2006)."

A draft full of "Smith found" is a draft that has not been synthesized yet.

**Selling a solution before a problem.** Symptom: the text names a method, framework, or tool and starts listing what it can do, before establishing the problem it answers. Reorder: the problem the audience has, why existing answers fall short, then the solution, named last.

## Challenge

Weak: "Our objectives were to measure the structural characteristics of anergic T-cells and their response to rechallenge." States data collected, not knowledge sought.

Strong: "To determine what causes mouse T-cells to be anergic, we evaluated their structural characteristics and how they responded to being rechallenged with antigen-loaded APCs."

**Buried hypotheses.** If one sentence implies three claims, split them, state each, and name the experiment that tests them.

**Goal order.** Order goals intellectually, never chronologically. The knowledge question comes first; a methods-validation goal is subordinate and says so. A strong verb on the secondary goal and "examine" on the primary one inverts the whole document.

**Question, hypothesis, objectives.** The question is always required — it defines the knowledge gap. A hypothesis is the question turned into a falsifiable prediction; use one when there is a relationship to test, not when there is a quantity to measure. Objectives describe the information to be produced, and serve as a map the reader checks the rest of the document against.

## Methods

LD structure: one framing sentence naming what the procedure was for, then the detail.

- No detail: "Enzyme inactivation was measured by the method of Turman et al." Forces a trip to another document.
- No frame: straight into concentrations and timings. Acceptable only under a hard limit.
- Correct: "To characterize the extent of enzyme inactivation associated with 3-HPAA metabolism, PGHS-1 or PGHS-2 was incubated with 25 μM 3-HPAA…"

**Schema-naming.** Name an established method and let the reader's knowledge supply the procedure: "We measured protein by the Lowry approach." Then state only the departures: "…by a modification of the Lowry assay, in which sodium citrate, instead of sodium tartrate, is used in reagent A."

## Results

**Choosing what to show.** Present what the story needs and archive the rest. When many variables covary, collapse them into one summary measure and put the family in a subordinate panel — stronger than either dumping all of it or writing "data not shown."

**LD in results.** State the pattern, then the detail. Never detail-then-summary, and never detail with no summary at all.

**Statistics phrasing.**

| Situation | Write |
|---|---|
| Clear effect | "Warming increased methane emissions by a factor of 3.4 (p < 0.05)." |
| Small but significant | "The treatment increased the response by only 30%, but the increase was statistically significant (p = 0.02)." |
| Large, above threshold | "The response in the treatment was 2.3 times higher than in the control, but the difference was significant only at p = 0.07." |
| Robust but weak | "The relationship between x and y is weak (R² = 0.10) but statistically significant (p = 0.001)." |

Never let "significantly increased (p < 0.05)" stand alone: it hides whether the effect was 1.2-fold or 12-fold, which is the part the reader needs. Report the magnitude and let the p-value support it. "No significant effect (p > 0.05)" written over a two-fold difference asserts a sameness the data do not show.

**Data, inference, interpretation.** Data are results; inference is what any practitioner would read off them; interpretation is your own claim about meaning. Cover all three somewhere, and keep them distinguishable even when they share a paragraph. Put data first and flag inference with a verb — *demonstrates*, *indicates*. A claim placed before its data reads as a plot imposed on the evidence.

## Resolution: repair procedure

1. Cut fluff, distractions, and any idea not already raised earlier. Move anything important into the section where it belongs.
2. Synopsize the key results.
3. Synthesize them — show how they answer the question.
4. Show what that contributes to the larger problem named in the opening.

**Failure symptoms.**

- *Weak*: summarizes results, then asserts importance without saying what was learned ("may provide new insights into…"). Almost always a missing concrete claim.
- *Distracting*: restates textbook truths, or raises a topic never introduced earlier. A new idea arriving at the end also costs readers — anyone searching for that topic will never find the document.
- *Undermining*: turns the strongest position in the document against itself.

**Phrases to strike from a resolution:** "more research is needed to clarify our findings"; "if the function of X can be fully established"; "but the importance of this has yet to be assessed"; "we hope this will stimulate further research"; "this topic deserves more research."

**Reordering fix.** "Our data provide evidence of safety and activity, although proof of clinical benefit remains to be established" becomes "While further trials will be necessary to establish the full benefits, our data provide evidence that it is safe and shows in vivo activity. It therefore may offer a new approach for treating…" Same content; the caveat no longer holds the last position.

**Ending on a question.** Legitimate when the question is concrete and visibly grew from the work — a new question about the world, not an admission that yours went unanswered.

**Proposal resolution.** Budget space for a closing paragraph that restates the big issue, explains how the proposed components work together to address it, and makes the final case. Reviewers often have not decided until they reach it.

## Paragraph structures

| Form | Where the point lands | Shape | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| TS-D | First sentence | Topic sentence, then development | Default; short paragraphs; most body text |
| LD | Within the first few sentences | Multi-sentence lead, then development | The point needs a run-up |
| LDR | First and last | Lead, development, synthesizing resolution | Long paragraphs; transitions |
| OCAR | Last sentence | Opening sets the stage, then challenge, action, resolution | Openings, resolutions, complex arguments carrying caveats |

Point-first should dominate. Point-last belongs at critical junctions — openings, resolutions, transitions — and accounts for roughly a quarter to a third of a document.

**Repairing an incoherent paragraph.** Identify the real story it should tell; decide whether it needs point-first or point-last; pull the tangled threads apart and give each its place. If the material carries two stories, make it two paragraphs and link them.

## Sentence revision: three steps

Original: "The qualitative agreement between caribou's preference for feeding on young leaves and the trend for protein to decline with leaf age supports the hypothesis that caribou migration is driven by the patterns of leaf-out and maturation spatially and temporally through their home range, rather than by weather."

1. **Fix the topic.** The grammatical subject runs 21 words. Open on the real subject with the verb right behind it: "Caribou prefer to feed on young, protein-rich leaves…"
2. **Fix the stress.** The original ends on "weather" — the rejected explanation. End on the claim: "…the spatial and temporal patterns of leaf-out and maturation."
3. **Pack the middle.** Everything of lower priority goes between.

Result: "Caribou prefer to feed on young, protein-rich leaves, supporting the hypothesis that migration is not driven by weather but by the spatial and temporal patterns of leaf-out and maturation."

**Checklist, applied to every clause as well as to the whole sentence:** the topic is short and clear; the main verb follows it immediately; the key message sits at the stress.

**Reordering the same facts changes the claim.** Three facts in one sentence yield six possible messages, not one. "Viruses were not studied in the sea until 1989 yet are its most abundant biological entities" is about viruses. "The most abundant biological entities in the sea are viruses, yet they were not studied until 1989" is about the sea and stresses the delay. Choose deliberately.

**Long sentences.** Length is not the problem; a delayed payload is. Open with a short main clause carrying the point, then hang subordinate clauses off it. "At the beginning of the second millennium AD, lake level at Lake Tanganyika fell and remained relatively low during the period from ~1050–1250 AD, which corresponds to the timing of the Medieval Warm Period in many locales, albeit with a later onset than in some areas" runs over forty words and reads easily because the action arrives early.

## Flow repairs

**Reach back and grab.** Take an idea from the closing arc and make it the topic of the sentence that opens the next. It may be the same word, a transformed version of the idea, or a whole restructured sentence. Lengthening a subject is an acceptable price for the connection.

**Delete the intruder.** When sentence one and sentence three are the real pair, cut sentence two and give its material its own arc elsewhere. Do not bridge to something that does not belong.

**Fix upstream.** When bridging produces local flow but pushes the story in the wrong direction, the fault lies in the earlier passage's aim, not in the transition. Rewrite the earlier passage.

**Stress-stress.** Two units share an underlying idea but never hand it off — neither one's opening picks up the other's close. It reads as two unrelated blocks. Fix by repeating the shared idea as the opening of the second unit, or by naming it at the close of the first.

## Fuzzy verbs and action verbs

| Fuzzy (weak) | Action (strong) |
|---|---|
| occur, affect, facilitate, perform, conduct, implement, evaluate, mediate, is controlled by | modify, accomplish, create, increase, decrease, invade, react, inhibit, disrupt, accelerate, migrate, eat, bind, fail |

A fuzzy verb reports that something happened without showing what. "Herbivores facilitate the invasion of exotic grasses by mediating competition" becomes "Herbivores preferentially eat native plants, giving exotic grasses a competitive advantage that allows them to invade."

**Fuzzy hypotheses are the worst case.** "Microbial community composition is controlled by plant inputs, water availability, and soil chemistry" is unfalsifiable — nothing could contradict it. "The chemical nature of plant inputs is the single strongest control on the composition of soil microbial communities" is testable, and can be wrong, which is what makes it worth reading.

## Nominalizations

| Verb | Nominalization |
|---|---|
| move | movement |
| differ | difference |
| suggest | suggestion |
| interact | interaction |
| analyze | analysis |
| develop | development |
| influence | an influence on |
| approach | took an approach to |
| yield | the yield of |

| Adjective | Nominalization |
|---|---|
| different | difference |
| difficult | difficulty |
| able | ability |
| capable | capability |
| similar | similarity |

Repair sequence: "There was a difference between the reaction rates of treatments X and Y" → "Reaction rates were different between treatments X and Y" → "Reaction rates differed between treatments X and Y."

Nominalizations earn their place when the name itself is the concept — *gene expression*, *ecosystem services*, *taxation without representation*, *the arguments developed above*. Naming defines a schema and compresses paragraphs into a word. It becomes jargon the moment the reader lacks that schema.

## Empty amplifiers

certainly, dramatically, entirely, highly, quite, rather, really, simply, substantially, very — and their adjective forms.

Delete test: remove the modifier. If the thought survives intact, it was empty. "The treatment dramatically increased X by a factor of 42" — once the number is there, the drama is redundant.

Modifiers that survive the test define rather than intensify: the *first* phase, a *preliminary* result, she smiled *sadly*. Keep those.

When deleting a modifier leaves the sentence hollow, the modifier was hiding a missing fact. Supply the fact rather than restoring the decoration: "Thermal stress induces structural and functional changes in GTH-7" becomes "Thermal stress alters the conformation of GTH-7's active site, reducing its affinity for GXP."

## Heavy and plain words

| Latinate or French | Plain |
|---|---|
| duration | length, time |
| consume | eat |
| mortality | death |
| permit | let |
| necessary | need |
| demonstrate | show |
| donate | give |
| initiate | start |
| attempt | try |
| utilize | use |
| methodology | method |
| efficacious | effective, worked |

Test each word: would you use it talking to a colleague over coffee? Endings in *-ate* and *-ion* mark the heavy stock. Keep the heavier word only when it carries a sense the plain one does not — a fire *consumes* fuel and cannot *eat* it; *forage* implies collecting, not merely preferring.

## Prepositional phrases and noun trains

| Prepositional phrase | Compound noun |
|---|---|
| source of water | water source |
| supply of nitrogen | nitrogen supply |
| distribution of resources | resource distribution |
| kinetics of enzymes | enzyme kinetics |
| burning of fossil fuels | fossil fuel burning |
| cancer of the lung | lung cancer |

Prepositional phrases attract nominalizations and passive verbs; collapsing one often converts a nominalization back into a verb — "starting with binding of Red22 to the coding region" becomes "starting with Red22 binding to the coding region."

**Stacking limit.** Two nouns: compound, almost always. Four: break it up. Three: judge by weight and by whether two of the words already read as one unit — "science fiction writer" works, "resource allocation modification" does not. Reintroduce a preposition when the train creates ambiguity ("microbial community composition influences" reads as a verb on first pass) or when it buries the word that should carry the stress ("limit the effects of global warming" beats "limit the global warming effects").

## Placing a technical term

- **Start of a sentence.** You assume everyone knows it. Risk: it reads as jargon to those who don't.
- **End of a sentence.** You define it for everyone. Risk: you look ignorant of what the field already holds.
- **Middle, in a set-off clause.** You assume most readers know it and signal it is not central. Risk: some readers skim past it.

Err toward overdefining: an expert's irritation is brief, a novice's confusion ends the reading. Spell out every abbreviation on first use — "Dendritic cells (DCs) are antigen presenting cells (APCs) that initiate immunity" — except abbreviations more familiar than their expansions. Name shorthand for what it means (DEC, CON for deciduous and coniferous), not for where the sample came from.

**Jargon vs technical term.** Jargon names a schema the reader does not hold, and has an adequate plain equivalent. A technical term names a schema the reader holds, or has no plain equivalent that would not mislead. The distinction belongs to the audience, not to the word.

## Condensing: the five targets

**Redundancy** — several words doing one word's work.

- "I will develop, test, and apply a new synthetic approach to produce photovoltaic plastics" → "I will develop a new approach to produce photovoltaic plastics."
- "Most, but not all of the test subjects responded" → "Most of the test subjects responded."
- Across sentences, collapse the second into the first. "The altered precipitation patterns associated with global warming will change the water regimes of most ecosystems, particularly those with arid, semi-arid, and Mediterranean climates. These dry environments currently comprise one third of the terrestrial land surface." → "Climate change will alter the water regimes of most ecosystems, particularly those in arid and semi-arid regions, which comprise roughly one third of the land surface."

**The obvious** — implied by context or by words already present.

- "There is evidence that X17 production can be associated with enzyme induction (Chu et al. 2008)" → "X17 production can be associated with enzyme induction (Chu et al. 2008)." The citation and the "can" already carry it.
- "Snow cover is a characteristic of high alpine ecosystems that is critical in regulating…" → "Snow cover in alpine ecosystems is critical in regulating…"

**Modifiers** — see empty amplifiers above.

**Metadiscourse** — discussing the discussion: "We found that…", "In this study, we measured…", "We argue that…", "These data may indicate…". Cut it where the situation already supplies it; new data reported here can only have been found by you. "We found that aniline did not react" → "Aniline did not react." Cutting metadiscourse also dissolves most active/passive arguments about describing your own actions.

**Verbosity** — several kinds of filler at once. "The data show that some enhancement in the applicability of these measurements can be accomplished with freeze-fracture prior to analysis by laser-ablation mass spectrometry" (25 words) → "Freeze-fracture pretreatment improved analyses by laser-ablation mass spectrometry" (8).

**Sixth pass: put the action in the verbs.** "Agents that can interfere with the binding of AS2 protein to DNA are capable of delaying the onset of ovarian cancer" → "Agents that interfere with AS2 protein's binding to DNA can delay the onset of ovarian cancer."

**Diagnostic for prose that is long *and* confusing.** Count the verbs, then ask where the actions are. If the actions live in nouns and the only verbs are heavy Latinate ones, rewrite with the actor in the topic and the action in the verb. Around four verbs per twenty-five words is comfortable.

**Page-limit trick.** Scan for paragraphs whose last line carries one or two words. Cut anything earlier in that paragraph and the orphan line vanishes; one word can buy a whole line.

**Stop condition.** A first draft going to coauthors can afford to run long — trimming an overloaded draft is easier for them than filling gaps in a thin one. Words that build flow and coherence are not filler. "Writing can get stripped down to the point that it becomes barren. Some filler can add flow, so use it, but carefully" beats the tighter "Writing can get too barren. Filler can add flow. Use it carefully."

## Limitations: placement map

| Kind of limitation | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| The question does not match what the method can answer | The introduction — narrow the question so the method can fill the gap you claim |
| Experimental or analytical detail | The methods, immediately. Explain how you work around it, why it does not matter for this question, or why something was not done |
| An unusual or novel approach whose strengths and weaknesses need calibrating | An opening "Experimental considerations" subsection of the discussion |
| Affects how the data should be interpreted | Early in the body of the discussion, briefly, once, then move on |
| Must be acknowledged in the conclusion | Compressed into a subordinate opening clause: "Despite the limited number of validated examples, …" |

Never place a limitation in an opening or a closing position unless it is the point of the document. Having addressed one, do not return to it.

Model treatment of an unavoidable design flaw — state why it was unavoidable, situate it in the field's general constraints, explain what compensates, then move on: "The predator-exclosure sites were not replicated, owing to the high costs of maintaining 8 km of electric fencing on a nearly daily basis for a decade. Ecologists are caught increasingly in the tension between the manipulations necessary to understand large-scale ecological processes and the impossibility of replicating large manipulations. Our control sites sampled a wide range of natural variation within our study area, increasing the likelihood that differences on the treatments were due to our manipulations."

There is no bad method — only a mismatch between what a method measures and what it is being asked. Reframing what the results are taken to show is often the whole fix.

## Checklists

**Internal structure**, applied paragraph by paragraph and section by section:

1. Does each unit make a single, clear point?
2. Where several paragraphs form a section, are the links among them explicit?
3. Has every stray thought that breaks the serial arc been removed?
4. Is each topic resolved before a new one opens?
5. Is every major unit marked by a subhead or by clear opening text?

Anything short of yes on all five means the structure is not finished.

**Revision passes.** Structure, then clarity, then flow, then language. The passes overlap and must be repeated; fixing language first is wasted work, because structural revision deletes the sentences you polished. When a pass stops turning up problems, return to structure and ask whether whole sentences or paragraphs can now go.

**Sentence pass.** Short clear topic; verb immediately after it; key message at the stress; the action in the verb rather than in a noun; old information in the topic and new information in the stress.

**Final pass.** Print it, stand up, and read it aloud. Awkward phrasing, broken flow, and clunky words survive the eye but not the ear.

## Message box (broad or non-expert audiences)

Fix two things and hold them in view: the **issue** and the **audience**. Then answer four questions in order.

1. **Problem** — the specific problem. Becomes the opening and challenge, usually collapsed into one lead.
2. **So what?** — why this audience should care. Different for colleagues, funders, and the public. Fold it into the lead.
3. **Solution** — what you did or propose. Becomes the body.
4. **Benefits** — what this audience gets. Becomes the resolution.

It works for expert audiences too; only the answers change.

For lay readers specifically: use a front-loaded structure; make the people doing the work into characters; define every technical term or avoid it; never introduce a technical term in the topic position; assume intelligence and ignorance, not stupidity; and show the process of working a problem out rather than staging a breakthrough.
