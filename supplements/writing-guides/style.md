# Prose Style

Derived from John R. Trimble, *Writing with Style: Conversations on the Art of Writing*, 3rd ed. (Pearson, 2011).

## Scope

Apply this to expository and persuasive prose written for a human reader: essays, memos, reports, analyses, arguments.

Do not apply it to code comments, machine-parsed output, API responses, log lines, or config, or where a fixed house format governs — a template, a schema, a regulatory form, a citation style. There the format wins and this guide is noise.

The source is a student-facing American book with a mid-century literary taste. Where a position below is taste rather than rule, it says so.

## The reader is present

Bad writing is mostly the failure to imagine a reader. The novice writes for himself, because the page never talks back. The veteran keeps a specific reader in the room and shapes every sentence around what that person will feel on meeting it. Everything below follows from this.

Two things are owed first: have something fresh to say, and don’t idle getting to it. Fail either and the reader leaves.

Invent her before drafting: a companionable friend with a warm sense of humor and a love of simple directness. Then serve her. Come to the point, anticipate boredom and confusion, offer variety and wit — serious is not the same as solemn — and talk rather than pontificate.

**The sentence audit.** While revising, put each sentence to her:

- Is there any conceivable way this could confuse her?
- Am I droning? Is she ready to silence me?
- Am I using this phrase to impress her, or is it the only clean way to say this?
- Will she hear a living voice, or am I starting to sound like a book?

Assume whatever isn't plainly stated will be misconstrued: she is a stranger to your ideas, and owes you one reading.

## Getting a draft down

When you don't yet know what you think, write to find out; no outline gets you past that. Let the generator run first and the critic in second — kept together, they stall each other.

1. **Stockpile, then interrogate.** Gather facts, quotes, objections, concrete details — if you have just enough, you don't have enough. Then put hard questions to the subject (why, who, how, when, where) and answer each in a few lines. Ten answered is most of the piece.
2. **Freewrite a throwaway draft.** Notes out of sight, start with "Well, it seems to me that…" and talk on the page as if explaining it to a friend. Twenty minutes, no pausing, no editing. If you stall, write the stalling down and continue — the jam either breaks, turns into an idea, or shows the argument doesn't hold.
3. **Critique it, then freewrite again, longer and just as fast.** Only then tinker for the right words.

If the opening won't come, write it last — often the second paragraph turns out to be the real opening.

## Openings

You get three or four sentences before the reader decides. Enter by the front door: state what you contend, with specifics, immediately.

A stalling opening — summary, definitions, throat-clearing, the grand generalization — is rarely a stylistic problem. It is a symptom: the writer hasn't decided what the piece argues and is delaying the confrontation. Fix the thesis and the opening fixes itself.

**The thesis test.** Write "I contend that—" and complete the sentence. Then check it:

- Could someone disagree? A thesis is a contention — "your thesis is not your subject, it's your take on your subject." If nobody could dispute it, you have a topic, not a thesis.
- Is it complex enough to carry the whole piece?
- Did you stick your neck out, or pussyfoot?

Keep the opening plain — razzle-dazzle only camouflages weak ideas — and full-bodied rather than a two-sentence splinter, which signals you are short on material. Put the thesis last in the paragraph, where it climaxes and springboards into the next.

## Middles

Treat yourself as a prosecutor, the piece as a case, the reader as a skeptical jury.

- **One main contention per paragraph**, however many examples it draws in.
- **Divide up the proof.** Work out what the thesis must establish — motive, means, opportunity — and prove one thing at a time.
- **Assert, then support; assert, then support.** An unproved assertion stays an assertion.
- **Make the evidence climb.** Save the strongest for last and label it: "Finally, and most seriously…"
- **Signpost.** Say how many pieces of evidence are coming and where you are among them. Turns, parallels, and contrasts need explicit connectives.
- **Make the opener a bridge as well as a label.** It should carry the reader across from the paragraph before while forecasting the new thought. An opener that only announces its topic leaves a seam.
- **Quote rather than paraphrase.** Readers want the actual words, not a digest of them; invented interior monologue is fair game.
- **Answer any question you ask.** Start in the next paragraph, explicitly.
- **Summarize periodically in a long piece.** Three or four sentences, so the reader keeps their bearings.
- **Hinge every sentence to its neighbors** — plain style, occasional repetition of key words, careful use of *this* and *that*.

## Closers

The last paragraph is what survives in memory, so conclude — don't recap. Three requirements hold at any length:

1. Focus on the main point, which may be your final point.
2. Give one new twist or phrase, so the reader is still learning at the end.
3. End with emotional impact.

In a long piece, make it half-summary, half-conclusion: gather the high points, name the implications, and make the paragraph self-contained enough to stand in for the whole. While drafting, set aside two or three choice ideas to spend here — a weak sentence is recouped by the next one, a weak ending is not.

## Words and sentences

**Cut.** Ask of each sentence: what words aren't pulling their weight? Collapse wordy phrases (*in the event that* → *if*), drop redundant synonyms, replace a limp noun phrase with one strong noun. *His bold and brash temper has been replaced by a careful and prudent manner* is fourteen words for *Prudence now tempers his impetuosity*. Prefer short words; they read faster and leave the long word room to land.

**Prefer active verbs.** "X does Y" matches the reader's curiosity — actor first. Make one pass looking at nothing but verbs, and recast *there is / it is* openings, which amble past the subject and verb without saying anything. Keep the passive deliberately to emphasize an effect ("Charles alone was injured"), to soften a request, or where the agent is unknown or irrelevant. Note the usual cause, though: writers reach for it out of insecurity. Vague prose also hides where the reader could disagree.

**Cut adjectives and adverbs.** Let accurate nouns work alone. Trite intensifiers — *very, extremely, really, clearly* — fail nine times in ten, and cutting them intensifies: *very hungry* is weaker than *ravenous*.

**Keep the reader mildly surprised** — not in suspense, surprised — because prose with no small shocks stops being read. Reach for metaphor and illustration hardest where the argument is most abstract, since the image outlives the abstraction.

## The middle register

Write in the middle register — the way an educated person talks when being careful. Not stiff, not slangy. It is the compromise between formal and informal, and the only one that holds precision, concision, ease, and freshness at once.

Make it the default, because the alternative is a defensive pose. Asked to write seriously, most writers try to *seem* wise and authoritative, and since nobody becomes more authoritative on demand, they fake it stylistically: studied objectivity, impersonal address, elevated diction, elaborate sentences. Repeated long enough, the pose hardened into a convention that now enforces itself — academese, businessese, committee prose.

It is harder than either extreme: it needs tone control, and tone is a matter of ear. Use the formal register only where the occasion truly calls for it — a legal brief, a policy statement, a scientific paper — and the job is to transmit information and stay offstage. Choose it; don't default to it.

Within a single document the register shifts by section. Methodology, quantitative claims, and compliance passages take the formal register legitimately; framing, rationale, and argument do not. Calibrate section by section rather than imposing one register throughout — and remember that formal is never license for the pose.

## Prohibitions that aren't rules

Each is taught as law and is not one. Professional prose breaks all of them unnoticed, which is why they survive.

- ***But*, *And*, *So*, or *Yet* at the head of a sentence** — do it, with restraint; all are lighter than *however* or *furthermore*. No comma after them.
- **Contractions** — use one where the rhythm asks, not where it doesn't, or the style gets overweighted with informality. *Why should we not have clean air?* against *Why shouldn't we have clean air?*
- **Addressing the reader as *you*** — say it when you need it, exactly as in conversation. "The reader" only adds distance the printed page already supplies.
- **First-person *I*** — reserve it for flagging a conjecture or warming a dry account; otherwise generalize impersonally, as if pointing out what any intelligent person could see. Well-supported assertions don't need props like *it seems to me*.
- **Ending a sentence with a preposition** — a superstition imported from Latin. Judge by sound.
- **Splitting an infinitive** — split it where that sounds natural and creates no ambiguity; the adverb then lands where it gets most notice.
- **One-sentence paragraphs** — use one to emphasize a point that would otherwise be buried, to mark a transition, or to rest a tiring reader. Make sure the sentence survives the attention.

## Form as a resource

Form is not a container to fill. Shape the piece for effect — a reader who is enjoying it keeps reading.

- **Plot it.** Hook curiosity with a real problem, develop it so the tension holds, resolve it.
- **Break the pattern to create emphasis.** Any change draws the eye — a short sentence among long ones, a one-sentence paragraph, white space. Whatever you want stressed, make different from its surroundings. Vary paragraph length for the same reason.
- **Hunt the merely-adequate sentence,** the commonest defect in competent prose. Recast until one sings; that is what buys attention for the next sentence.

## The read-aloud test

Run this on every draft; prose is judged by ear before anything else. Read the piece out loud, in a normal speaking voice, and listen for:

- **Stumbles and breathlessness.** A sentence you can't get through cleanly on one breath is one the reader can't parse cleanly. Break it or cut it.
- **Voice.** Does this sound like a person, or like it's coming out of a statue?
- **Repeats.** Unconscious word repetition, especially at the starts and ends of consecutive sentences and at paragraph openings.
- **Joints.** Anywhere you have to stop and re-orient, the reader will be lost. That is a missing bridge sentence.

If you wouldn't say it, don't write it.

When a paragraph sounds labored, stop revising it. Say it aloud to an imagined friend and write down what you said; talking forces the simplicity that writing tempts you out of.

## Revising and proofreading

Nine-tenths of writing is rewriting; rewriting is where the piece gets made, not repaired.

- **Separate the passes.** Verbs in one, wordiness in another, continuity in a third. A pass that watches for everything catches nothing.
- **Let it stand, then read it twice:** once as an average reader, hunting unconscious obscurity; once as your worst enemy, hunting everything else.
- **Rewrite wherever your own attention wanders.** If you drift there, the reader stops.
- **If the meaning doesn't survive one reading, the fault is yours.** First remedy: shorter words and sentences.
- **Borrow an ear when stuck.** Ask how a writer whose prose you admire would have written the passage.
- **Verify every quotation against its source before shipping.** Transcribing accurately is the most mechanical task there is; a writer who fails it forfeits trust in everything harder.
