# Structure

How to shape a technical or scientific document so a reader follows it and remembers it. Derived from Joshua Schimel, *Writing Science: How to Write Papers That Get Cited and Proposals That Get Funded* (Oxford University Press, 2012).

## Scope

Apply this to papers, proposals, technical reports, documentation, and long-form explanation — anything with a reader who must follow an argument from beginning to end. Do not apply it to a short chat reply, a commit message, a code comment, or a brief status note. Imposing whole-document story architecture on a two-line answer buries the answer under scaffolding nobody asked for. Below a few paragraphs, keep only the sentence-order and verb rules.

The governing principle throughout: "It is the author's job to make the reader's job easy."

## Before you draft

Find the story before you shape it. Work up from the material: explore what it might mean at its widest, then cut back to the simple core and tell it from the top down. Outliers and results that refuse to fit are often where the real story is; the average is usually the story someone already told.

Carry the material as far as it will go — data, information, knowledge, understanding. A document that stops at "here is what we measured" leaves the rest to the reader, who won't do it.

Name one point: if you cannot say in a sentence what the document establishes, you are not ready to structure it.

## What survives retelling

Five properties decide whether an idea sticks. Check them before drafting and before shipping.

- **Simple** — the core essence, compactly. Simple is not simplistic: a simple message condenses the hard argument, a simplistic one dodges it.
- **Unexpected** — frame a gap in what is known. A small gap beats no gap, and readers want facts only once they see they need them.
- **Concrete** — the dangerous altitude is the middle: field-specific concepts, neither everyday abstraction nor physical fact, read to outsiders as jargon. Anchor them to detail or to something the reader already holds.
- **Credible** — ideas grounded in prior work, data in methods, conclusions in the data. A break anywhere costs the whole chain.
- **Curiosity-engaging** — ask a real question rather than offering information.

Build on schemas the reader already holds, since new material attaches cheaply to an existing structure and expensively to nothing. Assuming a schema the reader lacks confuses them; explaining one they hold condescends.

## Choosing a structure

Choose by how patient the audience is.

- **OCAR** — Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution, in order. Slowest, and the default for readers who want the evidence before the conclusion.
- **ABDCE** — Action, Background, Development, Climax, Ending. Opens on action, then backs up to fill in the setup. Less efficient, and worth it when readers must be hooked first.
- **LDR** — Lead, Development, Resolution. The point up front, developed, then resolved. With ABDCE, this is what most proposals need.
- **LD** — Lead, Development. Everything essential in the first sentences, for readers who may stop at any point.

Missing any of the four elements leaves a document slippery. A reader should be able to read the opening, challenge, and resolution alone and still get the point.

Two constraints ride on top. The **hourglass**: wide opening, narrow challenge and action, wide resolution, with the base as wide as the mouth. The **spiral**: the resolution returns to the opening's problem with that problem moved. Section formats such as IMRaD are containers, not the story.

## The opening

Do three things at once: name the problem driving the work, introduce the characters, and target an audience. Your first words carry more leverage than anything else you write.

An opening can fail in only two ways. **Misdirection** — it sets up one story and the document turns out to be about another, so the reader gets whiplash. Forecast both threads in the first sentence instead. **No direction** — it explains background the target reader already holds and points nowhere. Find where the story actually starts and delete everything before it.

Match the opening's breadth to what the document delivers. Promise wider than you deliver and readers feel cheated; promise narrower and the readers who wanted it never arrive. Read the opening and the resolution with nothing between them and compare. Forced to choose, err wide; a narrow opening loses readers who never come back.

For an audience that does not share your schemas, use a **two-step opening**: engage an issue they already care about, then modulate to your narrower topic. Two steps only.

## From opening to question

Between the opening and the challenge, narrow. At each step build the logical connection to the next, rather than reporting everything known about the topic. The job is to frame the gap: what is not known, and why filling it matters to the problem you just named.

A literature review builds a wall and describes knowledge; an introduction describes the hole in it and so describes ignorance. Test every known thing you state: is it marking the boundary of knowledge? If not, cut or reframe it.

Never pitch a solution before establishing the problem it answers.

## The challenge

State the knowledge you hope to gain, not merely the data you will collect. Compressed, a good challenge is "to learn X, we did Y." Dropping the "to learn X" is the standard failure: the question feels obvious to you, and no reader knows the work as well as you do.

## The action

Everything between challenge and resolution. Give each part its own lead — state the purpose or the pattern first, then the detail. Detail with no frame forces the reader to hold facts with nowhere to put them.

Keep visible which material is data, which is inference any practitioner would draw, and which is your own interpretation, whether or not you separate them into sections. Present the data before the claim drawn from it; a claim placed first reads as a plot imposed on the evidence.

Choose which results to present: what does not serve the story dilutes it.

## The resolution

The last thing said is taken as the message, whatever you intended. Spend that position on three things: a synopsis of the key results, a synthesis showing how they answer the question, and a statement of what that contributes to the problem the opening named.

It fails three ways. **Weak** — asserts importance without saying what was learned. **Distracting** — restates textbook truths, or raises a topic the opening never introduced; a late idea belongs back in the opening, not as a twist at the end. **Undermining** — the most common and the worst: "more research is needed to clarify our findings" takes the strongest position and turns it against your own work. Ending on a new, concrete question that grew out of the work is fine; ending on doubt about the work is not.

## Internal structure

The same shape repeats at every scale: sections, subsections, and paragraphs each open, develop, and resolve, nested inside the arc above. Coherence lives in those arcs rather than in the sentences: a run of individually clear sentences that never resolves and never returns to its opening has no point.

Resolve a topic before opening the next. When an idea keeps resurfacing in scattered places, it has no arc — consolidate it into one, or cut it.

Paragraphs make their point first or last. Point-first — topic sentence, or short lead, then development — should dominate. Point-last belongs at openings, resolutions, transitions, and any paragraph long enough to need pulling together. Write point-first or point-last paragraphs, never point-nowhere ones.

## Information order inside a sentence

A sentence is the smallest story: the subject opens, the verb acts, the object resolves.

Two positions carry the weight. The **topic**, at the start, is what readers take the sentence to be about — put something they already know there. The **stress**, at the end, is where attention peaks — put the new information, the main message, or the term you want emphasized there. Of three items, the most important goes last, the second-most first, the least in the middle. The same applies inside each clause.

Keep the verb immediately after the subject; every word wedged between them dulls the sentence.

Long sentences are fine, delayed payloads are not. Open with a short main clause carrying the point, then hang the qualifications off it.

## Linking sentences

Sentences that individually read well can still fail to connect. Make one sentence's stress the next sentence's topic, so new information becomes the known ground for what follows; that builds a chain. Successive sentences sharing a topic build a list instead — right for a few facts elaborating one point, wrong for anything that has to move.

At a transition into a new arc, reach back and grab an idea from the arc that is closing and make it the topic of the opening sentence. A longer subject is a fair price for the connection.

If bridging produces local flow but sends the story the wrong way, the fault is upstream: rewrite the earlier passage rather than patch the seam. If two sentences won't connect because one doesn't belong, delete it and give its material its own arc.

## Energy

Put the action in the verb, and put the verb early.

**Fuzzy verbs** report that something happened without showing what — evaluated, facilitate, mediate, is controlled by. They cost the reader the mechanism. In a hypothesis they are fatal: a fuzzy hypothesis cannot be falsified, so it is not worth testing.

**Nominalizations** turn the action into a noun and leave a weaker verb to carry the sentence — "conducted an investigation" for "investigated." They add length and no information. Keep one only when the name genuinely is the concept.

**Passive voice** has two honest uses: making the acted-on the topic when the surrounding story is about it, and omitting an actor you have reason not to name. Everywhere else it drains the sentence, and objectivity comes from how you treat the evidence, not from removing yourself from the prose.

## Condensing

Condense in two stages. First prune the big limbs — decide what not to say. Brevity comes from selection, not compression: once the story is clear, what belongs is clear. Repairing broken arcs is part of this: an idea discussed in three places costs extra transitions and repetitions.

Then shake out the dead leaves: cut every word that adds no content, no clarity, and no coherence — redundancy, the obvious, empty modifiers, metadiscourse about your own process, and general verbosity. Expect to lose ten to twenty percent of a first draft, all of it weight.

## Limitations and negative results

Do not put limitations last. Results-then-caveat makes the caveat the resolution and therefore the message. Invert it: address each limitation as early as it can be addressed, constrain the claim to fit inside it, and end on what you did establish.

Match the limitation to the place. A mismatch between question and method is fixed in the opening, by narrowing the question until the method can answer it. Procedural limits belong with the procedure. Interpretive limits belong early in the discussion, stated once and then left alone. Where one must appear in a conclusion, compress it into a subordinate opening clause and finish on the claim.

There is no bad method, only a method asked the wrong question. Reframing what the results are taken to show is often the entire repair.

## Revising

Work in passes from the largest scale down: structure, then clarity, then flow, then language. The passes overlap and must be repeated: clarifying a sentence exposes structural problems, and polishing language before the structure settles polishes sentences you will delete. When the small passes stop finding anything, return to structure and ask what can be cut whole.
