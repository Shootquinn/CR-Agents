# Writing with Style

## Citation

Trimble, J. R. (2011). *Writing with style: Conversations on the art of writing* (3rd ed.). Prentice Hall. https://www.pearsonhighered.com

## Abstract

A composition teacher's shoptalk on expository prose, addressed to writers past the introductory-textbook stage. Covers idea generation and drafting, opening and closing an essay, paragraph continuity, diction, readability, the taboos passed down by composition teachers, critical analysis, dramatizing an argument, revising, proofreading, and — in a second part — the mechanics of punctuation, quotation, abbreviation, and usage. Method: named rules and tests, each worked against real student essays, the author's own drafts, and passages pulled from working writers. Scope: the University of Texas at Austin's Advanced Expository Writing seminar, three editions across 1975, 2000, and 2011.

## Architecture

Two parts, seventeen chapters, and a set of sources. The parts do different jobs and are read differently: Part 1 is an argument delivered in sequence, Part 2 is a reference shelf.

**Part 1 — Fundamentals (chapters 1–12).** The argument, in four movements.

Chapter 1 sets the premise everything else derives from: the difference between a novice and a veteran writer is not knowledge of grammar but whether a reader is present in the writer's mind while drafting. Chapter 2 covers process — how to get started at all — with a ten-step sequence for writers who are stuck.

Chapters 3, 4 and 5 take the shape of a piece in order: Openers, Middles, Closers. Chapters 6 and 7 drop to the sentence and the word — Diction, then Readability, which is where Trimble states his own stylistic position and backs it with twenty-eight numbered tips.

Chapter 8 clears the ground by dismantling seven received prohibitions. Chapter 9 is the book's practical examination: a sustained sentence-by-sentence critical reading of a single Mencken essay, the method of the preceding eight chapters applied to one specimen at full length. Chapter 10 is the advanced chapter, on shaping form for effect.

Chapters 11 and 12 close the part briefly — Revising is a single page consisting of a *Paris Review* exchange with Hemingway, and Proofreading is two pages on verifying quotations.

**Part 2 — Odds & Ends (chapters 13–17).** Reference material, consulted rather than read through. Punctuation (twenty-six pages, the longest chapter in the book, organised mark by mark), Quoting (the mechanical conventions of quotation, in eleven subsections), Abbreviations, a usage glossary, and a two-page Epilogue.

The load-bearing chapters are 1, 7 and 10. Chapter 1 supplies the axiom — write for a reader who is present to you — and chapters 3 through 6 are applications of it at different scales. Chapter 7 converts that axiom into a defensible style position, "General English," and is where the book is most explicitly arguing rather than instructing. Chapter 10 is the ceiling: form as a creative resource rather than a container, which is the only chapter that asks for more than competence.

Chapter 9 sits apart from the sequence and carries unusual weight for its position. It is the one place the whole method operates continuously on a real text rather than on constructed examples, and Trimble reports having taught it for nearly three decades.

The imbalance between the parts is deliberate and stated: Part 2's title concedes that punctuation, quoting, abbreviation and usage are apparatus rather than art. They occupy roughly a third of the page count and none of the argument.

## Front matter

"A Word About These 'Conversations'" opens with an epigraph attributed to its original speaker, not to Trimble:

> For me, writing is the only thing that passes the three tests of metier: (1) when I'm doing it, I don't feel that I should be doing something else instead; (2) it produces a sense of accomplishment and, once in a while, pride; and (3) it's frightening.

— Gloria Steinem (p. ix)

Trimble states the book's premise as a deliberate departure from the genre it belongs to:

> Books on writing tend to be windy, boring, and impractical. I intend this one to be different — short, fun, and genuinely useful. (p. ix)

His stated chief goal is "to take the mystery out of how skilled writers think, so you can begin thinking like them yourself," alongside a second goal of sharing "practical tips on how to make your prose more readable," on subjects from opening strategies to the use of semicolons, plus recurring questions about punctuation, quoting, usage, and stylistic taboos (p. ix). He describes the book outgrowing its original plan — meant to be "read over a couple cups of coffee," it now needs "a third, maybe even a fourth" — and calls the result "a friend I was loath to bid good-bye to" (p. ix).

He addresses scope directly, naming what the book leaves out and why:

> A few readers — teachers mainly — may be disappointed that I've excluded end-of-chapter exercises, not to mention discussion of research papers, grammar, syllogistic reasoning, patterns of "paragraph movement," and other such things conventionally covered by textbooks on writing. I can only answer that this is not — and doesn't aim to be — a conventional manual. (pp. ix–x)

He states the intended reader and the register he is aiming for:

> What I offer here is practical shoptalk for armchair consumption — in effect, an informal four-hour refresher course, with the emphasis on refreshment. The book is primarily geared to those writers who've already been through the textbook mill and who now hunger for helpful tips, inspiration, and a clear, lively synthesis of the essentials. But because it focuses on fundamentals, I hope it may prove useful to others, too. (p. x)

The Preface to the Third Edition recounts the book's publishing history and what changed in this edition. Trimble notes 32 reprintings over the first 25 years, followed by a "Silver Anniversary Edition," then another decade passing that brought his retirement from the University of Texas; his Pearson editor, Brad Potthoff, subsequently pressed him for a further revision (p. xi). He describes the third edition's changes directly:

> So for this latest edition, featuring two brand-new chapters — "Critical Analysis: Jousting with Mencken" and "Dramatizing Your Ideas" — I'm sharing some of the fun my students and I had in good old 325M, my Advanced Expository Writing seminar at UT. Meanwhile, throughout, I'm also including a sprinkling of updates — fresh help on taboos, exclamation points, ellipses, and usage manuals, among other things. (p. xi)

He invites corrections, comments, and sources for unattributed ("fugitive") quotations by email, and gives his University of Texas address for that purpose (p. xi).

The Preface closes with acknowledgments — pre-publication reviewers, University of Texas colleagues, Professor John Bean (author of *Engaging Ideas*), former students, a list of Pearson Education staff, the associate project manager and copyeditor for the edition, LawProse's Bryan and Karolyne Garner, and a dedication of the book to his wife, Jan (pp. xi–xii).


## Chapters

### Part 1 — Fundamentals

#### Chapter 1 — Thinking Well

**Claim.** Writing is a style of thought, not a set of mechanics, and it can be taught as one. The novice writer fails not from ignorance of grammar but from an unexamined egocentrism — writing for himself because the page never talks back — while the veteran writer has internalized the presence of a reader and shapes every sentence around that reader's needs and likely reactions. The chapter's task is to make the veteran's largely invisible thought process explicit enough for a novice to adopt it.

**Mechanism.** Trimble frames the gap between novice and veteran as the gap between a beginning chess player with no "chess sense" and a Grandmaster, and states his aim directly:

> "I hope to repair that neglect. My chief aim, both in this chapter and throughout the book, is to help you develop 'writer's sense.'" (p. 3)

The novice's root problem is that he writes for himself rather than for a reader — Trimble calls this **unconscious writing**, comparing it to a person who "turns his chair away from his listener, mumbles at length to the wall, and then heads for home without a backward glance" (p. 4). The remedy is a "few moments' reflection" that the writing/reading process is a communication between two people, subject to ordinary good manners. This produces the **conscious writer**, who "resembles a person who companionably faces her listener and tries her level best to communicate with him" (p. 4).

Trimble draws a developmental parallel: the novice writer's growth mirrors an infant's socialization, moving through three stages — "first, an awareness of himself from the reader's vantage point (objectivity); next, a capacity to put himself imaginatively in the mind of the reader (empathy); and finally, an appreciation of the reader's rights and feelings (courtesy)" (p. 4). Reaching courtesy as a writer yields "a readable style" (p. 4), just as reaching it as a child yields social sensitivity.

For the veteran, writing is defined as "the art of creating desired effects" (p. 5), and for an essay writer the chief desired effect is persuasion — of both the ideas and the writer as their source. Trimble gives four essentials for winning readers:

1. Have something to say that's worth their attention.
2. Be sold on its validity and importance yourself so you can pitch it with conviction.
3. Furnish strong arguments that are well supported with concrete proof.
4. Use confident language — vigorous verbs, strong nouns, and assertive phrasing. (p. 5)

He then states that this list, though it looks complete, is missing the decisive element: "The ultimate way we win readers is by courteously serving them — that is, satisfying their needs" (p. 5), on the reasoning that the writer exists only by the reader's continuing assent, so the writer is in the reader's debt, not the reverse (p. 5). This yields a second, longer list — "five specific ways you can serve your readers' needs," to be added to the four essentials above:

1. Phrase your thoughts clearly so you're easy to follow.
2. Speak to the point so you don't waste your readers' time.
3. Anticipate their reactions (boredom, confusion, fatigue, irritation).
4. Offer them variety and wit to lighten their work.
5. Talk to them in a warm, open manner instead of pontificating to them like a know-it-all. (p. 7)

Trimble flags that these five apply to conversation as well as writing, and expands on two of them within the chapter itself — #1 (clarity) and #3 (anticipating reader response) — reserving the rest for later chapters.

On serving readers, Trimble specifies two disciplines. First, **cultivate a psychological sense**: "you must sensitize yourself to what wins you over — how and why you respond, and what makes you feel well served — and gradually learn to extend that awareness to your reader" (p. 6). The mechanism for building this sense is reading critically rather than passively, interrogating a text's manner as well as its message — Trimble supplies sample self-directed questions: "Is his style too complex to be readable, or too plain, or is it just right — and why?" "What is his tone, and how does he achieve it? Do I like it or don't I?" "Why does he use a semicolon here instead of a period?" "Do I like this two-sentence paragraph?" "What effect do his contractions have on me?" (p. 6). Second, **school yourself to be other-oriented**: actively identify with readers, intuit their needs, and think of their convenience rather than your own (p. 6).

Under the clarity heading, Trimble names four attitudes shared by clear writers:

- They assume that their chief job is to communicate, ahead of persuading or charming.
- They assume, "with a pessimism born of experience, that whatever isn't plainly stated, the reader will invariably misconstrue," since she is "a perfect stranger to their garden of ingenious ideas" — so the writer's job is to guide her through step by step, per "the writer's Golden Rule," which he states is "the same as the moralist's: Do unto others . . ." (pp. 7–8).
- They assume that even their profoundest ideas are capable of being expressed clearly, rather than treating their reader as a clairvoyant.
- "They have accepted the grim reality that nine-tenths of all writing is rewriting . . ." (p. 8).
- "Perhaps most important of all, they are sticklers for continuity. They link their sentences and paragraphs as meticulously as if they might face criminal charges for negligence" (p. 8).

Under the anticipation heading, Trimble describes the veteran's habit of running a constant, sentence-by-sentence internal audit — "every sentence — yes, every sentence — receives a battery of challenges." He lists eleven of these self-directed challenges in full:

- "Am I droning here? Is she ready to silence me? Is there any way I can lighten this up?"
- "How can I get her to see — to feel — the urgency of this point?"
- "Is the continuity silky here, or is fatigue blinding me to a bump?"
- "Might she welcome an analogy here, or is this abstract idea clear enough on its own?"
- "Am I treating her as if she were an idiot?"
- "Is there any conceivable way this sentence might confuse her?"
- "Have I just used any of these words in previous sentences?"
- "Will this phrase strike her as pretentious? And, honestly, am I using it to impress her, or is this the only way I can express the thought cleanly?"
- "Will she get the nuance here, or should I spell it out?"
- "Can she jump on me for verbosity here?"
- "Will she hear a strongly conversational, living voice coming through, or am I beginning to sound like a book?" (pp. 9–10)

He extends the same vigilance to paragraphing, watching for "whale-like paragraphs" that leave the reader "sinking under their weight" and "mini-paragraphs that had his eye bouncing down the page," and to the joints between paragraphs, asking "Is the connection solid? ... Is there any conceivable way she can feel disoriented here?" (p. 10).

The chapter closes with a six-item procedure, "Some Concluding Thoughts," for turning first-draft "mumbo jumbo" into finished prose:

1. "Mumbo jumbo is another word for grunts of the mind. Mumbo jumbo is what comes out in first and second drafts when you're writing basically for yourself — that is, when you're still trying to fathom what you think about a subject."
2. "Once you've finished writing for yourself and begin writing for the reader, your mumbo jumbo will start turning into bona fide prose — i.e., sentences that make sense."
3. "If your reader can't get your full meaning in a single reading, however — and a single reading is all she owes you — you must face up to the fact that you're afflicted with some residual mumbo jumbo."
4. "The best remedy? Shorter words and shorter sentences."
5. "When you finally think you've finished a piece, reread it twice, first through the eyes of the average reader (for unconscious obscurities) and second through the eyes of your worst enemy (for all other lapses)."
6. "As a last caution, let the piece stand overnight. Then, in the morning, go at it again — you're bound to have a whole new outlook. Also, do as the professional author does and share it with some candid friends. Tell them, 'I'm interested in seeing this thing improved, not approved' — and mean it." (pp. 10–11)

**Evidence and examples.** Trimble supports the chapter's claims almost entirely through quoted authorities rather than worked before/after passages; each is reproduced here in full.

The chapter opens with two epigraphs. From Ernest Hemingway: "The indispensable characteristic of a good writer is a style marked by lucidity" (p. 2). From F. L. Lucas: "And how is clarity to be achieved? Mainly by taking trouble; and by writing to serve people rather than to impress them" (p. 2).

A footnote on page 3 attributes a supporting remark to Paul Burka, described as "a National Magazine Award-winning journalist and executive editor of Texas Monthly," who told one of Trimble's classes: "The hardest thing a writer has to do is curb his self-indulgence" (p. 3, n. 1).

Arguing that a pleasing manner makes arguments themselves more persuasive, Trimble quotes Samuel Butler: "We are not won by arguments that we can analyze, but by tone and temper, by the manner which is the man himself" (p. 5). Trimble adds his own qualification immediately after: "I don't wholly agree with that, but it's certainly close to the truth" (p. 5).

Illustrating the attitude that even difficult ideas can be expressed with lucidity, Trimble quotes novelist Somerset Maugham's *The Summing Up*:

> "I have never had much patience with the writers who claim from the reader an effort to understand their meaning. You have only to go to the great philosophers to see that it is possible to express with lucidity the most subtle reflections. You may find it difficult to understand the thought of Hume, and if you have no philosophical training its implications will doubtless escape you; but no one with any education at all can fail to understand exactly what the meaning of each sentence is." (p. 8)

To demonstrate that clarity is won through labor, not native ease, Trimble lets what he calls "a few clear writers speak for themselves." From historian George M. Trevelyan:

> "The idea that histories which are delightful to read must be the work of superficial temperaments, and that a crabbed style betokens a deep thinker or conscientious worker, is the reverse of the truth. What is easy to read has been difficult to write. The labor of writing and rewriting, correcting and recorrecting, is the due exacted by every good book from its author. . . . The easily flowing connection of sentence with sentence and paragraph with paragraph has always been won by the sweat of the brow." (p. 8)

From novelist James A. Michener:

> "I have never thought of myself as a good writer. Anyone who wants reassurance of that should read one of my first drafts. But I'm one of the world's greatest rewriters." (p. 8)

From E. B. White, whom Trimble introduces as "perhaps America's most respected 20th-century essayist":

> "The main thing I try to do is write as clearly as I can. Because I have the greatest respect for the reader, and if he's going to the trouble of reading what I've written — I'm a slow reader myself and I guess most people are — why, the least I can do is make it as easy as possible for him to find out what I'm trying to say, trying to get at. I rewrite a good deal to make it clear." (p. 9)

Closing the chapter's revision-and-feedback point, Trimble tells the anecdote of George Bernard Shaw and actress Ellen Terry, who had "confessed her reluctance to deface the manuscripts of a play he had sent her for criticism." Shaw's reply, quoted in full:

> "Oh, bother the MSS., mark them as much as you like: what else are they for? Mark everything that strikes you. I may consider a thing 49 times; but if you consider it, it will be considered 50 times; and a line 50 times considered is 2 per cent better than a line 49 times considered. And it is the final 2 per cent that makes the difference between excellence and mediocrity." (p. 11)

**Terms introduced.**

- **Writer's sense.** The literary counterpart of a chess player's "chess sense" — the tacit, cultivated instinct for how an experienced writer thinks through a piece, which Trimble states is his chief aim to help the reader develop (p. 3).
- **Unconscious writing.** Writing produced by a writer who has forgotten that a reader exists, and so writes to satisfy only himself; Trimble states this self-orientation is "the ultimate reason for most bad writing" (p. 3).
- **Conscious writer.** The writer who has grasped that writing is a social act addressed to another person, and who shapes the piece around that person's reception of it (p. 4).
- **Psychological sense.** The sensitivity, built by reading one's own reactions to other writers' prose, that lets a writer anticipate how his own reader will respond (p. 6).
- **Other-oriented.** The discipline of habitually thinking of the reader's needs and convenience rather than one's own (p. 6).
- **Mumbo jumbo.** Trimble's term for the "grunts of the mind" that come out in first and second drafts, produced while a writer is still writing for himself rather than for a reader (p. 10).

**Connections.** As the opening chapter of Part 1, "Fundamentals," this chapter establishes the book's governing frame — writing as a reader-directed social act — that later chapters apply to specific techniques. It expands within itself on two of its own five ways of serving readers (clarity and anticipating reader response, points #1 and #3 of the list on page 7) and explicitly defers the remaining three (brevity/directness, variety and wit, and tone) to chapters later in the book.

#### Chapter 2 — Getting Launched

**Claim.** There is no single correct way to start a piece of writing — composing habits are as individual as the writers who hold them — but a writer stuck for a way in can still be helped by a concrete, ten-step sequence for finding a subject, gathering material, and forcing a first draft onto the page.

**Mechanism.** Trimble opens by disclaiming any universal method: "Given our quirky methods of composition, I'm leery of recommending any one way as effective, for the question always becomes, 'Effective for whom?' Each of us finally does the job in the way that best suits his or her temperament and current deadline" (p. 12). He then offers ten numbered recommendations anyway, on the premise that "most of us are desperate enough to be always shopping for alternate strategies, bits of which we might later incorporate into our habitual method" (p. 13), and that finding even two or three usable ones justifies the list. The ten steps, in full:

1. **Listen to your feelings** (p. 13). Choose a subject that engages you emotionally as well as intellectually: "As in romancing, so in writing: you're most effective when your heart is in it." If the subject is assigned rather than chosen, the fix is to manufacture a stake in it "by learning your subject cold — by going after it aggressively, like an intellectual conquistador" (p. 14).

2. **Start small** (p. 15). Narrow the subject to manageable scope before writing: "It's better to start small and grow big than to start big and maybe grow overwhelmed." Trimble notes that the writer typically discovers what he thinks only by writing about it, so delimiting the subject at this stage is necessarily provisional.

3. **Stockpile data** (p. 17). After settling on a subject, delay drafting and instead gather "facts, quotes, parallels, ironies, puzzlements, gut impressions," with facts given priority because "readers like to be taught, and they invariably prefer the concrete to the abstract." Stated rule: "If you have just enough solid data, you don't have enough; with a big surplus, you're primed to write" (p. 17).

4. **Pose some tough questions** (p. 17–19). Bombard the subject with reporter's questions — "Why? Who? How? When? Where?" — and sketch tentative answers in mini-paragraphs, one question per 5-by-8-inch slip of paper (explicitly not the smaller 3-by-5 card, which he reserves for bibliographical data). Procedure: write the question at the top of a fresh slip, jot ideas beneath it, number slips by question, continue until roughly ten questions are answered, then shuffle and reread the slips together to surface "unexpected contrasts and similarities." He names two virtues of the method: psychological — "it enables you to write much of your paper before you begin writing it" — and organizational — it gives "convenient places to store your ideas, plus an easy way to retrieve and arrange them" (p. 19).

5. **Get an organizing principle — a thesis** (p. 19). Select the "meatiest, most comprehensive" idea from the slips to try out as the essay's thesis. Definition given in full: "It's a viewpoint, a contention. A good thesis, I would argue, is above all arguable — that is, not everyone will agree with it." He clarifies it need not be a right/wrong claim; it can instead concern whether something is urgent, interesting, or a good way to do something. Distinguishing rule: "Your thesis is not your subject. It's your take on your subject."

6. **Imagine a good audience** (p. 20). Where the real audience is fixed (a professor, a supervising partner), respect that person's known requirements, but beyond that, construct the audience you need: Trimble's own practice is to "envision my reader — no matter who it is — as a companionable friend with a warm sense of humor and a love of simple directness," on the theory that "people often act as they're treated."

7. **Freewrite a "zero draft"** (p. 20–21). Term defined: "a throwaway — a piece of freewriting that allows you to warm up, get into the flow, work past your inhibitions, bust through your writer's block." Procedure given in full: review the slips once, put them out of sight, then talk out your thoughts on paper as if explaining the subject to a friend, starting with the fixed formula "Well, it seems to me that ..." Set a timer for 20 minutes (extendable). Rules: never pause more than a second or two between sentences; do not censor; if stuck, write the stuck feeling down directly ("I seem to have stalled out here..."); use your own conversational idiom, not "the puffed-up language of academe." He names three possible outcomes of persisting through a logjam: the problem resolves itself through the act of verbalizing it, you stumble on a new insight, or you discover the argument doesn't hold up in its present shape.

8. **Critique your draft** (p. 21–22). After a break, reread the zero draft critically: check whether the thesis still holds up, judge which ideas have promise versus which are extraneous, mark passages that need more support, then return to the slips to check off points already used and underline points still to incorporate.

9. **Freewrite again for 45 minutes** (p. 22). Repeat the procedure from step 7 with the slips mostly (not entirely) hidden again, this time at 45 minutes rather than 20, maintaining speed throughout: "Take care that you don't start slowing up, for rapid writing encourages the mind to function freely... If you slow down to edit what you've written, you'll put an airtight lid on those thoughts." He frames the underlying mechanism as two separate mental "departments" — a generative one and a critical one — that function best kept apart during drafting.

10. **Tinker to get the words right** (p. 22). Once a further rough draft no longer feels necessary, move into the editing/revising stage — "tinkering" — with the object of finding "the words that best express your answer — and the organization that gives it the smoothest delivery."

**Evidence and examples.** Every step above is backed by specimen material, all reproduced here rather than summarized.

*Step 1 — emotional engagement.* Trimble supplies a personal case of his own, in a footnote: four months into writing his Ph.D. dissertation he asked himself, "Do you really want to be known as the world's expert on [my chosen subject]?" and answered aloud, "No!" — then, "Well, then, what would you really like to work on?" He reports: "After several minutes I knew, and immediately set to it, charged with excitement and energy. Those two questions changed my life" (p. 13).

For emotionally charged prose, he offers a full specimen from film critic Pauline Kael, from her collection *Deeper into Movies*, reviewing *The French Connection*:

> The noise of New York already has us tense. [The French Connection] is like an aggravated case of New York: it raises this noise level to produce the kind of painful tension that is usually described as almost unbearable suspense. But it's the same kind of suspense you feel when someone outside your window keeps pushing down on the car horn and you think the blaring sound is going to drive you out of your skull. This horn routine is, in fact, what the cop does throughout the longest chase sequence. The movie's suspense is magnified by the sheer pounding abrasiveness of its means; you don't have to be an artist or be original or ingenious to work on the raw nerves of an audience this way — you just have to be smart and brutal. The high-pressure methods that one could possibly accept in Z because they were tools used to show the audience how a Fascist conspiracy works are used as ends in themselves. Despite the dubious methods, the purpose of the brutality in Z was moral — it was to make you hate brutality. Here you love it, you wait for it — that's all there is. I know that there are many people — and very intelligent people, too — who love this kind of fast-action movie, who say that this is what movies do best and that this is what they really want when they go to a movie. Probably many of them would agree with everything I've said but will still love the movie. Well, it's not what I want, and the fact that Friedkin has done a sensational job of direction just makes that clearer. It's not what I want not because it fails (it doesn't fail) but because of what it is. It is, I think, what we once feared mass entertainment might become: jolts for jocks. There's nothing in the movie that you can enjoy thinking over afterward — nothing especially clever except the timing of the subway-door-and-umbrella sequence. Every other effect in the movie — even the climactic car-versus-runaway-elevated-train chase — is achieved by noise, speed, and brutality. (p. 14)

Trimble's gloss: "It's impossible to write electric prose like this without strong emotion to energize your thinking, so pick a subject you have a stake in and write about it just as candidly as you know how" (p. 14).

He adds two further cases of writers making the best of assigned subjects: landscape architect Russell Page, quoted from *The Education of a Gardener* — "Limitations imply possibilities. A problem is a challenge" (p. 15) — and an autobiographical anecdote about his first newspaper job, writing obituaries for *The Buffalo News*. His editor's rebuke, quoted in full: "Listen, young man, nothing you write for this paper will ever get read as carefully as what you're writing right now. The relatives of these folks will notice every single error. You get a date or address wrong, they'll spot it. You get a name misspelled, they'll spot it. And they'll resent it, too, you can betcha. But they'll also be grateful if you do justice to their grandpa or mother or whoever it is. They'll put your prose in laminate, son" (p. 15). Trimble reports the effect: he "pledged myself to start writing obits that deserved that laminate" and "ended up actually liking to write obits."

*Step 2 — starting small.* No separate worked example beyond the gardening metaphor: "A small garden well tended is far more comely than a large garden that shows overambition. So, too, with essays" (p. 15). Trimble ties this step to a broader claim about process, illustrated with a compiled set of specimen statements from professional writers on discovering meaning through the act of writing itself (p. 16–17):

> How can I tell what I think till I see what I say? — E. M. Forster
>
> Writing is an exploration. You start from nothing and learn as you go. — E. L. Doctorow
>
> I have never started a poem yet whose end I knew. Writing a poem is discovering. — Robert Frost
>
> There is always a point in the writing of a piece when I sit in a room literally papered with false starts and cannot put one word after another and imagine that I have suffered a small stroke, leaving me apparently undamaged but actually aphasic. — Joan Didion
>
> I don't write easily or rapidly. My first draft usually has only a few elements worth keeping. I have to find out what those are and build from them and throw out what doesn't work, or what simply is not alive.... I am profoundly uncertain about how to write. I know what I love or what I like, because it's a direct, passionate response. But when I write I'm very uncertain whether it's good enough. That is, of course, the writer's agony. — Susan Sontag
>
> Sometimes you get a line, a phrase, sometimes you're crying, or it's the curve of a chair that hurts you and you don't know why, or sometimes you just want to write a poem, and you don't know what it's about. I will fool around on the typewriter. It might take me ten pages of nothing, of terrible writing, and then I'll get a line, and I'll think, "That's what I mean!" What you're doing is hunting for what you mean, what you're trying to say. You don't know when you start. — Anne Sexton
>
> I write in the morning.... Then, after all the [dinner] dishes are moved away, I read what I wrote that morning. And more often than not, if I've done nine pages I may be able to save two and a half, or three. That's the cruelest time, you know, to really admit that it doesn't work. And to blue pencil it. — Maya Angelou
>
> I write to find out what I'm talking about. — Edward Albee
>
> I am an obsessive rewriter, doing one draft and then another and another, usually five. In a way, I have nothing to say, but a great deal to add. — Gore Vidal

And, transitioning into step 3, a longer specimen from E. B. White on delay as a natural part of composing:

> Delay is natural to a writer. He is like a surfer — he bides his time, waits for the perfect wave on which to ride in. Delay is instinctive with him. He waits for the surge (of emotion? of strength? of courage?) that will carry him along. I have no warm-up exercises, other than to take an occasional drink. I am apt to let something simmer for a while in my mind before trying to put it into words. (p. 17)

*Step 3 — stockpiling data.* Trimble supplies his own worked example of a data-gathering exercise, from an assignment he wrote for his Advanced Expository Writing seminar, after counting words in Hemingway's sentences:

> Although Hemingway is celebrated for his short sentences, he was equally at home with long ones. In fact, five consecutive sentences in his story "On the Blue Water" run 23, 109, 55, 58, and 60 words, and rank among the best he ever wrote. (p. 17)

His gloss: "That second sentence is mostly data." A footnote adds a supporting quotation from Thomas Griffith, former editor of *Life* magazine: "I work better professionally when my views are crowded and challenged, for I recognize that out of antagonism comes quality, which is why the best sculptures are of marble, not of soap" (p. 17).

*Step 4 — tough questions and the 5-by-8 slip system.* Trimble works a full example using Edward Albee's play *Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?*, posing the question "How does Martha protect herself from feeling pain and alienation?" and answering it as a numbered slip would be filled in:

> 1. She smothers any recognition of her father's lifelong indifference toward her (see p. 225) by vocally worshipping him — a good example of what psychologists term "reaction formation."
> 2. "I pass my life in crummy, totally pointless infidelities," she confesses (p. 189). Two probable reasons: to reassure herself that she is lovable and to discharge her strong masochistic feelings (e.g., "I disgust me," p. 189).
> 3. She externalizes that self-contempt — and feeds her insecurity — by loudly ridiculing her husband George.
> 4. She uses liquor to drown the pain. She's now an alcoholic: George remarks that she "can't get enough" liquor (p. 224).
> 5. She fancifully invents a child — a son — to bring beauty and meaning into her barren life. The son is one person who is all her own, to use as she wishes: to love and be loved by. (p. 18)

Trimble notes each of the five points "could be developed further in later slips and could eventually become a separate paragraph of your essay," and recalls a colleague, Professor Ernest Lovell, who "write an entire book on 5-by-8 slips... it turned out it was the sole method he ever used, and he swore by it" (p. 19).

*Step 7 — the zero draft.* A footnote supplies Anne Lamott's endorsement of rough first drafts, from *Bird by Bird*: "For me and most of the other writers I know, writing is not rapturous. In fact, the only way I can get anything written at all is to write really, really shitty first drafts" (p. 20). Trimble notes she is quoted again in Chapter 7.

*Step 8 — critiquing the draft.* A footnote offers a case from outside prose composition, poet Stephen Spender on Beethoven's working method: "Beethoven wrote fragments of themes in notebooks which he kept beside him, working on and developing them over years. Often his first ideas were of a clumsiness which makes scholars marvel how he could, at the end, have developed from them such miraculous results" (p. 21).

*Step 9 — the second freewrite.* Trimble quotes colleague Professor Betty Sue Flowers's formulation of why editing and generating must stay separate:

> You have to let the madman out. The madman has got to be allowed to go wild. Then you can let the architect in and design the structure. After that, you can have the engineer come in and put it together. And then you let the janitor in to clean it up. The problem is, most people let the janitor in before they let the madman out. (p. 22)

**Terms introduced.**
- **Bleeders** — writers who are "mentally constipated or perfectionistic, and refuse to move on from one sentence to the next until the first has been mercilessly flailed" (p. 12).
- **Appreciation** — a piece of writing that shares "your sense of delight" about a person, event, character, book, or locale, offered as one option when the writer is in good spirits (p. 13).
- **5-by-8 slips** — Trimble's preferred data-gathering medium: cheap gummed pads of 5-by-8-inch paper, one question per slip, distinguished from the smaller 3-by-5 card stock he reserves for bibliographical data (p. 17–18).
- **Thesis** — "a viewpoint, a contention," and specifically an arguable one: "not everyone will agree with it." Distinguished explicitly from subject: "Your thesis is not your subject. It's your take on your subject" (p. 19).
- **Zero draft** — "my term for a throwaway — a piece of freewriting that allows you to warm up, get into the flow, work past your inhibitions, bust through your writer's block" (p. 20).
- **Tinkering** — Trimble's own name for the revising/editing stage that follows drafting (p. 22).

**Connections.** Step 7's footnote points forward explicitly to Chapter 7, where Anne Lamott is quoted again. Step 6 points forward to the later chapter titled "Readability," where Trimble says he gives the audience-envisioning advice he previews here — "I normally try to follow the same advice I'll be giving you later, in the chapter on 'Readability'" (p. 20). The chapter's ten-step sequence (data gathering, thesis formation, drafting, critique, redrafting, tinkering) sets up the revision-focused material that follows it in the book.

#### Chapter 3 — Openers

> "It is in the hard, hard, rock-pile labor of seeking to win, hold, or deserve a reader's interest that the pleasant agony of writing again comes in." —John Mason Brown
>
> "What gets my interest is the sense that a writer is speaking honestly and fully of what he knows well." —Wendell Berry
> (p. 23)

**Claim.** A piece of writing gets only three or four sentences to prove itself before a reader decides whether to continue; a strong opener is therefore critical both because it wins the reader and because "the quality of an opener tends to forecast what follows" (p. 24) — it sets the tone the rest of the piece tends to keep. The most reliable openers approach the subject directly (the "front-door approach"); weak openers stall, summarize, or generalize before committing to a thesis (the "back-door approach").

**Mechanism.** Trimble frames the choice as a test of directness: "An essay, like a house, can be entered by the front door or the back door" (p. 24). Front-door writers "march into their subject with breathtaking assurance, clearly eager to share their opinions. They know what they think — and why they think it" (p. 24). Back-door writers delay that commitment, and Trimble gives a bulleted, complete account of why:

- They haven't taken the trouble to formulate a point of view, so they have little to argue, hence little reason to argue it. What's the point of coming to the point when you don't have a point?
- Because they have little to say, they fear their reader. They know he's apt to expose their bluff. So they instinctively delay a confrontation with him as long as possible — often right down to the last sentence.
- They haven't yet learned to value their reader's time. In fact, they haven't learned even to consider their reader, at least in any systematic way, for they're still preoccupied with merely getting ideas on paper.
- They have a vague notion that they're supposed to be writing for the World, not for a well-informed reader. And even though common sense tells them otherwise, they cling to that notion since it lets them rationalize flagrant padding. (p. 25)

He identifies a second, subtler form of back door: the "smoke screen" opener, produced by a writer who "grasps how the thing is supposed to sound" but "having zero to say, she must content herself with an empty gush — lovely, for sure, but still empty." He labels this maneuver "Establishing the Large Critical Overview" and calls the result "The Art of Saying Nothing Profoundly" (p. 25).

For actually building a good opener, Trimble gives eight numbered tips (pp. 27–28):

1. Before starting to write, do two things. First, ensure that you have a strong thesis. There's a good way to tell if you have one, but it takes courage: write on some notepaper, "I contend that—" and complete the sentence, then ask whether somebody else arguing the same thesis would intrigue you, whether it's complex or controversial enough to sustain lengthy exposition, and whether you have really stuck your neck out rather than pussyfooting. Second, have on hand a list of concrete details and apt quotations, and be ready to use them — abstract generalizations make a reader mutter "Sheesh" and tune out, while concrete details make her think, "Hey, this person has really done their homework."
2. If you choke at the thought of beginning and make false starts, try what a Pulitzer Prize–winning reporter once advised: "Pull yourself back from your desk, take a deep breath, and say to yourself, 'OK, now, what is it I'm really trying to say?' Then simply say it — talk it."
3. If you still feel discouraged, let the opener stand roughed out, or not written at all, and return to it after finishing the first draft — there's no rule that paragraphs must be written sequentially, and you may discover, as Trimble has "demonstrated to many a student through the years," that your second paragraph is your real opener.
4. Use the front-door approach. Idle chat will destroy your credibility.
5. Use natural, simple prose — the simpler the better. Grace notes can be added later, but the ideas themselves must carry the piece: "If they look banal to you, there's only one remedy: upgrade them. Don't try to camouflage their weakness with razzle-dazzle rhetoric. You'll razzle-dazzle yourself right into a bog of bull."
6. Unless you have good reason otherwise, make the opener full-bodied. A "splinter-sized" opener — two or three sentences, lacking point — signals to the reader that the writer is short on ideas; a "barnlike" opener signals a lack of proportion. (Trimble notes the exception: "there's always the glorious exception that makes a dictum like this look silly.")
7. Consider opening with a dramatically brief sentence — four or five words — which forces a bold assertion, gives the reader a handle on what follows, and surprises her, since most opening sentences run 15 to 25 words.
8. Where possible, organize the opening paragraph so the biggest punch — the strongest statement of the thesis — comes at the end. This builds toward a climax, gives a springboard into the next paragraph, and avoids repeating the thesis.

**Evidence and examples.** Every example in the chapter is a student essay opener on a Shakespeare play; Trimble explains in a footnote that he uses Shakespeare examples throughout this chapter and "in the chapters on 'Middles' and 'Closers' that follow... partly for their eloquence, partly because Shakespeare is our most universal author, and partly for purposes of continuity" (p. 24).

Front-door example, on Prince Hal in *1 Henry IV* (p. 24):

> Prince Hal is as hard to crack as a walnut. "I know you all," he says of Falstaff & Co. in his soliloquy ending I.ii, but what friend — what reader even — can speak with equal confidence about Hal himself? His true nature seems finally to be as riddling as Hamlet's or Cleopatra's; indeed, he seems at times to be a hybrid of those two characters: infinitely various, theatrical, cunning past man's thought, loving, brutal, equivocal — the list goes on. It's little wonder that Hotspur, so childishly open and simple, often surpasses Hal as the reader's favorite. It's also little wonder that we are hard pressed to decide whether Hal is actually likable or merely admirable.

Trimble calls out its directness, its command of specifics, and its refusal to hedge as the marks of the front door.

Back-door example, plot-summary opening, same assignment (p. 25):

> In the second scene of the first Act of William Shakespeare's The First Part of King Henry the Fourth, Prince Hal presents a soliloquy which serves as a crux of this play. Although this play would appear by the title to tell of King Henry IV, actually the principal character is the King's son, Hal. The play reveals what seems to be a remarkable change in character for the Prince and follows his exploits in a civil war waged against his father ....

He notes it continues as plot summary for four more sentences, then puts the test to the reader directly — "Would you be eager to read on? Would you even be awake to read on?" (p. 25) — and points out the padding tells: the author's full name instead of "Shakespeare," the unwieldy complete play title instead of *I Henry IV*, and the Act and scene "laboriously written out" instead of "I.ii" (p. 25).

Smoke-screen back-door example, on *Hamlet* (p. 25):

> Shakespeare's Hamlet, admired for its poetic style and intriguing characters, has remained a classic for over three centuries. The character of Hamlet is probably one of Shakespeare's most perplexing and most pleasing. He is easily identified with because of his multi-faceted personality and his realistic problems.

Trimble reports that after a conference in which he "helped her to read her opener from the reader's perspective," this same student abandoned the Hamlet paper and wrote fresh on King Claudius. Her revised opener (p. 26):

> He killed his brother. He married his brother's wife. He stole his brother's crown. A cold-hearted murderer, he is described by his brother's ghost as "that incestuous, that adulterate beast" (I.v.42). The bare facts appear to stamp him an utter moral outlaw. Nonetheless, as his soliloquies and anguished asides reveal, no person in Hamlet demonstrates so mixed a true nature as Claudius, the newly made King of Denmark.

Trimble uses the pair to demonstrate the lesson directly rather than just state it: same student, same play's cast of characters, one essay reworked from back door to front door once she grasped "that an essay is only as good as its thesis, that the first four or five sentences are make-or-break, that a back-door approach is transparently evasive, and that it's a delightful challenge to wake up your reader" (p. 26).

Three further front-door openers, from classmates later in the same semester, offered to show the directness, concrete detail, and "salesmanship" a strong opener has once a writer has learned the lesson (p. 26). Trimble quotes the first in full and the other two only for their opening sentences.

Full opener, on *The Taming of the Shrew* (p. 26):

> In The Taming of the Shrew, the servant is really a lord, and the lord's wife is really a page, and the schoolmaster is really a suitor, and the crazy suitor is really a wise old fox, and the perfect beauty is really a shrew, and the shrew is really a perfect wife, and things are not as they seem. Even the play itself pretends not to be a play by putting on a production within a production. In it, three characters are being duped by this rampant role-playing. By the examples of Sly, Kate, and Bianca, Shakespeare acquaints us with the effects of wealth, love, and power, respectively, and shows how the emergence of an inner (perhaps truer) character can be said to have been tamed. However, the "taming" occurs only as a result of the manipulation of the supposers by the posers. Moreover, while things are not as they seem because of the dual-roled characters, neither does the "taming" suggested by the title ever really take place.

Trimble later points back to this passage (in tip 8, p. 28) as the model for saving the thesis's biggest punch for the paragraph's last sentence.

Partial opener (initial sentences only), on the occult in Shakespeare (pp. 26–27):

> The occult element leavens Shakespeare's works with a pinch of the unknown and an implication that it should remain so. His artful but often annoying ambiguity seldom allows more than a fleeting glimpse at a forbidden terrain before it is bulldozed out of sight by convenient rationales. Several examples of Shakespeare's significant use of the occult immediately come to mind: the witches in Macbeth, the antics of Titania and Oberon in A Midsummer Night's Dream, the Ghost in Hamlet, and the figure of Owen Glendower in I Henry IV.

Partial opener (initial sentences only), on the Fool in *King Lear* (p. 27):

> "He that walketh with wise men shall be wise; But the companions of fools shall smart for it." King Solomon's proverb appears reversed in King Lear for it is a wise Fool who accompanies and counsels a seemingly foolish king. In the play, the Fool assumes myriad roles — that of teacher, loyal servant, comedian, and often the punitive voice of Lear's own conscience.

After the three, Trimble comments, in the conversational, direct-address register that is itself doing instructional work in this chapter: "Don't you know these writers had fun?" (p. 27) — modeling, with his own opener-to-the-tips-section, the same front-door directness he is teaching.

**Terms introduced.**
- **Front-door approach** — entering an essay directly, stating a clear, confident thesis at the outset (p. 24).
- **Back-door approach** — delaying the thesis through plot summary, throat-clearing, or padding (p. 24).
- **"I contend that—" test** — a diagnostic for thesis strength: complete the sentence and judge whether the resulting claim is intriguing, complex or controversial enough to sustain exposition, and genuinely committed rather than "pussyfooting" (p. 27).
- **"Establishing the Large Critical Overview" / "The Art of Saying Nothing Profoundly"** — Trimble's ironic labels for a back-door opener that sounds accomplished through grand generalization but conveys nothing (p. 25).
- **Full-bodied opener** — an opener with enough substance to avoid being "splinter-sized" (too short, pointless) or "barnlike" (too long, disproportionate) (p. 28).

**Connections.** A footnote on p. 24 states that the Shakespeare-based student examples in this chapter continue into the later chapters on "Middles" and "Closers," making Openers the first of a three-chapter sequence on the parts of an essay.

#### Chapter 4 — Middles

> "My style of writing is chiefly grounded upon an early enthusiasm for [Thomas H.] Huxley, the greatest of all masters of orderly exposition. He taught me the importance of giving to every argument a simple structure." —H. L. Mencken
> (p. 29)

**Claim.** The middle of an essay is inseparable from its opening because it "explains and develops the thesis," and inseparable from the process by which the writer arrived at that thesis, because "it amounts to a coherent retelling of that process" (p. 29). Trimble frames expository writing — informative writing with an implicit persuasive goal — through an extended analogy to a prosecuting attorney building a case for a jury, and argues that a successful essay depends on a sure sense of audience plus five further essentials: a well-defined thesis, a clear strategy, strong evidence, a clean narrative line, and a persuasive closing (p. 31).

**Mechanism.**

*The Analogy.* Trimble develops the prosecutor comparison point by point. Before presenting her case, the prosecutor sizes up her jury — "How sophisticated are they? What are their interests, their prejudices, their intellectual capacities?" — since that assessment determines her delivery and even her choice of evidence; the writer must do the same, but by "intuition, the laws of probability, and guesswork" (p. 30). She then states her thesis in precise language, calls witnesses (the evidence) to prove it, saves her strongest exhibit for last, and closes by recapping her high points and explaining why her version of events is the only reasonable one — following "the age-old formula of debaters: 'Tell 'em what you're going to tell 'em, tell it to 'em, and then tell 'em what you've told 'em'" (p. 31). A footnote qualifies the formula: "The formula works, of course, only when it's kept discreetly veiled. The trick is to follow it without appearing to; otherwise your presentation sounds mechanical" (p. 31, n. 2). While building her case she is simultaneously anticipating and defusing the opposing lawyer's contentions, demonstrating mastery of the facts, clarifying what's at issue, defining her terms, and supplying a running summary of how the evidence connects (pp. 30–31).

*The Checklist.* "Virtually everything our prosecutor did finds an exact correspondence in successful essay writing." At the top sits "a sure sense of the audience" — ignore it, Trimble writes, "and you might as well not even begin. It would be like telling a locker-room joke to your grandmother." After audience-sense come five essentials "which I recommend you memorize. You'll find them in every successful essay":

1. A well-defined thesis
2. A clear strategy
3. Strong evidence
4. A clean narrative line
5. A persuasive closing

(p. 31)

*The Hypothetical Case (process).* Trimble walks a hypothetical student through the stages that precede drafting: research and record all evidence (examples, statistics, quotations, arguments); organize the findings into lists, arriving at roughly 20 arguments favoring abolition of capital punishment and another 20 favoring its retention; weigh the arguments to reach an intuitive decision; classify the chosen arguments into major groups — moral, economic, political, legal — and work out how they interconnect; and decide a sequence for presenting them, weighing quality against quantity and settling on "an increasingly persuasive order of arguments," reasoning that "such an order would be agreeably climactic" (p. 33). Only then does he fix his audience firmly in mind, since audience determines "his choice of tone (serious, bantering, ironic, indignant), his diction (elegant, informal, tempered, blunt), his sentence structure (complex, occasionally complex, simple), and his mode of argument (technical, nontechnical, objective, subjective)" (p. 33). A footnote distinguishes this from writing to please a reader at the expense of one's own views: "I am not endorsing the practice of 'writing for the teacher' — i.e., giving the teacher... what you presume he wants to hear at the expense of what you genuinely believe. That's a sellout. I am recommending, though, that the writer remember who his reader is" (p. 33, n. 4).

*Continuity and Signposting.* Trimble states the underlying discipline directly, in a passage set off from the surrounding prose:

> "Good writers are sticklers for continuity. They won't let themselves write a sentence that isn't clearly connected to the ones immediately preceding and following it. They want their prose to flow, and they know this is the only way to achieve that beautiful effect." (p. 40)

He holds that the strongest connections come not from mechanical connectives but from "a coherent understanding of what he wants to say, a simple style, the occasional repetition of key words, and the careful use of pronouns such as this and that," comparing the effect to a furniture maker who "uses interlocking tongues and grooves to do the work of nails and screws" (p. 41).

Where the argument turns, or an idea needs to be paralleled or contrasted with an earlier one, the writer needs a more explicit connective — a conjunctive adverb or brief transitional phrase — a technique Trimble names directly: "I call this 'signposting' an argument" (p. 41). He draws on Rudolf Flesch's *The Art of Plain Talk* to distinguish bookish conjunctive adverbs (used chiefly in print) from conversational equivalents, noting the equivalence in each case "is approximate, not perfect," and that the bookish adverbs "can afford you greater variety and precision of meaning" (p. 41). Trimble sets out this bookish/conversational vocabulary as a boxed list on p. 41.

The list is set as a boxed panel with a drop shadow, indented from both margins, holding a single alphabetical list of transitional words in bold serif type, read down the left column and then down the right, with no column headings. Conversational equivalents follow certain entries in parentheses; entries with no parenthesis have no bookish/conversational split to show. In printed order (p. 41):

| Left column | Right column |
| --- | --- |
| above all | in particular |
| accordingly (and so) | instead |
| admittedly | in summary |

The bulk of the list, spilling across two columns, is itself part of Trimble's point: the sheer number of these words is what signposting an argument has to draw on. The list sits entirely on p. 41; p. 42 continues with the "Final Tips" subhead and carries no box or comparison of its own.

He recommends keeping the list "propped up before you the next few times you write an essay," noting it will supply directional signals, save word-hunting, and even suggest "an occasional new avenue of thought simply by tempting your mind to explore other directions of argument" (p. 42).

Trimble also reframes how a paragraph's opening sentence should be understood: instead of a topic sentence, view it as a bridge sentence — "aimed at smoothing our way into the new paragraph" — reporting that "more than one student has said that's the single best tip they've carried away from their writing conferences with me" (p. 43).

*Final Tips.* The chapter closes with seven numbered tips (pp. 42–43):

1. Ask yourself the reader's invariable question — "Well, what does it finally add up to?" — before writing a final draft, and be ready to answer it: "It finally adds up to this, in my opinion. . . ." If the reply contains an original perception, is debatable, and can be stated in one sentence, it's a good thesis; then prove it.
2. Think of yourself as a prosecuting attorney, your essay as a case, and your reader as a highly skeptical jury.
3. Determine what things you must substantiate — as a prosecutor must substantiate motive, means, and opportunity — classify your evidence accordingly, and substantiate them one at a time. Trimble calls this "dividing up the proof."
4. Signpost your argument every step of the way — tell the reader how many pieces of evidence are coming ("Three examples will bear this out. First, the original treaty of 1923. . . ."); if one argument is stronger than the others, save it for last and label it as such ("Finally and most seriously, capital punishment strikes at the very basis of morality itself.").
5. Assertions are fine, but unless proved with hard evidence they remain simply assertions: "assert, then support; assert, then support; assert, then support — and so on throughout your essay."
6. Transitional and one-sentence paragraphs are special-occasion devices with their own rules; the normal paragraph should be organized around a single major point, however many examples, qualifications, or illuminating sentences it draws in — "One main contention per paragraph" — "it's a sensible guideline to follow."
7. Treat each paragraph's opening sentence as a bridge sentence rather than a topic sentence, smoothing the reader's way into the new paragraph.

(pp. 42–43)

**Evidence and examples.**

*The hypothetical capital-punishment essay.* As demonstration of the checklist in action, Trimble quotes the imagined student executing each stage of the process. The thesis sentence closing his opening paragraph:

> "This gradual trend toward the abolition of capital punishment reflects a growing awareness that such extreme punishment doesn't make sense — economically, morally, or pragmatically." (p. 34)

The economic paragraph following directly from it:

> "Considered from a coldly economic point of view, capital punishment is a waste of human resources. Instead of killing a man, society should take advantage of his ability to work and pay restitution." (p. 34)

and the paragraph building the strongest economic point last:

> "Nor let us overlook the staggering court costs. With capital punishment, a single, speedy trial is unheard of. Almost invariably a case will be retried repeatedly as the condemned person exhausts every possible appeal and delay." (p. 34)

Moving to the stronger, moral group of reasons:

> "But beyond the mere economics of the issue, capital punishment is a moral outrage. First, it is a basic violation of the Judeo-Christian ethic, the cornerstone of our democratic society." (p. 34)

He supports this by quoting Jesus, Clarence Darrow, and George Bernard Shaw arguing that compassion rather than revenge is the more civilized justice, and counters the "eye for an eye" objection with the superseding commandment "Thou shalt not kill." The second moral argument:

> "Furthermore, capital punishment — which is essentially a lynch mob by proxy — lowers the standards of public morality. In effect, it encourages barbarism by the state — indeed, it brings society down to the level of a ruthless murderer. Once the state has the power to murder with the grace of the statute book, historically it loses all sense of proportion. We have seen this happen in Great Britain in the 18th century, when even the pettiest crimes were thought fit for punishment at the gallows." (pp. 34–35)

The third and strongest moral argument:

> "Finally and most seriously, capital punishment strikes at the very basis of morality itself. Morality rests upon the fact that we are mortals, frail and imperfect in our understanding, not infallible. By contrast, capital punishment presumes that man can set himself up as God, and that juries never make mistakes. The moral presumption in this is surely as great as that of the criminal who takes the life of his victim." (p. 35)

The pragmatic argument, introduced "with the gusto of Churchill on D-Day":

> "Both economically and morally, then, capital punishment simply doesn't make sense. But the most damaging indictment against the practice is pragmatic: it fails to achieve its purpose, which is the deterrence of crime. Now why does it not deter a criminal? Because it rests upon a false assumption: that murder or rape, for example, is committed consciously, is premeditated. But this is patently not so. Most capital crimes are crimes of passion, committed unthinkingly in the heat of the moment. The criminal never considers punishment." (p. 35)

He supports this with statistics that most murders occur within the family and that death-penalty states show murder rates no lower than other states, plus the historical example of pickpockets working the crowds at British public executions. He then adds a fairness argument:

> "So capital punishment doesn't work. But when we try to force it to work, we find that we can't even administer it fairly. First, there is the economic bias: the rich can always pay their way out, while the poor will die. Second, the meting out of the death penalty often depends upon whom you kill, for human life is not valued equally." (p. 35)

illustrated with examples of criminals executed for killing public figures against others paroled in three years for killing people "of lesser renown." The essay's closing sentence:

> "The evidence all in, the conclusions are inescapable: economically the proponent of capital punishment is a waster, morally he is a bankrupt, and pragmatically he is a fool." (p. 36)

*The Model.* Trimble presents an essay written by a college junior, Danny Robbins, later a professional sportswriter, as "a splendid example of all five points on our earlier checklist, but especially of #2: a clear strategy. This is about as well-organized an essay as you are likely to see." He connects it to the chapter epigraph, quoting George Bernard Shaw: "Effectiveness of assertion is the Alpha and Omega of style. He who has nothing to assert has no style and can have none; he who has something to assert will go as far in power of style as its momentousness and his conviction will carry him" (p. 36). The essay, on *Antony and Cleopatra*, is reproduced here in full, verbatim, per the brief's rule that worked examples are not paraphrased.

> **The Character and Purpose of Caesar**
>
> Octavius Caesar in Shakespeare's *Antony and Cleopatra* embodies all the ideals of ancient Rome. His pursuit of world power at any cost is consistent with the militaristic, male-oriented society of which he is a part. The Roman spirit, it seems, is so deeply ingrained within Caesar that there is absolutely nothing else in the world of any importance to him besides strength and conquest. In fact, he seems so one-dimensional a character that he may not be a true character at all. I think he is merely a symbol — a voice that recurs in the play not to capture the imagination or make one learn something about human nature but rather to provide a measuring stick by which one can calculate change in Mark Antony. (p. 36)

> Certainly there are aspects of Caesar's character that cry out for further development by Shakespeare. He is so young, yet acts so old. And nowhere does Caesar show the sensitivity, curiosity, or frivolity one might expect from a 23-year-old. It seems that if Shakespeare really wanted to make Caesar a provocative character, he could have done something with these qualities. But he doesn't. It appears that Caesar is so typecast, so stereotyped as a Roman, that the reader or spectator must view him for what he stands for rather than for what happens to him in the play. No matter what the situation, his actions are perfectly Roman. And in this manner, it appears that his function is like that of a "constant" in a mathematical equation, a figure of never-changing value. Antony would be the "variable" in the equation. He is changed by the passion of Cleopatra, and Caesar's function is to provide contrast for this. Caesar, then, must not change. Three instances, covering the entire time span of the play, bring this out. (pp. 36–37)

> In Act I, Caesar criticizes Antony behind his back for the good times Antony has in Egypt. The play has just begun, and Caesar is already telling Lepidus that
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;From Alexandria
> This is the news: he fishes, drinks, and wastes
> The lamps of night in revel; is not more manlike
> Than Cleopatra, nor the queen of Ptolemy. . . .
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;You shall find there
> A man who is the abstract of all faults
> That all men follow. (I.4.3–10)
>
> This is Caesar's very first speech, and in it one finds a 23-year-old man condemning pleasure. Caesar cannot understand why Antony does not take up arms with the triumvirate, why pleasure comes before duty. This opening speech is a clear disclosure of Caesar's personality. But perhaps more importantly, Antony's values are being compared to Caesar's. Not only do we see the things that Caesar values — masculinity, work, ambition — but it is significant that Antony is the subject of Caesar's first lines. In the total scope of the play Antony is the "subject" of all of them, whether he is mentioned by name or not. (p. 37)

> Then in Act II there is another, more telling glimpse into Caesar's character. He and Antony are trying to patch up their damaged relationship, but Caesar pursues reconciliation in a purely utilitarian manner. He is a Roman first, a friend second. Caesar acts purely as a soldier. And he is concerned with Antony as merely a once-famous soldier who can help him defeat Pompey. Caesar is so wrapped up in his quest for world power that he will sell his sister "whom no brother / Did ever love so dearly" (II.2.150–151) to Antony to get Antony's support. Antony seems to go along with Caesar to appease him for the moment and end the conversation. Nevertheless, the end result is that the shallowness of Caesar's nature is exposed again. He, unlike Antony, shows no regard for the beauty of human relationships. He is concerned only with using people to advance his military goals. The fact that Caesar shows no love or compassion — not even for his sister — highlights the relationship between Antony and Cleopatra. (p. 37)

> Caesar acts no differently in the final Act of the play. In fact, he appears more ruthless. After Antony's death, Cleopatra seeks mercy from Caesar. Caesar — who in the war against Antony has just slaughtered many men in his own self-interest — says:
>
> She [Cleopatra] shall soon know of us . . .
> How honorable and how kindly we
> Determine for her. For Caesar cannot live
> To be ungentle. (V.1.58–61)
>
> This is, of course, a joke. Caesar has murdered Pompey, Lepidus, and Antony. The "mercy" Caesar plans for Cleopatra is to use her as a public display of his "generous heart." Proculeius lets slip this notion: "let the world see / His nobleness well acted" (V.2.44–45). For the first time Caesar is making an outward show of pity and kindness and, true to his nature, he is sincere about none of it. Furthermore, the sparing of Cleopatra's life has a military purpose — to make him look good in the eyes of his subjects — just like everything else he does. (pp. 37–38)

> Thus Caesar's character never changes from beginning to end. He is not to be pitied or even contemplated to any great extent by the audience. Caesar acts simply as a standard by which one can study the effects of Cleopatra's love on Antony. Shakespeare seems to be using Caesar as a symbol of Roman society, a yardstick by which Antony's deviance from Roman ideas can be measured. There is nothing deep or stimulating about the man. His traits are negative and obvious, so obvious that I think Shakespeare made them this way on purpose. Caesar is supposed to be a model Roman, whereas Antony is supposed to be — and is — a richly complex human being. (p. 38)

*The Model Analyzed.* Trimble critiques the essay against the five-point checklist.

On the thesis: he quotes it again — "I think he is merely a symbol — a voice that recurs in the play not to capture the imagination or make one learn something about human nature but rather to provide a measuring stick by which one can calculate change in Mark Antony" — and notes it is "placed right where it ought to be for greatest effect — at the climactic end of the opening paragraph." The lead-in "I think" both "primes us for a major assertion" and signals that the claim is offered as opinion rather than fact, yet "it's a firmly held opinion, and we admire his courage for stating it so unequivocally." Danny is "boldly crawling out on an interpretive limb, just as I'll advise you to do in Chapter 9" (p. 39).

On strategy: Trimble reduces the opening paragraph to three linked assertions:

> A. Caesar embodies the Roman ideal.
> B. In fact, he is nothing but the Roman ideal — that is, he is one-dimensional, a walking symbol.
> C. From B we must infer that his dramatic function is to serve as a yardstick by which we can measure the change in his fellow Roman, Mark Antony. (p. 39)

He observes that proving A and B lets Danny persuade the reader that C is "at the very least, probably valid," and that the curtain-raising sentence — "Caesar, then, must not change. Three instances, covering the entire time span of the play, bring this out" — is what "Mencken had in mind when he spoke of 'the importance of giving to every argument a simple structure'" (p. 39), tying the analysis back to the chapter's own epigraph.

He then points to the parallel opening of each supporting paragraph as evidence of transparent signposting:

> a. "In Act I, Caesar criticizes Antony . . ."
> b. "Then in Act II there is another, more telling glimpse . . ."
> c. "Caesar acts no differently in the final Act of the play. In fact, he appears more ruthless." (p. 39)

and notes "a progression in the persuasiveness of the examples. Each is stronger than the last, thus building toward an intellectually and aesthetically satisfying climax" (pp. 39–40).

On evidence: the three examples "cover the entire time span of the play," which Trimble says defuses any objection that the evidence is stacked, and on four occasions Danny "quoted actual lines," which "greatly enhances the concreteness of the examples" rather than leaving the reader to "supply the appropriate textual support" (p. 40).

On narrative line: "There are no bumps in this essay. Each sentence, each paragraph, is hinged on the one that precedes it," a continuity Trimble attributes both to Danny's clear plan of attack and to deliberate parallel structuring — paragraph 2 repeating the pattern of paragraph 1, each of those paragraphs ending on a key sentence, paragraphs 3–5 all beginning alike, and the closing paragraph looping back to the opening paragraph (p. 40).

On the closing: the final paragraph is "a beautiful wrap-up: succinct, bold, and complete enough to gather in all the major points the essay has been making. We feel them now fixed in our memory" (p. 40).

*Bergen Evans's bridge sentences.* To illustrate the bridge-sentence technique, Trimble reproduces eleven paragraph openers from Bergen Evans's *Atlantic Monthly* article "But What's a Dictionary For?," a review of Merriam-Webster's *Third New International Dictionary*:

> a. What underlines all this sound and fury?
> b. So monstrous a discrepancy in evaluation requires us to examine basic principles.
> c. Yet wild wails arose.
> d. More subtly, but persuasively, it has changed under the influence of mass education and the growth of democracy.
> e. And the papers have no choice.
> f. And so back to our questions: what's a dictionary for, and how, in 1962, can it best do what it ought to do?
> g. Even in so settled a matter as spelling, a dictionary cannot always be absolute.
> h. Has he been betrayed?
> i. Under these circumstances, what is a dictionary to do?
> j. An illustration is furnished by an editorial in *The Washington Post* (January 17, 1962).
> k. In part, the trouble is due to the fact that there is no standard for standard.
> (p. 43)

Trimble comments that "even out of context, these sentences suggest how skillfully Evans is guiding his readers — building bridges for us, persuading us. We never come to a new paragraph wondering, 'Where am I? How did I get here?'" (p. 43).

**Terms introduced.**
- **Signposting** — using a conjunctive adverb or brief transitional phrase to tell the reader what kind of thought is coming next, especially when an argument turns or when an idea is to be paralleled or contrasted with an earlier one (p. 41).
- **Dividing up the proof** — classifying evidence according to the several things a thesis must substantiate, then proving each in turn, by analogy to a prosecutor establishing motive, means, and opportunity (p. 42).
- **Bridge sentence** — Trimble's proposed replacement for "topic sentence": a paragraph's opening sentence understood as smoothing the reader's way into the new paragraph rather than announcing its topic (p. 43).
- **"One main contention per paragraph"** — Trimble's guideline that a normal paragraph, however many examples or qualifications it draws in, stays organized around a single major point (p. 43).

**Connections.** Continues the Shakespeare-essay demonstration begun in Chapter 3 (Openers): the checklist critique explicitly calls back to "our earlier imaginary student" and to "the front-door approach in action" established there (p. 39). Points forward explicitly to Chapter 9, where discussing the model essay's thesis Trimble notes the student is "boldly crawling out on an interpretive limb, just as I'll advise you to do in Chapter 9" (p. 39). The five-point checklist and prosecutor analogy set out here recur in condensed, procedural form as the chapter's "Final Tips" (pp. 42–43). #### Chapter 5 — Closers

**Claim.** A final paragraph carries as much weight as an opening one, because it is what the reader's memory retains once the piece is finished. A closer that merely stops, or that idles through a rote recap, wastes the position of greatest emphasis in the whole piece.

**Mechanism.** Trimble builds the chapter around the internal monologue of the "capital-punishment student" from earlier chapters, caught at the point of writing his own closer, tired and tempted to quit early:

> "This is ridiculous—my brain's turning to mush. Maybe I'll just stop here. The piece is virtually done anyway—I've made my main points. Besides, who's going to know the difference?" (p. 44)

Conscience and Common Sense talk him out of it:

> "No, I guess I can't quit yet. Buckley wouldn't accept an argument that merely stops. He'll want to see the thing end, to enjoy a sense of closure. He once said that's a basic aesthetic desire in all of us. 'Every reader wants his final reward.'" (p. 44)

The line "Every reader wants his final reward" is Buckley's, quoted by the student, not Trimble's own formulation.

The student then reasons his way to why the ending matters, on grounds of memory and recency:

> "Then, of course, there's the matter of what he'll be able to recall. Since my memory certainly has its limits, I'm sure his does, too. If that's the case, his sense of this piece is bound to be colored by the last sentences he reads. My opener may have disposed him to read eagerly, and my middle paragraphs may have sustained his interest, but my final graf may well be the chief thing he carries away with him. That's certainly the way it is with the last minute of a basketball game, or the last kiss at the door. Hmm. I can see that I have to make it memorable—as powerful as my opener, if I can." (p. 44)

He continues into page 45, working out what the closer must do and must avoid:

> "But I wonder how I should slant it toward him? I suppose, if he's anything like me, by the time he's gotten this far, he'll be tired. He's bound to welcome a final gathering up of my argument in a form that can be grasped handily. This would also leave him feeling that my argument really does hang together. He mustn't have any doubts on that score. I want him utterly convinced." (pp. 44–45)

> "But I imagine he'll be bored if my closer simply recaps earlier points, and especially if I repeat my earlier phrasing. He'll feel I'm merely going through the motions. He'll also feel that he's stopped learning things. I've got to keep him hooked to the very end. I've got to leave him convinced that my mind is still blazing with ideas." (p. 45)

Out of this reasoning, Trimble names the formula the student arrives at:

> "The closer our student finally devises is half-summary, half-conclusion, similar to a prosecutor's closing appeal to the jury. He neatly sums up the high points of his evidence, reexplaining why his argument is reasonable. He also takes care to point out its important implications, so that the reader will be convinced that the argument is substantial. He makes the whole paragraph self-contained and packed so that it could serve as a fair substitute for the essay itself, as indeed it may in his reader's overworked memory. And he finishes off with a sentence that has such satisfying finality that his last period feels unnecessary." (p. 45)

Trimble scopes when this formula applies:

> "For a long paper—say, 10 pages or more—this formula for a closer is ideal. In fact, it's obligatory, since you will have given your reader a volume of ideas to digest. Unless your presentation has been unusually coherent, he's apt to be left seeing trees but no forest. He really needs a systematic wrap-up." (p. 45)

> "With shorter papers, though, you should take liberties with this formula, particularly if your next-to-last paragraph has already gathered up many of the threads of your argument. You certainly don't want to insult your reader's intelligence." (p. 45)

Regardless of length, he states three imperatives that always hold:

> "There remain, however, three imperatives, no matter how brief your essay.
> 1. Focus on your main point (which may be your final point).
> 2. Gratify us with at least one last new twist or phrase to make your point memorable.
> 3. End with emotional impact." (p. 45)

The chapter closes by returning to the epigraph: a weak sentence-end can be fixed by the sentence after it, but an essay has no sentence after its last one.

> "What F. L. Lucas said at the opening of this chapter about a sentence-end is probably even more true of an essay-end. A weak sentence-end can always be recouped by a strong following sentence; a weak essay-end cannot. Knowing this, many experienced writers take the precaution, during the early drafting stage, of setting aside a couple of choice ideas or phrases for use in their closer. That's a smart policy." (p. 47)

**Evidence and examples.**

*Epigraph*, attributed to F. L. Lucas, not Trimble:

> "The most emphatic place in a clause or sentence is the end. This is the climax; and during the momentary pause that follows, that last word continues, as it were, to reverberate in the reader's mind. It has, in fact, the last word. One should therefore think twice about what one puts at a sentence-end." — F. L. Lucas (p. 44)

*The four student closers.* Trimble presents these as satisfying the three imperatives, all drawn from short essays on King Lear written for the same upper-division Shakespeare course. He frames their apparent naturalness as the product of practice rather than raw talent:

> "What looks so natural is really the effect of repeated practice, careful revision, and considerable reader feedback, not just from me but from their classmates as well." (p. 46)

Closer 1 (p. 46), which builds to a closing antithesis and embeds a direct quotation from Shakespeare's *King Lear* (V.iii.8–13):

> "After his defeat and capture, Lear's transformation of character is complete. To be a prisoner of his daughters should be the most humiliating experience in a king's life, yet we find Lear expressing real happiness. Because he is with Cordelia, the longing for power and loyalty has been replaced with a desire for love and compassion. At last Lear sees a love without price and power. He actually looks forward to being a prisoner with Cordelia:
>
> Come, let's away to prison.
> We two alone will sing like birds i' th' cage.
> When thou dost ask me blessing, I'll kneel down
> And ask of thee forgiveness. So we'll live,
> And pray, and sing, and tell old tales, and laugh
> At gilded butterflies . . . (V.iii.8–13)
>
> The kind of love he now wants is the antithesis of the worship that his other daughters promised him. Lear has discovered a human love based on sharing and feeling, and found that it is worth far more than crowns or kingdoms. The tragedy of King Lear is that Lear's ideal universe discovers itself in a prison rather than in a kingdom. For when Lear had the power to preserve love he could not see it, and when he had the wisdom to see love he could not preserve it." (p. 46)

The verse quoted inside this closer is Shakespeare's, from *King Lear*, not the student's or Trimble's own line — the student is demonstrating the technique of ending on someone else's most concentrated language.

Closer 2 (p. 46), shorter, building to a closing tricolon:

> "So, by a series of occurrences very close to the core of the man, Lear, this king becomes aware of life just as it is lost to him forever. The only non-static character in the play, Lear becomes the tragic one. The tragedy is one like saving a man's life so that he may be executed. But, in that saving, Lear is, if only briefly, whole, magnificent, wise." (p. 46)

Closer 3 (pp. 46–47), which ends on a stated moral judgment:

> "Even though Lear changes into a wise, compassionate, and fit ruler, his sorrows begin anew. The sentimentalist's phrase "poetic justice" holds no meaning for Shakespeare. Ruin wrought in the old king's heart and brain is irreparable, and the tornado that whirls him to his doom carries with it the just and the unjust. Lear's little golden pause of peace, when he and Cordelia reunite, followed by the intolerably piercing scene in which he bears her dead body out of prison muttering that they have hanged his "poor fool," shows that even the virtuous suffer—not at the hands of the gods, who are indifferent, but at the claws of beastly humans. In King Lear, the consequences of imprudent action were never followed out to a grimmer end." (pp. 46–47)

Closer 4 (p. 47), which ends on a quotation from the play itself, again drawn from Shakespeare rather than the student:

> "It seems we can really only speculate as to what Shakespeare is trying to say about life in King Lear. There are no religious morals or Elizabethan motifs jumping out at us like handy crutches. Perhaps Shakespeare is trying to convey in Lear an inner human dignity in suffering. Lear, the exalted, suffers with the common. He shares with all of his brothers the ability to suffer. Suffering is his bond. His ability to feel the pangs of rejection, defeat, and total disillusionment enables Lear, who has "ever but slenderly known himself," to achieve a spiritual stature in death denied him in life." (p. 47)

The phrase "ever but slenderly known himself" is a quotation from *King Lear* itself (Regan's line about Lear, Act I, scene i), embedded by the student and, again, attributable to Shakespeare rather than to the essay's author or to Trimble.

**Terms introduced.**

- **Closer** — Trimble's term for an essay's final paragraph, treated throughout as a distinct compositional problem with its own requirements, parallel to the opener.
- **Half-summary, half-conclusion** — Trimble's description of the closer formula appropriate to long papers: a paragraph that sums up the evidence, restates why the argument is reasonable, states the argument's implications, and is self-contained enough to stand in for the whole essay in the reader's memory (p. 45).
- **Three imperatives** — the chapter's named, numbered requirement that holds regardless of essay length: focus on the main point, supply at least one new twist or phrase, end with emotional impact (p. 45).

**Connections.** The chapter opens by having the student aim to make his closer "as powerful as my opener" (p. 44), referring back to an earlier chapter on openers that this one is built to pair with; that earlier chapter's number is not confirmed within this excerpt. The chapter's closing paragraph (p. 47) explicitly returns to its own epigraph from F. L. Lucas, extending his point about sentence-endings to essay-endings.

#### Chapter 6 — Diction

**Claim.** Word choice does most of the work of style. Trimble organizes the chapter around three word-level habits — cutting to the fewest words that carry the meaning, preferring active verbs to passive ones, and reaching for fresh phrasing over stock phrasing — and argues that a writer's command of these three, more than anything else, produces the impression of confidence or of evasiveness that a reader forms.

**Mechanism.**

*Conciseness.* Trimble traces wordiness to habit: school assignments that reward hitting a word count condition writers to think in "long, ready-made phrases," which dulls sensitivity to words as words.

> "We may hear someone say "at this point in time" and pride ourselves on recognizing the phrase as a cliche, but we'll probably not notice that it's also redundant. (What does in time say that at this point doesn't already say?)" (p. 48)

The fix is a habit of substitution — a short set of stock wordy phrases replaced by their plain equivalents:

> "Why say "in the event that" when you can say "if"? Or "in order to" when you can say "to"? Or "for the reason that" when you can say "since"? Why write "They speak with great bitterness" when you can write "They speak bitterly"?" (p. 49)

The diagnostic question he has writers ask of their own drafts is: "What words aren't pulling their weight here?" (p. 49) — asked sentence by sentence, cutting synonyms and collapsing wordy phrases into single strong nouns.

*Vigorous verbs.* Trimble first establishes why "X does Y" is the natural sentence order — it puts the actor up front, matching a reader's curiosity, and it keeps the grammatical subject and the actual actor as the same entity. He then gives the working definitions:

> "We call a verb "active"—short for "active voice"—when its grammatical subject is doing whatever the verb describes. We call a verb "passive" when its grammatical subject isn't doing anything; instead, it's being done to." (p. 50)

He sets off two summary statements as points to remember:

> "Active verbs move us forward; passive verbs move us backward. Active verbs give us the actor up front; passive verbs make us wait to learn the actor." (p. 50)

> "A passive verb always includes some form of the verb be—is, are, was, were, has been, or have been—plus a past participle (typically a verb ending in -ed). Contrary to popular belief, "is" alone isn't passive. Inert, yes; passive, no." (p. 51)

The chapter's stated rule is that active voice is generally preferable, but it gives the exceptions in full — four situations where the passive is the right choice:

> "Sometimes, to be sure, the passive voice is actually desirable. You may want to emphasize the effect of a particular action: "Charles alone was injured in the accident." Or you may want to soften the phrasing of an idea: "You'd be advised to leave now." Or you may be ignorant of the agent performing the action: "The ransom note was left in the mailbox." And sometimes it doesn't matter who performed an action: "Our stadium was rebuilt in 2010."" (p. 52)

The practical test he prescribes: take an old piece of your own writing and convert every unnecessarily passive verb to active, and separately recast *is/are* constructions — especially expletives ("there is," "there are," "there were") and impersonal constructions ("it is," "it was") — into direct statements (p. 52). Immediately following this test, Trimble sets out five numbered before/after pairs contrasting a passive or expletive original with its active revision, introduced by the line "Note, in these examples, the difference in conciseness:" (p. 52).

On the page, each numbered passive sentence is followed directly beneath by its unnumbered active rewrite, indented one further step — the pairs stacked vertically rather than set in parallel columns, with italics marking the verb construction under discussion in each line:

| Passive (numbered line) | Active (indented line below) |
| --- | --- |
| 1. His impetuosity *has been replaced* by prudence. | Prudence now *tempers* his impetuosity. |
| 2. It *is said* that power is *corrupting*. | Power *corrupts*. |
| 3. Meaning *was found* by Freud in everything. | Freud *found* meaning in everything. |
| 4. *There were* 200 guests *in attendance* at the party. | Two hundred guests *attended* the party. |
| 5. It *was decided* to destroy the evidence. | [?] *decided* to destroy the evidence. |

The bracketed question mark in row 5 is Trimble's own typography, not a transcription gap: it occupies the subject slot the passive left empty, showing on the page that the sentence never names who acted. Reading down each pair, the active rewrite is visibly shorter than the passive original, so the gain in conciseness registers before the point is argued in prose.

On why writers fall into the passive despite knowing better, Trimble frames it as a matter of confidence rather than mere habit: a writer secure in an idea "instinctively assert[s] what we know," while an insecure writer "unconsciously turn[s] to the passive voice as a refuge" (p. 53). The recommended countermeasure is an editing pass devoted to nothing but verbs: "We only have to read our piece once through looking exclusively at our verbs" (p. 53), a step he says should be done with every piece written.

*Freshness.* The chapter's third habit is the pursuit of surprise. Trimble states the goal directly: "you have to keep the reader in a state of near-perpetual surprise. Not suspense, but surprise" (p. 53), and analogizes it to a pitcher varying pitches so the batter can't settle into a rhythm. Two techniques are given for generating fresh phrasing. First, a habit of analogical thinking to be applied to every sentence:

> "Always be thinking in terms of "like." Such-and-such is like—what? Challenge your imagination. What is it similar to? Do this with every sentence you write. Make it part of your writing habit." (p. 55)

Second, a vocabulary-immersion exercise borrowed from Charles Ferguson's *Say It with Words*, which Trimble reports having tried himself with good results:

> "Let a person think, and as far as possible speak, for one day a week in the terms common to some particular profession or trade. In his reflections let him pick images from this vocabulary, and let him by this process see how many can be carried over into common speech and writing. On Monday it might be that he would choose his images from cooking; on Tuesday from engineering; on Wednesday from railroading; on Thursday from nuclear science; on Friday from agriculture; on Saturday from sport. And by Sunday he could certainly need a rest, but if he continued the process he might choose his terms from the wealth of language in the field of religion." (p. 55)

A subordinate piece of mechanism, given in a footnote, concerns tool use rather than technique: use a thesaurus "to be reminded of words, not to learn them, for if you learn them there, you'll learn them out of context and risk misusing them," recommending Roget's International Thesaurus (5th ed., HarperCollins, ed. Richard Chapman) and Dictionary.com for free online use (p. 49, n. 1).

**Evidence and examples.**

*Epigraphs.* The chapter opens with two, both attributed to their speakers rather than to Trimble:

> "Less is more, in prose as in architecture." — Donald Hall (p. 48)

> "In composing, as a general rule, run your pen through every other word you have written; you have no idea what vigor it will give your style." — Sydney Smith (p. 48)

*The prefab-phrase catalog* (p. 48), offered as specimens of dead bureaucratic diction: "please be advised," "enclosed please find," "thanking you in advance," and "in reference to yours of . . ."

*The marshmallow sentence.* Trimble's central worked example is a real conferencing session with a student. The original:

> "His bold and brash temper has been replaced by a careful and prudent manner." (p. 49)

Trimble narrates the revision step by step: he asked the student "What words aren't pulling their weight here?"; the student cut "bold and" as redundant with "brash," replaced "brash temper" with the single noun "impetuosity," cut "careful and" as redundant with "prudent," and replaced "prudent manner" with "prudence." The result:

> "His impetuosity has been replaced by prudence." (p. 49)

Trimble notes the original ran 14 words against the revision's 7, and adds that the sentence can be tightened further once verbs are addressed — a forward reference within the chapter to its own later section on vigorous verbs. He attaches a Hemingway line to the larger point about revision effort: "Writing must be a labor of love or it is not writing." (p. 49)

*Active/passive illustration* (p. 50), the minimal pair used to introduce the terms:

> "Active: Bill climbed the mountain.
> Passive: The mountain was climbed by Bill." (p. 50)

*The "good prose / weak prose" handshake passages* (p. 51), two extended first-person impersonations — direct prose speaking to the reader, then indirect prose speaking to the reader — used to dramatize the psychological effect of verb choice:

> "Good prose is direct, definite. Like a firm handshake, it betokens and inspires confidence. It tells the reader: "You're in good hands with me. I have worked out my views on this subject and believe they make sense, so I'm giving it to you just as I see it. I respect you too much to waste your time with hedged prose, and I respect myself too much to come across as wishy-washy. Sure, we'll probably disagree here and there, but at least we'll both know where we disagree."" (p. 51)

> "Weak prose, conversely, is roundabout, vague. Like a limp handshake, it betokens insecurity. It implies to the reader: "You probably should have chosen a different guide. See, I'm truly afraid of you. And afraid of being me, too. I'm not even sure what I think about this subject, so I can't give it to you straight. Being vague is the only refuge I have—my smoke screen, you might say. Vagueness lets me get by with sort-of-understanding, and it also disarms you a bit since you'll have difficulty knowing where you disagree with me."" (p. 51)

*The Declaration of Independence example.* Trimble reports an analysis by Theodore Bernstein, in *The Careful Writer*: of roughly 1,500 words in the Declaration, only about a dozen are passive constructions, the rest carrying the document's persuasive force through active, unequivocal verbs. The specimen line quoted:

> ""[King George III] has plundered our seas, ravaged our Coasts, burnt our towns, and destroyed the lives of our people."" (p. 52)

*Mina Shaughnessy footnote* (p. 53), attached to the point about confidence driving verb choice:

> "Writing is, above all, an act of confidence, an assertion of the importance of what has gone on inside the writer, an exhibition of his thoughts or experiences." — Mina Shaughnessy (p. 53, n. 2)

*Freshness epigraph:*

> "The difference between the almost right word and the right word is really a large matter—'tis the difference between the lightning-bug and the lightning." — Mark Twain (p. 53)

*Specimens of fresh phrasing*, each quoted and attributed to its author, offered as instances of the surprise Trimble is describing rather than as Trimble's own writing:

> "There is no deodorant like success." — Elizabeth Taylor (p. 53)

> "the drama, which develops at about the speed of creeping crab grass . . . ." — John Aldridge (p. 53)

> "He had an upper-class Hoosier accent, which sounds like a handsaw cutting galvanized tin." — Kurt Vonnegut (p. 53)

> "Have I been toiling to weave a labored web of useless ingenuity?" — T. S. Eliot (p. 53)

*Professionals on surprise* — a run of short quotations Trimble assembles from writers discussing their craft, each attributed:

> "We throw in as many fresh words as we can get away with. Simple, short sentences don't always work. You have to do tricks with pacing, alternate long sentences with short, to keep it alive and vital. Virtually every page is a cliff-hanger—you've got to force them to turn it." — Theodor Seuss Geisel (Dr. Seuss) (p. 54)

> "Creativity is continual surprise." — Ray Bradbury (p. 54)

> "Best of all his style is laced with little surprises of diction or structure and the small shocks of well-made metaphors." — an anonymous critic (p. 54)

> "I find the great charm of writing consists in its surprises." — Oliver Wendell Holmes (p. 54)

> "Carefully examined, a good—an interesting—style will be found to consist in a constant succession of tiny, unobservable surprises." — Ford Madox Ford (p. 54)

*A single fresh adjective:*

> "He wrote with a surgical indifference to feelings." — William Nolte (p. 54)

*A fresh verb, with Trimble's own gloss on the mechanics of the surprise:*

> "A prig is one who delights in demonstrating his superiority on small occasions, and it is precisely when he has a good case that he rises to the depths of prigocity." — Dwight Macdonald (p. 55)

Trimble explains this line works through two surprises at once: the substitution of "rises" for the expected "sinks," and the nonce word "prigocity," which he says converts mere priggishness into "a complete and seemingly even fussier state of being" (p. 55).

*An image tacked onto a sentence's end:*

> "A professor must have a theory, as a dog must have fleas." — H. L. Mencken (p. 55)

*The John Mason Brown / Willy Loman metaphor*, from a review of *Death of a Salesman*, given as an example of a sustained metaphor running through a whole passage rather than a single line:

> "Mr. [Lee J.] Cobb's Willy Loman is irresistibly touching and wonderfully unsparing. He is a great shaggy bison of a man seen at that moment of defeat when he is deserted by the herd and can no longer run with it. Mr. Cobb makes clear the pathetic extent to which the herd has been Willy's life." — John Mason Brown (p. 56)

Trimble's gloss: the metaphor gives the reader "a visual—even an emotional—correlative of the bare abstract idea," which is why the idea "comes alive to us, and haunts our imagination with its poignancy" (p. 56).

*Joseph Conrad's statement of artistic purpose*, offered as the payoff of the visual-writing point:

> "My task which I am trying to achieve is, by the power of the written word to make you hear, to make you feel—it is, before all, to make you see. That—and no more, and it is everything." — Joseph Conrad (p. 56)

*The Updike vignette*, from *Assorted Prose*, given in full and flagged by Trimble for a second, close reading ("Read it twice, please"):

> "We recently had a carpenter build a few things in our house in the country. It's an old house, leaning away from the wind a little; its floors sag gently, like an old mattress. The carpenter turned his back on our tilting walls and took his vertical from a plumb line and his horizontal from a bubble level, and then went to work by the light of these absolutes. Fitting his planks into place took a lot of those long, irregular, oblique cuts with a ripsaw that break an amateur's heart. The bookcase and kitchen counter and cabinet he left behind stand perfectly up-and-down in a cockeyed house. Their rectitude is chastening. For minutes at a stretch, we study them, wondering if perhaps it isn't, after all, the wall that is true and the bookcase that leans. Eventually, we suppose, everything will settle into the comfortably crooked, but it will take years, barring earthquakes, and in the meantime we are annoyed at being made to live with impossible standards." — John Updike (p. 56)

Trimble points to "the originality of perception, the "small shocks of well-made metaphors," the fine wit (especially the pun on "rectitude"!)" as what the passage demonstrates (pp. 56–57).

*The Gallico golf-gallery passage*, from a *Vanity Fair* piece titled "A Large Number of Persons," which Trimble calls "the funniest—and truest—I've ever read" as a spoof of sports-crowd behavior:

> "The golf gallery is the Punchinello of the great sports mob, the clown crowd, an uncontrollable, galloping, galumphing horde, that wanders hysterically over manicured pasture acreage of an afternoon, clucking to itself, trying to keep quiet, making funny noises, sweating, thundering over hills ten thousand strong, and gathering, mousey-still, around a little hole in the ground to see a man push a little ball into the bottom of it with a crooked iron stick. If the ball goes in they raise a great shout and clap their hands and sometimes slap one another on the back, crying "Oh, boy!" and "Beautiful, beautiful, magnificent!" And when the white pellet just sneaks past the rim of the orifice or twists out of it, or goes up and looks in and sticks on the edge, a great mass murmur of pity runs through the group and they sound their "Oh's" like a Greek chorus greeting the arrival of a new set of catastrophes. Then it is that they make their absurd clucking noises and shake their heads, some in unison, some in antiunison, like mechanical dolls all set off at once.
>
> The golf gallery is closest of any to the game that is being played. Every individual in the stampede is familiar with the implements used and the problems that arise from tee to green. They are really vicarious players, and the crass outsider who rattles a toy movie camera at one of the artists just as he is about to apply a delicate brush of his poker against the side of the quiescent ball, is given the hissing and glaring-at of his life. The Jones galleries were something to see, up and away over the hills before the master had completed the poem of his follow-through, running, crowding, tearing, galloping, hustling—men, women and children, in sunshine or in cloudburst, their tongues hanging out, their faces red, their sports clothing disheveled, elbowing one another in the wild route over the lea to secure a momentary vantage point from which to bear witness to the next miracle." — Paul Gallico (p. 57)

**Terms introduced.**

- **Active voice / passive voice** — "We call a verb "active"—short for "active voice"—when its grammatical subject is doing whatever the verb describes. We call a verb "passive" when its grammatical subject isn't doing anything; instead, it's being done to" (p. 50). A passive verb is defined precisely as some form of *be* (is, are, was, were, has been, have been) plus a past participle; Trimble notes that "is" alone is not itself passive (p. 51).
- **Expletive construction** — Trimble's label for sentence openers like "there is," "there are," "there were," grouped with impersonal constructions ("it is," "it was") as devices that let a writer "amble past the subject and verb positions of a sentence without having said a thing" (p. 52).
- **Simile / metaphor** — given in a footnote: "A simile compares two things using like or as ("That dorm is like a zoo"); a metaphor makes the comparison implicitly ("That dorm is a zoo")" (p. 55, n. 3).

**Connections.** The chapter is internally cross-referential: the marshmallow-sentence example in the Conciseness section is explicitly picked back up in the Vigorous Verbs section, where Trimble further tightens "His impetuosity has been replaced by prudence" by addressing its passive verb (p. 49, p. 50). The running head identifies this chapter as part of the book's "Fundamentals" section (pp. 50, 52, 54); its relationship to the specific chapters preceding and following it is not stated within these pages.

#### Chapter 7 — Readability

**Claim.** Readability is partly a matter of personal taste, but Trimble stakes out a position: a readable style comes from having something fresh to say, not wasting the reader's time, and writing in an authentic, conversational voice rather than a falsely formal "godlike" one adopted out of fear of judgment. The practical vehicle for that authenticity is a middle style he calls "General English," and the chapter backs the argument with two headline tips and twenty-six further ones.

**Mechanism.** The chapter opens with epigraphs from Blaise Pascal — "When we encounter a natural style, we are astonished and delighted; for we expected to see an author, and we find a man" — and Robert Frost — "Sentences are not different enough to hold the attention unless they are dramatic. No ingenuity of varying structure will do. All that can save them is the speaking tone of voice somehow entangled in the words and fastened to the page for the ear of the imagination" (p. 58).

Trimble states his own working definition: "A readable style is one that invites reading. That circular definition I think we can all agree on. But when we ask what makes a style readable, we move into personal taste" (p. 58). He then names two bedrock, non-negotiable requirements of an author:

1. **Something fresh to say** — content that will either teach or amuse the reader. "If he doesn't, I stop reading."
2. **Not wasting the reader's time** in getting it out. "If he idles, I figure I'm better off reading someone else" (p. 58).

Beyond those two requirements, what he finds most appealing is an *authentic manner*: a writer who comes across as "a vital, companionable human being, not a stuffed shirt or emotionally unfeeling," who talks to the reader, unbends, and writes in "homespun English rather than highbrow English," "stamped with the mark of a quirky personality," carrying "the ring of honesty and passionate conviction" (p. 58–59). He offers Anne Lamott and Dorothy Allison as specimens of this quality (quoted in full under Evidence and examples).

**The Godlike Pose.** Trimble names and diagnoses a common defensive habit: when asked to produce serious writing, fear compels most writers to try to appear "godlike: wise, rational, authoritative." Since no one can actually become more rational or authoritative on demand, writers compensate stylistically instead, "donning the trappings of pure rationality and authority: studied 'objectivity,' impersonality of address, elevated diction, a grave manner, elaborate sentences, and the rest" (p. 60).

**The Dogma of Formalism.** Trimble traces where the pose becomes doctrine. The pattern repeats across "thousands of precedents" until the trappings of authority become "part of the established style of serious discourse," at which point "Decorum — not just the individual's ego — began insisting on a standard of stylistic acceptability," producing "convention reinforcing instinct, and instinct in turn rigidifying convention — in short, a vicious circle" (p. 61). He illustrates the circle with the stock commencement speaker who, unable to answer "My God, what can I say that will be equal to the occasion?", falls back on "an impossibly formal Address — a collection of platitudes substituting for genuine feeling and conviction" that bores its audience, while convention makes it "all the harder for a new commencement speaker to be simply himself" (p. 61).

He then names the procedure for breaking the circle: subject the "dogma of Formalism" to analysis by asking four questions — "How solid, in fact, is its rationale? What are its actual effects? What (if any) reasonable alternative is there to it? And what are the stylistic practices of our best contemporary authors?" (p. 61).

Working through those questions: the *rationale* is sound as far as it goes — teachers who preach the formal style are trying to instill "stylistic discipline and grace" and to teach that talking and writing are not the same thing, reacting against an unthinking informal style that is "loose, banal, and imprecise" (p. 61–62). But the formal style has its own defect: it "tends to lack ease and freshness, since it inhibits variety of diction, simplicity, and anything offbeat" (p. 62). Its *actual effects* are two, both negative: first, it often produces writing as bad as what it means to discourage — "'bow-wow language,' Mencken called it, marked by stilted diction, abstract phraseology, frozen sentence rhythms" (p. 62); second, and more insidious, it promotes "phoniness and empty conventionality — the Standard Way of Thinking," since a person taught to write like someone who was himself taught to write like someone else drifts from what he actually thinks, producing "businessese," "academese," "officialese," "committee prose" — labels that differ "but not their gobbledygook essence" (p. 62). Trimble flags that this recurs in the next chapter ("More on this in our next chapter," p. 62) and cites George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" as making the same diagnosis (quoted in full under Evidence and examples, p. 62).

Trimble locates the error in a false either/or: a widely used Freshman English textbook's rule on contractions (quoted in full below, p. 63) "postulates only two kinds of style, both of them extreme — an informal, colloquial style versus a formal style — and implies that only the latter is legitimate for serious writing" (p. 63).

The textbook rule itself, quoted in full:

> The use of contractions (I'll, can't, couldn't, didn't, he's, shouldn't) is appropriate in informal and colloquial styles but not in a formal style. (p. 63)

**The "General English" style.** Trimble's alternative to the formal/informal either-or is a middle style, which he attributes to language expert Porter Perrin: "General English" — "essentially a happy compromise between formal and informal." Being a compromise, it is, in his account, "by far the most palatable of the written styles," with an area of appropriateness that is "virtually unlimited" in the real world, because skilled writers can stay within it and still satisfy what he calls "the four essentials of prose: precision, conciseness, ease, and freshness" (p. 63) — essentials he says are difficult to satisfy in any other style. He reports that "General English" has been displacing formal English "as the prevailing literary style in recent years" (p. 63).

Trimble captures the special character of the style with a line from Somerset Maugham: "good prose should resemble the conversation of a well-bred man" (p. 63) — Trimble adds, parenthetically, "(Or woman, he surely meant to say.)" He points to prior specimens in the book as illustrations: the passages by White in Chapter 1, by Kael in Chapter 2, by Updike in Chapter 6, and by Lamott and Allison in this chapter, noting that each is "conversational in tone — unaffected, idiomatic, straightforward — but also beautifully wrought," with labor "lavished on these sentences" concealed so they "seem happy accidents" (p. 63). He credits the style's appeal to its "authenticity and graceful informality" for readers, and to writers' own sense that it "frees them to discover their own voice" and "reinforces their desire to speak the truth as they see it" (p. 63–64). He quotes Bonamy Dobree, from *Modern Prose Style*: "The modern prose writer, in returning to the rhythms of everyday speech, is trying to be more honest with himself than if he used, as is too wreckingly easy, the forms and terms already published as the expression of other people's minds" (p. 64).

Trimble warns that naming "General English" as the answer does not make it easy: "Writing an informal style is easy — you just talk on paper. Writing a formal style is pretty easy, too, once you have the knack... But writing a good 'General English' style is hard," because it requires "a sophisticated control of tone" (p. 64). Being a compromise, the style mingles contraries — "formal and informal diction, objectivity and subjectivity, impersonality and directness" — and the writer's task is twofold: get the right mix ("as tricky as concocting a good sweet-and-sour sauce"), and work around the edges of the extremes without a misstep — knowing when a colloquialism reads as easy informality versus cheapening a sentence, when an unusual word reads as piquant versus pretentious, when a personal touch reads as welcome versus narcissistic. He states that "guessing right requires a good ear, taste, and tact — all of them intuitive, finally, and acquired only through considerable reading and writing" (p. 64).

**Closing Thoughts.** Trimble frames the choice of style as a moral one and demonstrates the technique in the same breath, presenting the passage as what he tells his students when the issue comes up near the end of class (p. 65). The voice here is doing the instructional work itself — direct address, rhetorical questions, contractions — so it is worth quoting at length rather than paraphrasing:

> "Each time we write, we're making a choice as to the kind of person we prefer to be. Since it's so important, let's make that choice a conscious one for a change. Here's what it involves: 'Do I want to be authentically me, speaking my own thoughts in my own idiom, or am I content to be a pseudo-self, using borrowed thoughts, borrowed language, and a borrowed personality to gain the approval of a few literary traditionalists?'" (p. 65)

He continues, addressing when the formal style is actually warranted:

> "Sometimes, of course, stuffy or not, the formal style will seem to be the only one appropriate to the occasion, either because tradition decrees it or because the subject calls for an impersonal treatment. If you're writing a legal brief, for example, or a statement of corporate policy or a scientific paper, your job is to transmit information, not personal reflections; and you'll show readers that you understand that job by adopting a serious, reasonably judicial manner that keeps you offstage." (p. 65)

He then turns to the counter-case, warning against reflexive formality: "But, for heaven's sake, let's not allow ourselves to be slaves to blind convention... they may simply be afraid to be themselves, and may be just waiting for some free spirit to come along and give them the courage of their instincts" (p. 65).

As his specimen of a writer who broke convention successfully, Trimble points to Franklin Roosevelt, who, facing convention that "almost demanded" a "lofty, presidential style" for his radio addresses, "blithely ignored convention, choosing instead to give what he called 'fireside chats' — personal, down-to-earth talks laced with colloquialisms and jokes" (p. 65–66), reasoning that ordinary citizens would prefer "relaxed plain talk to studied oratory" — a bet Trimble says was "proved right" (p. 66).

He closes the section by directing students to test his claim against the evidence rather than take it on authority: "Look at the evidence — magazines such as *The New Yorker*, *Harper's*, *Forbes*, *The Atlantic Monthly*, and *Time*; newspapers like *The New York Times*, *The Wall Street Journal*, and *The Washington Post*; the latest books of Pulitzer Prize-winning nonfiction," concluding that they show "a revolution in the notion of what constitutes a good style for serious writing — a movement toward greater naturalness, vigor, informality, and individuality." He adds: "When even *The New York Times* permits contractions in its editorial columns, as it does now, you know that literary Victorianism is on its way out" (p. 66). A footnote corroborates this with usage expert Bergen Evans, coauthor of *A Dictionary of American Usage*, remarking in 1962 that "As written English is used by increasing millions and for more reasons than ever before, the language has become more utilitarian and more informal... you can't hold the language of one generation up as a model for the next" (p. 66).

**Tips for Improving Your Readability.** Trimble names two headline tips as "the two best ways I know for promoting an authentic and readable style" (p. 66):

1. View your reader as a companionable friend — someone with a warm sense of humor and a love of simple directness.
2. Write like you're actually talking to that friend, but talking with enough leisure to frame your thoughts concisely and interestingly.

He tells the reader to post these two "on the wall by your writing desk" and practice them (p. 66).

He then supplies 26 further tips, noting they "occasionally... reiterate or anticipate points I make elsewhere" but are included here "for convenience of reference" (p. 67). The full, numbered set follows.

1. As a rule of thumb, after three longish sentences in a row, make the fourth a short one, and don't fear the super-short sentence — "sometimes just a single word will be plenty long" (example under Evidence and examples, p. 67).
2. Use occasional contractions. They help a writer "unbend," let readers relax, and free up the writing voice. The most popular involve *am*, *are*, *is*, and *not*: I'm; you're, we're, they're; he's, she's, here's; won't, wouldn't, shouldn't, don't, doesn't, can't. But contractions are "like kisses: bestowed too freely, they lose their effect, in fact seem cheap" — save them for humanizing a stiff sentence (p. 67).
3. Generally, prefer *that* to *which*: "The one is conversational; the other, slightly more bookish." Save *which* for after a comma, introducing a nonrestrictive clause. Trimble's own practice: "If I could cut the clause, I'll use 'which' and a comma before it; otherwise, I'll normally use 'that.'" When in doubt, read the sentence aloud and test it on the ear (p. 67–68). He cites Rudolf Flesch: "You have to go by feel, not by rule" (p. 68).

4. If you mean "I," say "I." Don't wrap yourself in pomposities like "the writer," "one," "this author," or "we." Reserve "we"/"our" for when both reader and writer are meant; reserve "one" for "a person." Address the reader as "you," not "the reader" — "the printed page already puts enough distance between the two of you" (p. 68).
5. Use dashes to isolate concluding phrases for emphasis or humorous effect. Trimble points back to Pauline Kael's review in Chapter 2 as a model (p. 68).
6. Professionals quote, amateurs paraphrase. "Readers love listening to people talk — love hearing the actual words, not a preemptive digest of them." Use dialogue wherever it fits, and don't be shy about inventing imagined thoughts — "one of a writer's best resources" (p. 68). Four specimens follow this tip in the text: an imagined interior monologue for Shakespeare's Enobarbus, a student passage by Matt Darroh, a passage by architectural critic Witold Rybczynski, and a passage by Stewart Brand quoting building historian Orlando Ridout (all quoted in full under Evidence and examples, p. 68–69).

7. The more abstract your argument, the more you should lace it with "word pictures" — illustrations, analogies, vivid quotations, metaphors, similes. These aid readers' understanding and memory; "they'll probably remember your illustration or analogy far longer than the abstract idea itself" (p. 69). Illustrated with a passage from Ron Suskind's *A Hope in the Unseen* (quoted in full under Evidence and examples, p. 70).
8. Minimize your adjectives; let nouns, especially accurate ones, work alone. Trimble quotes Voltaire — "The adjective is the enemy of the noun" — and Twain — "As to the Adjective: when in doubt, strike it out" — and reproduces Twain's 1880 letter of advice to a schoolboy, D. W. Bowser (quoted in full under Evidence and examples, p. 70).
9. Minimize your adverbs too, especially trite intensifiers like *very*, *extremely*, *really*, *clearly*, and *terribly*, which Trimble says show "a 90% failure rate." Cutting the intensifier "usually gains" intensity — "very hungry" against "ravenous." He concedes an exception: "the right adverb, fresh and adroitly placed, is one of life's finest small pleasures" (p. 70). Illustrated with two passages from Stewart Brand's *How Buildings Learn* (quoted in full under Evidence and examples, p. 71).

10. Use the fewest words possible and the simplest words possible. A longer word can occasionally work best — for concision, cadence, or surprise — but the more a writer "surrenders to the temptation to write fancy," the further the prose strays from true feeling toward mere impressiveness. He cites Thoreau's "Simplify, simplify," Maugham's "To write simply is as difficult as to be good," and Hemingway's "Writing plain English is hard work" (p. 71).
11. Be sure each sentence is somehow connected to the ones immediately before and after it — the only way, Trimble says, to achieve "a 'clean narrative line' — the hallmark, in my opinion, of professional prose" (p. 71).
12. In a long essay or report, periodically summarize the argument so readers can keep their bearings, ideally as brief transitional paragraphs of three or four sentences (p. 71).
13. If you put questions to your reader, answer them promptly — ideally starting the very next paragraph, and explicitly. If something else must be explained first, tell the reader that, so the question isn't forgotten: "the reader isn't clairvoyant; she knows only what the writer remembers to tell her" (p. 71–72).

14. Use semicolons to reduce choppiness, particularly across sentences with parallel structures, and for a change of pace (cross-referenced to the semicolon section of Chapter 13, p. 72).
15. Read your prose aloud, always. Trimble supplies a checklist of ear-tests: Do you sound comfortable with your own ideas? At ease with your reader? Can you read each sentence without stumbling or running out of breath? Does it sound like you or "like it's coming out of some statue"? Have you avoided unconscious word repeats, especially at the start and end of consecutive sentences and at paragraph openings — spots skilled writers "always double-check" (p. 72)?
16. Instead of always spelling out "first" and "second," occasionally use the numerals themselves in parentheses. It is "a superstition that numerals have no place in serious writing," provable by browsing any major anthology of expository prose (p. 72).

17. Numbers are tricky, and "everybody has a theory," which Trimble offers as "a useful reminder of just how variable 'rules' can be." He surveys competing authorities: some experts write out any number needing a hyphen or space ("twenty-two," "two hundred") as words; others write out anything two syllables or fewer; *The Chicago Manual of Style* spells out "whole numbers from one through ninety-nine"; still others say "Use numerals for everything over twenty." His own practice follows the *Associated Press Stylebook*: "Spell out whole numbers below 10, use figures for 10 and above," which he restates for memorability as "If it's a one-digit number, make it a word" (p. 72–73). He admits to occasional exceptions to his own rule and closes by quoting Anne Lamott again: "writing needs to breathe and move" (p. 73). A footnote notes the AP rule also matches the U.S. Government Printing Office *Manual of Style* and MLA style (p. 73).

18. When a sentence begins with *And* or *But* — which Trimble says a writer "most definitely should" do now and then — don't follow it with a comma; the comma "would just kill any gain" in quickened pace. A comma is needed only if a parenthetical phrase follows, in which case a second comma closes that phrase: "But, considering the evidence, she's probably right" (p. 73). Illustrated with a passage from Witold Rybczynski's *Home* (quoted in full under Evidence and examples, p. 73).
19. *So* and *Yet* also make good sentence lead-offs, though amateur writers resist the practice "even after seeing the practice credentialed in reputable publications." Illustrated with passages from Paul Graham and from *New York Times* food columnist Mark Bittman (quoted in full under Evidence and examples, p. 73–74). Trimble notes that, like *And* and *But*, *So* and *Yet* are normally spared commas because they are short and brisk, while their polysyllabic counterparts — *Consequently*, *However* — normally take one (p. 74).

20. As a sentence starter, prefer *But* to *However* — "two fewer syllables" and no comma, making it "a cleaner, punchier transition word — especially at the head of a paragraph, where it's peerless." *However* works best positioned internally, next to the point of emphasis; at the sentence head it forces the reader to "go figure out where the stress ought to be" (p. 74).
21. If you have a good wit, share it — let your sense of verbal play show. "Serious writing" need not be solemn writing. Trimble quotes F. L. Lucas's *Style*: "No manual of style that I know has a word to say of good humour; and yet, for me, a lack of it can sometimes blemish all the literary beauties and blandishments ever taught" (p. 74).

22. Paragraphing matters as much as any other element of style. Long paragraphs intimidate readers; lots of short ones can suggest "a breezy, Madison Avenue glibness" or an unwillingness to pursue a point; a succession of same-length paragraphs can suggest a tired imagination. The moral is to vary paragraph length "to keep things alive and vital," but to make sure each paragraph's lead-off sentence — its "bridge sentence" — also functions as a topic sentence that forecasts the paragraph's new thought (p. 74–75). Trimble points readers back to the 11 bridge sentences he quotes from Bergen Evans at the end of Chapter 4 ("Middles") as a worked demonstration (p. 75).
23. On the creative use of white space (a topic he says he returns to in Chapter 10): when shifting between two large sections of an exposition, skip four spaces instead of the usual two between paragraphs. This cues readers visually that a major new section is starting, gives them a pause point, and spares the writer from manufacturing a transition — "white space substitutes for *meanwhile*, a most convenient pseudo-bridge that pros rely on when all else fails" (p. 75).

24. Choose your title with care. Leave the "teasing" title to writers who put cuteness before communication; focus on making the title "accurately descriptive," with zing if possible — it is the reader's introduction to the writer as well as to the paper (p. 75).
25. If a paragraph sounds labored, back off and ask "How would I say this to a friend?" — say it aloud, then write down as closely as possible what was said. Talked-out sentences will usually outperform the earlier written version, because writing tends to overcomplicate while talking forces simplicity (p. 75).
26. As a related remedy, take a short break and read a writer whose style you relish, try to feel yourself writing those paragraphs as you read them, then ask "how would [Wonderful Writer] write this?" and try again. Even when it doesn't fully work, Trimble says, "it will at least give you a fresh perspective. That's maybe half the battle right there" (p. 75).

**Evidence and examples.** As his primary specimens of an authentic, "General English" manner, Trimble reproduces two passages from Anne Lamott's *Bird by Bird: Some Instructions on Writing and Life* (p. 59):

> What I've learned to do when I sit down to work on a shitty first draft is to quiet the voices in my head. First there's the vinegar-lipped Reader Lady, who says primly, "Well, that's not very interesting, is it?" And there's the emaciated German male who writes these Orwellian memos detailing your thought crimes... Quieting these voices is at least half the battle I fight daily.... (Lamott, quoted p. 59)

> Perfectionism is the voice of the oppressor, the enemy of the people. It will keep you cramped and insane your whole life, and it is the main obstacle between you and a shitty first draft. Perfectionism will ruin your writing, blocking inventiveness and playfulness and life force (these are words we are allowed to use in California). Perfectionism means that you try desperately not to leave so much mess to clean up. But clutter and mess show us that life is being lived. Clutter is wonderfully fertile ground — you can still discover new treasures under all those piles, clean things up, edit things out, fix things, get a grip. Tidiness suggests that something is as good as it's going to get. Tidiness makes me think of held breath, of suspended animation, while writing needs to breathe and move. (Lamott, quoted p. 59)

Trimble frames these as Lamott "battling her perfectionism right in front of us as she reminds us — and no less herself! — of its high costs" (p. 59).

Trimble's second specimen is novelist Dorothy Allison, whose paragraphs he says ring with "conviction, passion, and integrity" (p. 59):

> I have always passionately loved good books — good stories and beautiful writing, and most of all, books that seemed to me to be intrinsically important, books that told the truth, painful truths sometimes, in a voice that made eloquent the need for human justice. That is what I have meant when I have used the word literature. It has seemed to me that literature, as I meant it, was embattled, that it was increasingly difficult to find writing doing what I thought literature should do — which was simply to push people into changing their ideas about the world, and to go further, to encourage us in the work of changing the world, to making it more just and more truly human. (Allison, quoted p. 59–60)

> All my life I have hated cliches, the cliches applied to people like me and those I love. Every time I pick up a book that purports to be about either poor people or queers or Southern women, I do so with a conscious anxiety, an awareness that the books about us have often been cruel, small, and false. I have wanted our lives taken seriously and represented fully — with power and honesty and sympathy — to be hated or loved, or to terrify and obsess, but to be real, to have the power of the whole and the complex. I have never wanted politically correct parables made out of my grief, simple-minded rote speeches made from my rage, simplifications that reduce me to cardboard dimensions. But mostly that is what I have found. We are the ones they make fiction of — we queer and disenfranchised and female — and we have the right to demand our full, nasty, complicated lives, if only to justify all the times our reality has been stolen, mismade, and dishonored. (Allison, quoted p. 60)

> What permits such miracles of literary authenticity to happen? The answer, I think, is either a religious reverence for truth at whatever cost — this is proverbially the case with major artists — or else genuine self-acceptance. If a person accepts herself, she will be herself, and will speak her mind in her own idiom without inhibition. She won't be engaged in posturing with her reader, or counterfeiting her real personality and feelings, because she'll have no wobbly idealized self to defend. (Allison, quoted p. 60)

Trimble uses this last passage to name the underlying cause of inauthentic prose: fear of rejection "straitjackets most of us from early in life," so that "instead of learning to discover our own writing voice, we learn to mimic the voices of others" (p. 60) — the pivot into his discussion of the Godlike Pose.

As evidence that the formal-style dogma produces bad writing, Trimble quotes George Orwell's essay "Politics and the English Language" (p. 62):

> As I have tried to show, modern writing at its worst does not consist in picking out words for the sake of their meaning and inventing images in order to make the meaning clearer. It consists in gumming together long strips of words which have already been set in order by someone else, and making the results presentable by sheer humbug.... In our time it is broadly true that political writing is bad writing. Where it is not true, it will generally be found that the writer is some sort of rebel, expressing his private opinions and not a "party line." Orthodoxy, of whatever color, seems to demand a lifeless, imitative style. (Orwell, quoted p. 62)

A footnote to the "General English" discussion carries an epigram from George Bernard Shaw: "In literature the ambition of a novice is to acquire the literary language; the struggle of the adept is to get rid of it" (Shaw, quoted p. 64).

For tip 1 (the value of the super-short sentence), Trimble quotes a student, Gregg Hopkins:

> Many American parents think that today's colleges are veritable breeding grounds for premarital sex. Nonsense. Each year, literally tens of students graduate with their virtue still intact. (Hopkins, quoted p. 67)

Trimble calls the final sentence "the literary knuckleball I spoke of in Chapter 6" and an illustration of an aesthetic axiom, "The smaller the sign [i.e., cue,] the greater the pleasure": the reader's momentary misreading of "tens of students" as "tens of thousands" produces the joke's double take (p. 67).

For tip 6 (quoting over paraphrasing, and inventing imagined thoughts), Trimble's first specimen is an imagined interior monologue for Shakespeare's Enobarbus, at the point where "events inexorably force" him to an impossible decision:

> "My mind tells me to leave Antony for Rome. My heart tells me to leave Rome for Antony. Both courses of action are right, and both are wrong. To go either way is to deny a central fact of my existence. I am a Roman, but I am also a man. There seems to be only one solution: death. It will eliminate the need to choose." (quoted p. 68–69)

His second specimen is a student paper by Matt Darroh on Updike's short story "A&P":

> Mr. Lengel offers Sammy some well-intended advice: "You'll feel this for the rest of your life...." In other words: "You'd better get used to keeping your mouth shut when you don't agree with certain things because that's life, and that's what you have to do. If you want to be successful, you need to quit being so idealistic. That's what I did, and that's what you have to do." (Darroh, quoted p. 69)

His third specimen, "drolly contrasting" the modern chair with its predecessors, is architectural critic Witold Rybczynski, from *Home: A Short History of an Idea*:

> It exhibits lightness and movability, and it invites admiration for these qualities — just as a well-made camp cot does. But it does not ask to be sat in, or at least not for very long. The Rococo chair invites conversation, and the Victorian chair invites after-dinner naps, but the Modern chair is all business. "Let's get this sitting over with and get back to something useful," it commands. It is about many things, this chair, but it is no longer about ease, leisure, or, if truth be told, about comfort. (Rybczynski, quoted p. 69)

His fourth specimen is Stewart Brand, from *How Buildings Learn: What Happens After They're Built*, quoting building historian Orlando Ridout on what house facades communicate:

> "The front facade [says Ridout] is a self-conscious part of the house, where the owner is trying to make a statement to the world about what he is about — whether it's 'I'm a simple man with simple tastes' or 'I'm richer than you are and don't you forget it' or simply, 'I have crossed the threshold of gentility. I can now afford a brick house with a fancy entrance porch.'" (Brand/Ridout, quoted p. 69)

For tip 7 ("word pictures"), Trimble reproduces a passage from Pulitzer Prize-winner Ron Suskind's *A Hope in the Unseen*, on the pressures facing an ambitious student at an inner-city Washington, D.C. high school:

> Cedric's 4.02 grade-point average virtually ties him for first in the junior class with a quiet, studious girl named LaCountiss Spinner. Pride in such accomplishment is acceptable behavior for sterling students at high schools across the land, but at Ballou and other urban schools like it, something else is at work. Educators have even coined a phrase for it. They call it the crab/bucket syndrome: when one crab tries to climb from the bucket, the others pull it back down. The forces dragging students toward failure — especially those who have crawled farthest up the side — flow through every corner of the school. Inside the bucket, there is little chance of escape. (Suskind, quoted p. 70)

For tip 8 (minimizing adjectives), Trimble reproduces Mark Twain's 1880 letter of advice to a schoolboy correspondent, D. W. Bowser:

> I notice that you use plain, simple language, short words and brief sentences. That is the way to write English — it is the modern way and the best way. Stick to it; don't let fluff and flowers and verbosity creep in. When you catch an adjective, kill it. No, I don't mean utterly, but kill most of them — then the rest will be valuable. They weaken when they are close together. They give strength when they are wide apart. An adjective habit, or a wordy, diffuse, flowery habit, once fastened upon a person, is as hard to get rid of as any other vice. (Twain, quoted p. 70)

For tip 9 (minimizing adverbs), Trimble reproduces two "proofs," both from Stewart Brand's *How Buildings Learn*, noting that in each Brand "saves his adverb for the next-to-last word, where it won't get overlooked" (p. 71):

> James Donnelly of Whole Earth inked every page of the manuscript bright red with line-editing corrections, for which I am whimperingly grateful. (Brand, quoted p. 71)

> Because of water, houses deteriorate most from the bottom up and the top down. Damage comes from below thanks to what the British call, knowledgeably, "damp." (Brand, quoted p. 71)

For tip 18 (sentence-initial *And*/*But* without a following comma), Trimble reproduces a passage from Rybczynski's *Home*:

> The modern kitchen, in which everything is hidden in artfully designed cabinets, looks well organized, like a bank office. But a kitchen does not function like an office; if anything, it is more like a workshop. (Rybczynski, quoted p. 73)

For tip 19 (sentence-initial *So*/*Yet*), Trimble quotes software developer and essayist Paul Graham:

> Part of what software has to do is explain itself. So to write good software you have to understand how little users understand. (Graham, quoted p. 74)

And *New York Times* food columnist Mark Bittman, writing on the Italian coastal province of Liguria:

> The coastline, riddled with natural harbors, made the Ligurians great traders, too. Genoa, the region's capital, was a powerful city-state that vied with Venice to rule the Mediterranean in the 15th and 16th centuries. So food products from all over northern Italy and the world were exported or imported through Liguria. (Bittman, quoted p. 74)

**Terms introduced.**

- **"General English"** — a middle prose style, term borrowed from language expert Porter Perrin, "essentially a happy compromise between formal and informal," capable of satisfying precision, conciseness, ease, and freshness at once (p. 63).
- **The Godlike Pose** — Trimble's name for a writer's defensive attempt, under the pressure of "serious" writing, to appear "wise, rational, authoritative" by adopting the trappings of authority rather than becoming more rational or authoritative (p. 60).
- **The Dogma of Formalism** — Trimble's name for the inherited, unexamined belief that only a lofty, formal style is legitimate for serious writing (p. 61).
- **"Bow-wow language"** — H. L. Mencken's term, quoted by Trimble, for writing marked by "stilted diction, abstract phraseology, frozen sentence rhythms" that results from straining after formality (p. 62).
- **The Standard Way of Thinking** — Trimble's term for the "phoniness and empty conventionality" that results from a writer imitating a style itself learned by imitation, surfacing as "businessese," "academese," "officialese," "committee prose" (p. 62).
- **"Clean narrative line"** — Trimble's term for prose in which each sentence connects to the one before and after it, which he calls "the hallmark... of professional prose" (p. 71).
- **"Word pictures"** — Trimble's term for illustrations, analogies, vivid quotations, metaphors, and similes used to concretize abstract argument (p. 69).
- **Bridge sentence** — Trimble's term for a paragraph's lead-off sentence when it does double duty as a topic sentence, forecasting the paragraph's new thought (p. 74–75).

White) and Chapter 2 (Pauline Kael) as prior illustrations of the "General English" manner, and from Chapter 6 (Updike) for the same purpose, while also reusing Chapter 6's term "literary knuckleball." Points forward explicitly to Chapter 8, which the text signals will continue the diagnosis of formal-style dogma ("More on this in our next chapter," p. 62) — Chapter 8 ("Superstitions") in fact takes up several of the same prohibitions this chapter argues against, including the ban on contractions, on sentence-initial *And*/*But*, and on addressing the reader as "you." Cross-references Chapter 4 ("Middles") for its worked example of bridge sentences (the Bergen Evans passage, p. 75) and Chapter 13 for its treatment of the semicolon (p. 72). Anticipates Chapter 10 for further treatment of numerals in formatted text and of white space as a structural device (p. 73, 75).

#### Chapter 8 — Superstitions

**Claim.** Trimble argues that seven widely taught prohibitions on English usage — never begin a sentence with *But* or *And*, never use contractions, never address the reader as "you," never use the first-person "I," never end a sentence with a preposition, never split an infinitive, never write a one-sentence paragraph — are not genuine rules of the language but superstitions, upheld by a class of "literary prudes" he satirizes as members of "TOTELS" (The One True English Language Sect). He takes each of these "Seven Nevers" in turn, traces its rationale, and shows it violated routinely by writers he considers exemplary, then states his own, more permissive practice for each.

**Mechanism.** The chapter's structure is itself a diagnostic device. Trimble first constructs a satirical institution — TOTELS, its three Articles of Faith, its Creed, and its "Seven Core Rules" — as a way of naming and unifying rules that are usually encountered piecemeal. He explains the coinage:

> "Unaccountably, their faith has gone nameless. I will repair that oversight right now and christen it "The One True English Language Sect''—or "TOTELS," for short." (p. 77)

The three Articles of Faith, which he says he is "taking the liberty to translate into plain English":

> "Article I: The English Language is a system of Laws—not, as the new Linguistic Heresy claims, just a system of human conventions for communication.
> Article II: Being a system of Laws, the Language was meant to be static, not dynamic. Hence any changes in it are to be regarded as Corruptions.
> Article III: Because of Articles I and II, Correct Usage depends upon our honoring the Rules which our Elders have sagely inferred from the Laws of the Language." (p. 77)

The TOTELarian Creed, which he says "the devoutest members somehow know without ever having to read it":

> "We believe in Rules, Authority, and The One True English Language. We believe in the sanctity of Formal English, which shall ever be revered for its elaborate syntax, baroque sentences, ornate words, and stiff expressions, all of which we pledge ourselves laboriously to employ. Above all, we believe that naturalness is unnatural, that informality is unacceptable, and that individuality is unpardonable. Amen." (p. 78)

From these Articles and this Creed, Trimble derives the "Seven Nevers" — the rules the rest of the chapter takes apart one at a time:

> "1. Never begin a sentence with *But* or *And*.
> 2. Never use contractions.
> 3. Never refer to the reader as *you*.
> 4. Never use the first-person pronoun *I*.
> 5. Never end a sentence with a preposition.
> 6. Never split an infinitive.
> 7. Never write a paragraph containing only a single sentence." (p. 78)

For each Never, his method is consistent: state the rule and its usual justification, cite counter-evidence from writers, critics, or public figures whose usage violates it, and then give his own qualified guidance — rarely a blanket permission, more often an "it depends," governed by ear, context, and moderation rather than by law.

Before reaching the Nevers, Trimble names the people who enforce them and their likely motives for doing so:

> "These people arrived at their literary prudishness by various means—some through a puristic concern for the language that gradually stiffened into morbid scrupulosity; some through ignorance reinforced by others' ignorance; some through a hunger for the security of dogma and absolutes; and some, it would seem, merely through the snob appeal of elitism." (p. 77)

He attributes an observation about literary prudes to the linguist Donald Lloyd: they "are the people who put triumphant exclamation marks in the margins of library books" (p. 76). He also glosses one flashpoint in the sect's history in a footnote — "the war against Webster III," meaning the controversy over *Webster's Third New International Dictionary*, which in 1961 drew criticism for abandoning usage labels like "incorrect" and "colloquial" in favor of describing how language is actually used; he cites the *Harbrace Guide to Dictionaries*, ed. Kenneth Wilson et al. (New York: Harcourt, Brace, 1963), for a full account (p. 77, n. 1).

**Evidence and examples.**

*1. "Never begin a sentence with But or And" (pp. 78–80).* Trimble offers three reasons teachers still preach the rule:

> "(1) they were taught it themselves at an impressionable age and have never thought to question its legitimacy; (2) they hope to discourage anything smacking of informality in student writing, perhaps because what informality they've seen has read like cafeteria chatter; (3) they can use this rule as a way to force students to move beyond simple sentences ("The plan is long overdue") to compound ones ("The plan is long overdue, but implementing it will be difficult")." (p. 78)

Against the rule, he points to the front page of *The New York Times*, which he says shows "maybe half a dozen of each, just on that one page alone" (p. 79), and to Bergen Evans's *Atlantic Monthly* defense of *Webster III* (the same article cited in the preceding chapter), in which "Professor Evans begins 29 sentences with *But* and, coincidentally, another 29 with *And*" (p. 79). He reproduces two paragraphs of that article in full:

> "The ultimate of "permissiveness," singled out by almost every critic for special scorn, was the inclusion in the Third International of *finalize*. It was this, more than any other one thing, that was given as the reason for sticking to the good old Second International—that "peerless authority on American English," as the *Times* called it. But if it was such an authority, why didn't they look into it? They would have found *finalize* if they had.
> And why shouldn't it be there? It exists. It's been recorded for two generations. Millions employ it every day. Two Presidents of the United States—men of widely differing cultural backgrounds—have used it in formal statements. And so has the Secretary-General of the United Nations [U Thant], a man of most unusual linguistic attainments. It isn't permitting the word but omitting it that would break faith with the reader." (p. 79)

On the same page, Trimble contrasts *but* and *and* with their more formal substitutes — "however, on the other hand, nonetheless, and nevertheless" for *but*, and "furthermore, in addition, indeed, and moreover" for *and* — calling the alternatives "heavy-footed" and "polysyllabic."

His caution: use the construction "with some restraint," noting that Evans's 29 *and*s and 29 *but*s avoid monotony only "by being sprinkled through ten close-knit pages" (p. 80).

*2. "Never use contractions" (pp. 80–81).* Trimble notes that the two paragraphs just quoted from Bergen Evans "contained four such instances" of contractions, and refers back to earlier-quoted passages from John Updike, E. B. White, Pauline Kael, Anne Lamott, and James Michener as further counter-evidence (p. 80). He frames the deeper issue as one of a writer's fear of readerly rejection, comparing it to Prufrock's and Eleanor Rigby's isolation, and quotes Robert Frost:

> "The style is the man. Rather say the style is the way the man takes himself; and to be at all charming or even bearable, the way is almost rigidly prescribed. If it is with outer seriousness, it must be with inner humor. If it is with outer humor, it must be with inner seriousness. Neither one alone without the other under it will do." (p. 80)

Trimble's own gloss: "a writer who never uses contractions is akin to someone whose outer seriousness has no inner humor under it," while "a writer who constantly uses them is like someone whose outer humor has no inner seriousness under it" (pp. 80–81). His stated rule of practice:

> "Contractions are best used in moderation. When your ear tells you that the rhythm of a sentence seems to require a contraction, go ahead and use it without apology. It's perfectly acceptable in a "General English" style. ... But where a contraction is not required, it's best not to use one, for to do so is to risk overweighting your style with informality." (p. 81)

*3. "Never refer to the reader as 'you'" (pp. 81–82).* Trimble rejects both alternatives — never referring to the reader at all, or calling the reader "the reader" — as bad psychology. He quotes critic Wayne Booth's term for the first alternative, the "pedant's stance":

> "consists of ignoring or underplaying the personal relationship of speaker and audience and depending entirely on statements about a subject—that is, the notion of a job to be done for a particular audience is left out. ... The writer who assumes that it is enough merely to write an exposition of what he happens to know on the subject will produce the kind of essay that soils our scholarly journals, written not for readers but for bibliographies." (p. 81)

He cites Theodore Bernstein, whom he identifies as *The New York Times*'s English-usage expert and founding editor of the paper's international edition in Paris, from Bernstein's book on outmoded usage rules, *Miss Thistlebottom's Hobgoblins*:

> "Informality in all kinds of writing has been in the ascendancy in recent times and now the indefinite *you* is accepted and widely used." (p. 81)

Trimble adds his own caution against overuse and states the rule as a moral: "If you don't need to say *you*, don't. If you do need to, say it without embarrassment exactly as you would in conversation" (pp. 81–82).

*4. "Never use the first-person pronoun 'I'" (pp. 82–83).* Trimble describes the rule's alternatives — complete self-effacement, or self-transcendence via "the present writer" or "we" — as assuming that the best expository prose is the most impersonal, an assumption he ties back to the Creed's "individuality is unpardonable": "In theory, then, the best prose would come from a machine, which is precisely what many TOTELarians try to imitate" (p. 82). He endorses, "all that being said," the advice of Strunk and White in *The Elements of Style*: "Place yourself in the background" (p. 82), while warning that young writers misapply this by turning a critical analysis into autobiography. He parodies the resulting openers:

> "When I first read this work, I thought such-and-such. And I had a terrible time with it. But as I turned things over in my mind, it eventually dawned on me that. ... And then I saw that. ..." (p. 82)

He quotes screenwriter-director Nora Ephron's Preface to *Wallflower at the Orgy*:

> "Because I began as a newspaper reporter, it took me a long time to become comfortable using the first-person singular pronoun in my work. In the articles in this book I use it gingerly, often after considerable prodding from my editors. I was uncomfortable with it. The work I have done subsequently is considerably more personal and considerably more full of the first-person singular pronoun, but I still believe that the best approach to its use ought to be discomfort. Do you really need it? Does it add something special to the piece? Is what you think interesting enough to make the reader care? Are you saying something that no one has said? Above all, do you understand that you are not as important as what you're covering?" (pp. 82–83)

Trimble's closing rule:

> "Reserve "I" for when you truly need it — either to emphasize that such-and-such is admittedly just a conjecture or personal prejudice, or to add some humanity to an otherwise dry account. The rest of the time, try to generalize objectively and more or less impersonally, as if you're pointing out what any intelligent person could see for himself. If your assertions are indeed intelligent and well supported, they won't need props like "it seems to me," "I think," "I feel," and "in my opinion."" (p. 83)

*5. "Never end a sentence with a preposition" (pp. 83–84).* Trimble quotes what he calls H. W. Fowler's "crushing rebuttal" from Fowler's *Dictionary of Modern English Usage*:

> "It is a cherished superstition that prepositions must, in spite of the incurable English instinct for putting them late ..., be kept true to their name [preposition comes from a Latin word meaning "to place in front"] and placed before the word they govern. ... Those who lay down the universal principle that final prepositions are "inelegant" are unconsciously trying to deprive the English language of a valuable idiomatic resource, which has been used freely by all our greatest writers except those whose instinct for English idiom has been overpowered by notions of correctness derived from Latin standards. The legitimacy of the prepositional ending in literary English must be uncompromisingly maintained; in respect of elegance or inelegance, every example must be judged not by any arbitrary rules, but on its own merits. ..." (p. 83)

He follows this with the Winston Churchill anecdote: when an editor of his memoirs "corrected" a sentence of Churchill's ending in a preposition, Churchill wrote back, "This is the kind of impertinence up with which I shall not put" (p. 84).

*6. "Never split an infinitive" (pp. 84–85).* Trimble opens with a *New Yorker* cartoon: "Satan is seated on his throne in Hell, silently glowering. Before him stands one of his lieutenants, obviously worried, asking uncertainly, "If everybody's doing it, is it still a sin?"" (p. 84). He explains that splitting an infinitive — saying "to fully cooperate" rather than "fully to cooperate" or "to cooperate fully" — is not a recent phenomenon: infinitives "have been split repeatedly by reputable authors (Shakespeare among them) ever since the 14th century," until 18th- and 19th-century grammarians tried to "regularize" English on the Latin model and decreed that infinitives should not be split by adverbs, on the principle that related words belong together (p. 84). He offers three justifications infinitive-splitters would likely give if asked:

> "First, the split infinitive usually sounds better — that is, it has a more idiomatic rhythm to it, in part at least because we invariably hear infinitives split in common speech. Second, it allows the modifying adverb to be positioned where it will receive the most notice, which is directly ahead of the verb. (Example: "To really know her, you have to live with her.") Third, since grammarians recommend that modifiers should come, if possible, next to the word they modify, the adverb has as much right to sit next to the verb as the preposition *to* — and perhaps more right, not only because it is the more weighty of the two words, but because it frequently changes the very sense of the verb." (p. 84)

He states his own practice: "like most writers I go by ear and sense. When my ear tells me that a split construction sounds more natural, and when my sense assures me there's no ambiguity, I happily split the infinitive. If my ear tells me it's a toss-up, I don't split it because there's no pressing reason to" (p. 85). He cites grammarian George Curme's *Syntax*: "The split infinitive is an improvement of English expression" (p. 85).

*7. "Never write a paragraph containing only a single sentence" (pp. 85–86).* Trimble calls this rule "generally sound" and objects only to the dogmatic "Never." He again quotes Theodore Bernstein, from *Miss Thistlebottom's Hobgoblins*:

> "One cannot be arbitrary about paragraphing. It is a means of grouping thoughts, but much more it is a visual device. Much depends on the subject, the typography, the purpose of what is being written, the readers to whom it is addressed, and the conditions under which they are likely to read it." (p. 85)

He describes the one-sentence paragraph's virtues in newspaper writing: "It simplifies the reporters' task of presenting their facts in descending importance; it enables a hurried reader to digest those facts quickly; it offsets the tedium created by narrow columns and small type; and so on" (p. 85). Against the view that the device is fit only for journalism, he cites Robert Selph Henry's *The Story of the Confederacy*, which he calls one of "the indispensable studies of the American Civil War," noting that Henry "uses 17 one-sentence paragraphs in the first two chapters alone" and opens the book with two in succession:

> "The Confederacy was a belated attempt to exercise the right of a state to withdraw from the United States of America.
> Because it was belated, because it opposed a mere right in the abstract to the concrete force of economics and the inevitable trend of history, because it was burdened with the defense of the anachronism of slavery, it failed." (p. 85)

Trimble names three situations that can occasion a one-sentence paragraph:

> "(1) when you want to emphasize a crucial point that might otherwise be buried; (2) when you want to dramatize a transition from one stage in your argument to the next; and (3) when instinct tells you that your reader is tiring and would appreciate a mental rest." (p. 86)

His caution: "it's potentially dangerous. Don't overdo your dramatics. And be sure your sentence is strong enough to withstand the extra attention it's bound to receive when set off by itself. Houseplants wilt in direct sun. Many sentences do as well" (p. 86).

*Closing Thoughts (pp. 86–87).* Trimble recounts informally polling his classes each semester, starting in the 1970s as a new assistant professor in Texas, by asking, "Did you by any chance have any no-no's drilled into you in high school?" He reports hearing "the same seven no-no's" from nearly every student across three years, and that students were "scandalized" at his own contrary position, taking him for "some Berkeley heretic who had broken ranks with the English Teachers' Union" (p. 86). He connects this to a line from John Dewey's *Experience and Education* (1938):

> "He is lucky who does not find that in order to make progress, in order to go ahead intellectually, he does not have to unlearn much of what he learned in school." (p. 86)

He offers three reasons students hold onto the taboos despite contrary evidence in their own reading: they were taught them young by authoritative teachers; they unconsciously "gloss over inconvenient evidence" in what they read; and — "perhaps even more compelling" — because prose that naturally breaks the taboos does so without drawing attention to itself, so readers do not notice professionals breaking the rules (pp. 86–87). He quotes Judge Jerome Frank: "The basic appeal of the language is to the ear. Good writing is speech heightened in tone and polished in form" (p. 87). The chapter closes with two rules of thumb Trimble says should be "burned into every writer's brain":

> "If you wouldn't say it, don't write it." (p. 87)

> "Don't write any sentence you can't speak easily." (p. 87)

**Terms introduced.**

- *TOTELS / TOTELarians* — Trimble's coined name, "The One True English Language Sect," for the community of literary prudes who treat Formal English as a matter of religious law; he introduces it because, in his account, "their faith has gone nameless" (p. 77).
- *Pedant's stance* — critic Wayne Booth's term, quoted by Trimble, for a mode of writing that ignores or underplays the personal relationship between writer and reader and depends entirely on statements about a subject, leaving out "the notion of a job to be done for a particular audience" (p. 81).
- *"General English" style* — the conversational-but-serious register Trimble advocates as an alternative to Formal English; invoked here as the register in which occasional contractions are "perfectly acceptable" (p. 81).

**Connections.** The chapter continues the treatment of formality and audience from the preceding chapter, explicitly reusing that chapter's citation of Bergen Evans's *Atlantic Monthly* article and its earlier-quoted passages from Updike, White, Kael, Lamott, and Michener as counter-evidence against the contractions taboo (pp. 79–80). It also carries forward the "General English" style and the discussion of readers' fear of rejection introduced in that same preceding chapter (p. 80). Chapter 8's rejection of Formal English absolutism sets up the more granular diction and sentence-level guidance of later chapters, which assume the permissions established here (contractions, *I*, sentence-initial *But*/*And*, terminal prepositions, split infinitives, one-sentence paragraphs) rather than re-arguing them.

#### Chapter 9 — Critical Analysis: Jousting with Mencken

The chapter opens with two epigraphs, both attributed to their original speakers, not to Trimble:

> If I am a writer today, it is because my betters tugged and teased and bullied and seduced me into learning a craft, when all I had to begin with, like so many hundreds of others, was a talent. So I did learn; I go on learning.

— Brendan Gill, *Here at The New Yorker* (p. 88)

> Anybody who learns how to write learns how by doing it very intensively and also by being a reader and sort of attentive to styles.

— Peter Goldman, former *Newsweek* senior editor (p. 88)

Trimble's own opening paragraph quotes a third writer, Somerset Maugham, on H. L. Mencken's peer Voltaire: "If you could write lucidly, simply, euphoniously and yet with liveliness, ... you would write perfectly: you would write like Voltaire" (p. 88). Trimble then turns to Mencken (1880–1956) himself, calling him, by report of "other scribes," among America's finest and wittiest twentieth-century stylists, while noting "some of his prejudices make today's world cringe" (p. 88).

**Claim.** A critical reading of a persuasive but flawed text is best learned by doing it on a real specimen, sentence by sentence, rather than by abstract rule. Trimble stages this using H. L. Mencken's 1926 essay "Literature and the Schoolma'm" (from *Prejudices, Fifth Series*), which he assigned for nearly three decades in his Advanced Expository Writing seminar (English 325M) at the University of Texas. The essay is chosen precisely because it mixes "truths, half-truths, and fallacies" (p. 89) with real stylistic brilliance, forcing the reader to evaluate argument and style separately rather than let one carry the other.

**Mechanism.** Trimble's procedure has three stages: (1) present the specimen text in full; (2) supply the reader with a critical apparatus — questions to ask of every assertion, background context, and cautions against being intimidated by reputation or vocabulary; (3) show the apparatus in use, via his own and his students' worked rebuttals, ending with one complete student essay reproduced as a model response.

**Evidence and examples.**

*The specimen text.* Trimble reproduces Mencken's "Literature and the Schoolma'm" in full (pp. 89–92), with a footnote crediting the source and permission: "From PREJUDICES: FIFTH SERIES by H. L. Mencken, copyright 1926 by Alfred A. Knopf, a division of Random House, Inc. and renewed 1954 by H. L. Mencken. Used by permission of Alfred A. Knopf, a division of Random House, Inc." (p. 89). Mencken's essay, quoted here in full and attributed throughout to Mencken, not Trimble, opens:

> With precious few exceptions, all the books on style in English are by writers quite unable to write. The subject, indeed, seems to exercise a special and dreadful fascination over schoolma'ms, bucolic college professors, and other such pseudo-literates. One never hears of treatises on it by George Moore or James Branch Cabell, but the pedagogues, male and female, are at it all the time. In a thousand texts they set forth their depressing ideas about it, and millions of suffering high-school pupils have to study what they say. Their central aim, of course, is to reduce the whole thing to a series of simple rules — the overmastering passion of their melancholy order, at all times and everywhere. They aspire to teach it as bridge whist, the American Legion flag-drill and double-entry bookkeeping are taught. They fail as ignominiously as that Athenian of legend who essayed to train a regiment of grasshoppers in the goose-step.

— H. L. Mencken, "Literature and the Schoolma'm" (p. 89–90)

The essay continues through several linked claims, each attributed to Mencken. He argues that style "cannot be reduced to rules" because it is "the outward and visible symbol of a man," as fixed to its owner as skin, so that teaching it is "as silly as to set up courses in making love" (p. 90). He then states what Trimble later calls the essay's central "faulty inference": that because most high-school students write "a mass of confused and puerile nonsense," the pedagogues wrongly conclude the fix is technical training, when in Mencken's view "what ails them is not a defective technical equipment but a defective natural equipment. They write badly simply because they cannot think clearly. They cannot think clearly because they lack the brains" (p. 90).

Mencken supports this with two contrasting cases (pp. 90–92). President Warren Harding, "a highly self-conscious stylist," spoke with clarity and even eloquence on a subject he knew and cared about, the ideals of the Elks, but turned "obscure and absurd" when addressing Dante, a subject beyond his grasp — proof, for Mencken, that muddled style simply mirrors muddled thought, not poor technique. His own eleven-year-old niece, by contrast, already writes "clear, dignified and admirable English" about things that interest her, unspoiled by any schoolma'm's training, but would write as badly as Harding if asked to address Balkan politics. Mencken closes by estimating that only "about one-eighth of one per cent. of the human race" is born capable of clear thought, so that teaching the rest to think is as futile as "trying to teach a streptococcus the principles of Americanism"; the only fit use for the rest, he concludes, is to make them Ph.D.'s and set them "to writing handbooks on style" (p. 92).

*Trimble's critical apparatus.* Immediately after the essay, Trimble calls Mencken "an equal-opportunity satirist," convicting both teachers and students, and argues this even-handedness is itself useful because it "keeps us from succumbing to a comfortably simplistic Good Guys vs. Bad Guys mode of thinking" (p. 92). He sets a reading discipline to apply to every sentence, phrased as standing questions (pp. 92–93): does a claim, on its face, really ring true; does Mencken support it convincingly or merely assert it and expect the reader's trust; does what he says in one paragraph square with what he says in another; and does he define his terms wherever they need defining, or let words mean whatever suits him? Trimble demonstrates the last question on Mencken's own opening line, asking what "write" can mean in "writers quite unable to write," since it plainly is not a claim about grammar or spelling — so what exactly is being asserted, and can a reader let it pass (p. 93)?

To prepare students for the assignment, Trimble supplied a set of tips, cautions, and resources (pp. 93–94). He: gave them a handout on common logical fallacies, on the reasoning that they could not spot what they had no name for; urged them to look up unfamiliar words rather than assume pedantry on Mencken's part; urged wider reading in Mencken to get a fuller sense of his style and preoccupations; recommended George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" (1946) as a companion piece attempting what Mencken calls impossible; urged them to work out their own views on whether writing can be taught, drawing on their own experience in the seminar; reminded them to weigh the essay's 1926 publication date against the state of writing instruction at the time; and told them to treat "Schoolma'm" exactly as they would a classmate's draft — genially skeptical, not awed. He also told them to test counter-hypotheses against Mencken's claims — for instance, whether bad student prose might have causes besides missing "brains" — and closed by quoting columnist James Kilpatrick's assessment of Mencken as a writer "extravagant, intrepid, full of wild humor and large hyperbole" who nonetheless has "a way of passing from the sands of human affairs the nugget truth" (p. 94), asking students to hunt for those nuggets even amid the essay's excesses.

*Four student openers.* Trimble reports he assigned students to write their own response essays, opening "right down to cases," in imitation of Mencken's own direct opening move (p. 94). He reproduces four opening paragraphs from among "the many glorious openers" in his files (pp. 94–95), each unsigned. In brief: the first opener restates Mencken's syllogism about brains and clear thinking, then turns it back on him — Mencken's own essay is stylistically clear but, the student argues, its ideas are muddled. The second calls Mencken "the most infuriating writer" the student has read, arrogant and prone to inventing facts, yet credits him with never producing "empty drivel." The third continues that assessment, crediting Mencken with usually being right "on target" even when particulars are shaky, and invokes Hemingway's phrase about a writer's "built-in, shock-proof shit-detector" as an apt description of Mencken's instinct for the essential point. The fourth calls the essay "a hoot" and enjoyable to read, while flatly rejecting three of its central claims — that writing can't be taught, that style can't be taught, and that poor writers simply lack brains — as "nonsense," even while granting that Mencken's real purpose is to "skewer dullards" rather than to argue rigorously.

*Trimble's own ten-point rebuttal.* Trimble then works the essay himself, from the stance he says he would take as "a former pre-law student" and "a pedagogue with his honor to defend" (p. 96), giving the key points he would make against Mencken's argument (pp. 96–98):

1. Mencken never connects bad student writing to the teaching he condemns — if the pedagogues really are as incompetent as he claims, poor teaching is a plausible cause of poor student prose that he never considers.
2. He fails to apply his own case studies to students: Harding and the niece both write well on subjects they know and care about and badly otherwise, which suggests student writing may suffer from uninspiring assignments rather than absent brains.
3. His claim that thinking is "not acquired, but congenital" ignores concrete ways people learn to reason better — studying common fallacies, learning that assertion is not proof, linking sentences into a clear line, stating an unambiguous thesis, distinguishing fact from opinion and fair from unfair sampling, and anticipating a reader's confusions.
4. His claim that style is as uncontrollable as mood, and therefore untaught, ignores that style is simply self-presentation on the page, which can be deliberately shaped — toward simplicity, concision, a natural voice, stronger transitions, vivid diction — as Trimble illustrates with Mencken's own remark elsewhere that Lincoln's early prose was "remarkable only for its badness" before years of work refined it.
5. His claim that style cannot exceed the ideas behind it is undercut by his own essay, whose style is clear and lively even though several of its ideas are contradictory or commonplace.
6. His dismissal of teaching "lovemaking... out of a book" is answered by the later existence of widely used sex manuals and university courses in human sexuality.
7. He conflates "style" with "writing": writing is governed by real, teachable rules of grammar, spelling, and usage, while style, the personality of the prose, is less rule-bound but still shapeable.
8. He blurs speech and writing, though writing lacks a present, responsive audience and so demands skills speech does not — building context from scratch, encoding tone without a speaker's voice, thinking linearly, and mastering the written conventions.
9. His remark that Harding "put words together with skill" implies craft, and craft — including the recursive editing that turns a poor draft into a polished one — can be taught.
10. Trimble's summary rationale: the exercise trains "a healthy skepticism," testing every claim against common sense, personal experience, and other authorities, which is what he takes critical thinking to mean.

*A full student rebuttal: Jolene Shirley's "Can Writing Be Taught?"* Trimble reproduces one complete student essay as a model response, by Jolene Shirley, then a geology major (pp. 98–100). Rather than argue point by point, Shirley answers Mencken's title question — can writing be taught? — with a narrative: a fictional teacher walks a struggling student through successive drafts of one paper, and each pass corrects a different layer of the writing, in an order that recapitulates skills Trimble's own seminar had covered in its first five weeks. The first pass fixes the paper's self-contradiction and absent thesis; the second cuts wordiness and passive, indirect phrasing; the third corrects mechanics — usage pairs like "effect"/"affect" and "its"/"it's," documentation, punctuation; the fourth sharpens style itself, pushing the student toward specific, concrete diction and varied paragraph length in place of vague abstraction. Shirley concedes Mencken's point that raw talent — "something of the devilish in it," in Mencken's own phrase, quoted back at him — cannot be taught, while insisting that grammar, organization, concision, clarity, and vividness can be, and that a student equipped with this "blueprint" writes better on her next paper too, since her internal editing skill compounds. Trimble frames the piece as proof that a class assignment can be executed many different ways, calling it "no less fun, no less effective" than the point-by-point rebuttals (p. 95). A footnote on the same page quotes interior designer Stephen Shadley to the same effect: "You learn that there are a jillion different solutions to every problem" (Stephen Shadley, *Architectural Digest*, September 2001, p. 100, quoted in a footnote at p. 95).

**Terms introduced.**

- *Faulty inference from a sound observation* — Trimble's name for the structure he identifies in Mencken's central argument: the observation that student writing is often bad is sound, but Mencken's inference that this proves an innate defect rather than a teachable one is, in Trimble's analysis, unsupported (pp. 90–96).
- *Critical thinking* (as operationalized here) — for Trimble, the practice of testing every claim in a text against whether it "rings true," whether it is argued or merely asserted, whether it is internally consistent from paragraph to paragraph, and whether its key terms are defined (pp. 92–93).

**Connections.** The chapter functions as an applied capstone to the seminar sequence Trimble describes running across "325M": Jolene Shirley's essay explicitly walks through thesis statements, active voice, concision, mechanics, and concrete diction as skills already taught earlier in the course, so this chapter presupposes and exercises material Trimble treats at length elsewhere in the book on clarity, concision, and revision. Trimble's recommendation that students read George Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" (p. 93) points outward to that essay as a companion specimen — an attempt, in Trimble's framing, at exactly what Mencken calls impossible.

#### Chapter 10 — Dramatizing Your Ideas

The chapter opens with two epigraphs, both attributed to their original speakers, not to Trimble:

> My first newspaper city editor gave me a piece of advice that I have always remembered: "When you write a page of copy, kid," he said, "take it out of your typewriter and read it over. If any part of it bores you, then God only knows it's going to bore the reader."

— Tommy Thompson, author of *Blood and Money* (p. 101)

> Being, like all those who have worked in Hollywood, somewhat of a connoisseur of the damp fart, I place Mr. [Edmund] Wilson high on the list. His careful and pedestrian and sometimes rather intelligent book reviews misguide one into thinking there is something in his head besides mucilage. There isn't.

— Raymond Chandler, author of *Farewell, My Lovely* and *The Big Sleep* (p. 101)

Trimble's own opening paragraphs, quoted here as an example of the technique being demonstrated as it is described, mix long expository sentences with a compressed, punchy final one: "If you survived that high-school initiation rite known as the 'Five-Paragraph Theme,' you probably learned to view form rather mechanically, as a rigid organizing structure into which you dutifully slotted your ideas" (p. 101).

**Claim.** Form is not a fixed container to fill but a creative resource with rhetorical potential — everything on the page, from punctuation to plotline, can be shaped to produce pleasure in the reader, which Trimble calls the chief effect writing aims for. Trimble names this "artful presentation" and argues it applies as much to expository prose as to fiction: writers who master it turn from mere "chroniclers" into "dramatist[s] of ideas" (p. 103).

**Mechanism.** Trimble grounds the chapter in Barbara Tuchman, "two-time winner of the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction and, until her death in 1989, the country's premier popular historian" (p. 102). He recounts, from her book *Practicing History*, how her Radcliffe honors thesis failed for exactly the reason this chapter addresses: "The experience was terrible," she recalled, "because I could not make the piece sound, or rather read, the way I wanted it to. The writing fell so far short of the ideas.... I finished it, dissatisfied. So was the department: 'Style undistinguished,' it noted" (p. 102). Trimble glosses this verdict: "Translation: You bored us" (p. 102). Tuchman went into journalism for training, then returned to history writing and became a bestseller. Trimble quotes two passages from her explanation of what she learned, telling readers to substitute their own discipline for "history" as they read:

> To write history so as to enthrall the reader and make the subject as captivating and exciting to him as it is to me has been my goal since that initial failure with my thesis. A prerequisite ... is to be enthralled one's self and to feel a compulsion to communicate the magic. Communicate to whom? We arrive now at the reader, a person whom I keep constantly in mind. Catherine Drinker Bowen has said that she writes her books with a sign pinned up over her desk asking, "Will the reader turn the page?"

— Barbara Tuchman, *Practicing History* (p. 102)

> The writer of history, I believe, has a number of duties vis-a-vis the reader, if he wants to keep him reading. The first is to distill. He must do the preliminary work for the reader, assemble the information, make sense of it, select the essential, discard the irrelevant — above all, discard the irrelevant — and put the rest together so that it forms a developing dramatic narrative.

— Barbara Tuchman, *Practicing History* (p. 102)

Trimble draws the moral: "Be kind to your readers. They need all the help they can get — to get interested, to stay interested, to follow the arguments, to grant their validity" (p. 102). From this he derives the arc a writer follows: no longer "a mere chronicler, writing chiefly for herself," she "will become a dramatist of ideas," staging key points "for maximum drama" and orchestrating their sequence "so seamlessly that the reader feels swept along" (pp. 102–103). He states the chapter's organizing claim directly: "Artful presentation boils down to three things" (p. 103) — attractive formatting, interesting phrasing, and dramatic plotting — which structure the rest of the chapter as three numbered sections.

**Evidence and examples.**

*1. Attractive Formatting.* Trimble opens this section by quoting Samuel Johnson, "the great 18th-century critic," who "once famously declared that 'all pleasure consists in variety'" (p. 103) — the basis for his advice to vary word length, sentence length, and paragraph length, "not just a little, but a lot" (p. 103). He warns readers trained on the Five-Paragraph Theme that this will feel wrong at first, since it violates a rule of "correctness" from an earlier classroom, and urges them instead to "begin monitoring the paragraphing practice of professional writers," attending to how stylistic choices make them feel as readers, on the theory that "if something gives you pleasure, doing the same thing will give your own readers pleasure, too" (p. 103).

He then turns the chapter's own opening seven paragraphs into a worked exercise, inviting the reader to audit his practice against Tuchman's: "Note that three of the first seven paragraphs consist of just a single sentence. Good or bad?" (p. 103). He continues into sentence-length counts on the following page: the shortest sentences in that opening run 6, 7, 4, 4, and 2 words; the average is 14 words per sentence, with the two longest running 33 and 38 words (p. 104).

On word length, Trimble defers to Tuchman rather than counting syllables himself:

> In my opinion, short words are always preferable to long ones; the fewer the syllables the better, and monosyllables, beautiful and pure like "bread" and "sun" and "grass," are the best of all.

— Barbara Tuchman, quoted p. 104

Trimble states one disagreement with her: "Where do I disagree? With her word 'always,' which feels glib" (p. 104). He argues that longish, fun words — he cites "higgledy-piggledy," "balderdash," "curmudgeon," and "persnickety" — sometimes are "the very best word — best for accuracy, tone, rhythm, variety, impact" (p. 104), but affirms her general rule: "the fewer the syllables, the better," because it produces "a smoother, clearer, more conversational style — and a perfect foil for whatever longer words you might want to sprinkle in" (p. 104). A footnote adds Malcolm Gladwell's tribute to his mother from the Acknowledgements page of *Outliers: The Story of Success* (2008): "My mother, for her part, taught me how to express myself; she taught me that there is beauty in saying something clearly and simply" (p. 105, footnote).

Two further footnotes on p. 104 supply outside authorities for short words. Vincent Bugliosi, "the famed Los Angeles prosecutor and author of *Helter Skelter*," is quoted: "Lincoln's Gettysburg Address consisted of only ten sentences. Of his 271 words, 202 of them were just one syllable. But these historic words went through five drafts and were the result of two weeks' thought and preparation" (*Outrage*, Norton, 1996, p. 151; quoted p. 104). H. W. Fowler, called "England's preeminent writing authority," is quoted from *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage* (Oxford, 1926, p. 333): "it is a general truth that the short words are not only handier to use, but more powerful in effect; extra syllables reduce, not increase, vigour" (quoted p. 104). A third footnote cites Judge Richard Posner, who rates Benjamin Cardozo one of the four most eloquent judges in Supreme Court history and compared 60 of Cardozo's New York Court of Appeals opinions against those of his colleagues, concluding "Cardozo's sentences are shorter, making for a crisper, more dramatic style" (*Cardozo: A Study in Reputation*, University of Chicago Press, 1990, pp. 135, 127; quoted p. 104).

Trimble gives a direct exercise: "In the next essay you write, challenge yourself to work in both a one-sentence paragraph and a two-word sentence. For a gold medal, toss in a one-word sentence, too" (p. 105). A footnote confirms the grammatical legality of sentence fragments used this way, citing "America's top grammar-and-usage expert, Bryan Garner," and his entry "Incomplete Sentences" in *Garner's Modern American Usage*, 3rd ed. (Oxford U.P., 2009), pp. 455–457, adding that David Foster Wallace called the book "a work of genius" in his *Harper's* review (April 2001), reprinted as "Authority and American Usage" in *Consider the Lobster and Other Essays* (Back Bay Books/Little, Brown, 2006), pp. 67–127 (p. 105, footnote).

He then gives two further formatting devices. First, segmenting: in an essay four pages or longer, break it into "big chunks, each several related paragraphs long" — "[t]wo such chunks per essay is fine, but three is normally much better" — using extra white space (double or triple normal line spacing) at each turn, which spares the writer "having to concoct an elaborate transition there, since the shift speaks for itself" (p. 105). Second, subheads — "short, boldfaced phrases that announce, like a headline, the topic of the section they're introducing" — which additionally let a skimming reader glean subtopics and relocate sections later (p. 105). Trimble points readers wanting more to Jan V. White's *Editing by Design*, 3rd ed. (2003), calling it "the bible of many designers, art directors, and editors" (p. 105).

Trimble devotes a full passage to list formatting, opening with a diagnosis: writers trained in "so-called formal writing" run listed items into body text with only commas or semicolons, a mistake pros call "burying the information" (p. 106); displayed, indented lists with white space around them dramatize the same content instead. He states there are two choices. Enumeration uses numbers, "sometimes set in parentheses, sometimes not — it's all taste," with the lead-in sentence ending in a colon, and offers two equally acceptable punctuation styles (p. 106):

> 1. Item one.
> 2. Item two.
> 3. Item three.

or

> 1. item one;
> 2. item two; and
> 3. item three.

Trimble prefers the first style — periods, capitalized items — "whenever my lead-in speaks of a particular number of items," both because a missing number would leave readers wondering "how come" after being primed to count, and because it lets him cite an item later by number, "e.g., 'As we saw in case #3, the legislature....'" (p. 106). Where the lead-in gives no specific count — using a word like "several" instead — he recommends bullets instead, since they "imply no rank order, dispense with the clutter of numbers and parentheses, display your points with even greater white space, and offer visual variety" (p. 106).

He gives two equally reputable bulleted formats side by side — one capitalizing each fragment and ending it with a period, the other lowercasing and using semicolons with "and" before the final item — illustrated with a business-benefits list ("We expect several benefits: ... New customers ... Expansion of our business overseas ... More efficient use of our warehouses ... Fewer staff members on overtime"), and notes that in both versions the listed items are kept syntactically parallel, "otherwise, readers could stumble as they moved from point to point" (p. 107).

The two lists sit side by side on the page under the sentence "Look how smoothly and efficiently these lists read:", carrying the same lead-in line and the same four items; only capitalization and end punctuation differ, and neither column carries a heading beyond the shared lead-in:

| (left column) | (right column) |
| --- | --- |
| We expect several benefits: | We expect several benefits: |
| • New customers. | • new customers; |
| • Expansion of our business overseas. | • expansion of our business overseas; |
| • More efficient use of our warehouses. | • more efficient use of our warehouses; and |
| • Fewer staff members on overtime. | • fewer staff members on overtime. |

The wording is identical in both columns, so the punctuation is the whole of the contrast: one version sets the four benefits as free-standing sentences with initial capitals and full stops, the other as clauses of a single sentence with lowercase initials, semicolons, and a closing "and" before the last item — both offered as syntactically parallel lists that read smoothly (p. 107).

Immediately after, he demonstrates a principle of aesthetics — "Any change in a pattern will draw the eye" — with three lines of repeated type characters, set centered between the sentence "Look, for example, at these lines of type and see where your eye immediately alights:" and the paragraph beginning "The moral for us writers is this:", each designed so the reader's eye is pulled to the single anomalous character or spacing break in the run (p. 107):

    ••••••c••••••••••••••••••••••••••••
    xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxXxxxxxxx
    bbbbbbbbb bbbbbbbbb bb bbbbbbbbbbbb

In line 1, thirty-four bullets carry a lowercase roman *c* in the seventh position; in line 2, forty-one lowercase *x*'s carry a capital *X* in the thirty-fifth position; in line 3, thirty-two lowercase *b*'s break by word spaces into runs of nine, nine, two, and twelve. The first line catches the eye on a letter among dots, the second on a capital among lowercase, the third on a short run among long ones — where the change is in spacing rather than in the character itself.

He draws the moral: "Anytime you want to throw extra emphasis on something — a word, a sentence, a paragraph, a section — make it different from its surroundings. You'll also create more variety in the bargain" (p. 107).

*2. Interesting Phrasing.* At the sentence level, Trimble writes, artful presentation means "casting each sentence so that it compels our readers' attention and renews their interest" (p. 107). He lists the resources available, noting he cannot catalogue them all: "wit, succinctness, frankness, off-beat diction, irony, quick shifts in tempo, sprightliness, fresh metaphors, spot-on analogies, and droll hyperbole" (p. 107), adding the truism "You can't write better than you can read" as the reason writers absorb these moves by observing more advanced writers (p. 107). A footnote illustrates spot-on analogy with Anne Fadiman's *Ex Libris: Confessions of a Common Reader*, quoting her brother Kim's explanation for why three books stay spread-eagled on his bedside table: "To use an electronics analogy, closing a book on a bookmark is like pressing the Stop button, whereas when you leave the book facedown, you've only pressed Pause" (p. 107, footnote).

For droll hyperbole, Trimble cites essayist Joseph Epstein, who in a piece on high school reunions described an old classmate as "a woman with more eye shadow than a raccoon" (p. 108). Trimble reasons that a phrase like this earns a writer's next sentence its readership: a writer works for it partly for the pleasure of being "his own first reader," but "equally important because it buys him attention for his next sentence" (p. 108). He extends this into an analogy: writers occupy "the honor of Center Court," obligated to "play each shot intently, as if it were match point," because readers cannot foresee what phrases lie ahead and judge only by "the shots they've already seen, plus the current one" — so "each shot amounts to match point" from the reader's side (p. 108). He notes students are slow to grasp this because, unlike professionals, they typically write for "a captive audience — a teacher paid to read their every word" (p. 108).

He offers a worked practice from Bill Broyles, "founding editor of *Texas Monthly* and, later, editor in chief of *Newsweek*," who as an editor "would draw a heavy blue line across the page at the first point where his attention wandered, then immediately return the MS to its author for rewriting":

> If I drift off there, our readers are apt to also. They'll page right ahead to the next article. So the writer's got a problem there — a big one — and he needs to address it before we can proceed.

— Bill Broyles, quoted p. 108

Trimble traces the wandering-attention problem partly to Tuchman's "failure to 'discard the irrelevant'" and partly to underthought ideas, then quotes James Dickey — "winner of a National Book Award for poetry (*Buckdancer's Choice*) and author of the best-selling novel *Deliverance*" — on the labor of revision:

> It takes an awful lot of time for me to write anything. I have endless drafts, one after another; I try out 50, 75, or a hundred variations on a single line sometimes. I work on the process of refining low-grade ore. I get maybe a couple of nuggets of gold out of 50 tons of dirt.

— James Dickey, quoted p. 108

But Trimble says the more common culprit is what he calls "MA phrasing" — his shorthand for "merely adequate," itself "a kind way of saying 'inadequate'" (pp. 108–109). His students, editing each other's papers, marked prose with "MA" alerts and often supplied "witty recastings that take the very same idea and make it sing," which Trimble says teaches fresh phrasing faster than anything else, until "the writer starts pouncing on MAs himself" and grasps Robert Frost's line: "All the fun's in how you say a thing" (p. 109).

A second editing symbol, "MO," stood for "missed opportunity" — flagging places where a writer, often lapsed into "term-paper mode," passed up "a natural place for an anecdote. Or some dialogue. Or an analogy. Or a list. Or a personal example. Or a witticism. Or a description. Or a structural surprise" (p. 109). The intended lesson was that writers should actively "create those opportunities" rather than wait for them (p. 109).

*3. Dramatic Plotting.* At the macro level, Trimble warns against viewing an essay as simply an essay, since "the conventional essay format, which you've had pounded into you since junior high, is dead. It's static, formulaic, boring — in a word, undramatic" (p. 109). Readers, he argues, "want ideas dramatized. They don't want just to read about ideas, they want to experience them" and "want an easy way to keep everything clear in their heads as they move through a piece" (p. 109). His stated solution is "to give readers the sense of hearing a story" (p. 110), pointing back to Jolene Shirley's Mencken rebuttal from the previous chapter as a full essay-as-story, and to his own opening anecdote about Tuchman's Radcliffe thesis as an example of injecting story elements into exposition (p. 110).

He defines the goal as contriving Tuchman's "developing dramatic narrative," which at its simplest does three things (p. 110):

1. immediately hooks the readers' curiosity with some intriguing problem, issue, or question — something intrinsically dramatic enough for them to care about it;
2. goes on to develop aspects of the problem, thereby sustaining the dramatic tension; and
3. finally resolves the problem, thereby achieving closure.

Trimble states this is achieved through plotting at every level — sentence phrasing, paragraph and page-level variety, white-space breaks or subheads, and a coherent, "clean," reasonably dramatic overall story line viewed "through your reader's eyes, not just your own" (p. 110). He closes the section with two diagnostic questions to ask of every sentence (p. 111): "If I were the reader here, would this excite me or bore me?" — which forces reading one's own prose "through a stranger's eyes" — and "How can I dramatize this point more vividly?" He describes his own practice of casting a sentence "four or five ways" in his head before typing it, then continuing to rework it on rereading, testing whether he can cut words, "smuggle in some wit," add an example, or freshen the diction (p. 111).

Two footnotes close the section. One observes that two of the most perennially popular sentences in English — "Tell me a story" and "Once upon a time" — are each four words long, and argues stories are "one of humanity's most potent sources of pleasure" that all writers, not just novelists, should draw on (p. 110, footnote). The other, appended to a reference to Mencken (from the previous chapter) having "more fun with his writing" than anyone, reveals that Mencken's line about training "a regiment of grasshoppers in the goose-step" referred to a nonexistent legend: "There was no such legend. Mencken made it up," and Trimble admits he himself once searched for that "same crazy Athenian" before realizing "Mencken was just pulling their leg" (p. 111, footnote). Trimble ends the instructional portion of the chapter by quoting Gloria Steinem: "Writing is the only thing that, when I do it, I don't feel I should be doing something else instead" (p. 112).

Trimble then introduces the chapter's closing specimen: a student essay by Amy Jetel, "then an English major in my Advanced Expository Writing seminar," who had waited tables at Macaroni Grill and then Chuy's, a Tex-Mex restaurant in Austin, for over three years while self-supporting through college (p. 112). She wrote the essay in 1999 for the assignment "Friendly Persuasion." Trimble introduces it as "one of the most instructive ones I read all semester," adding, "It forever changed how I viewed waiters and their customers" (p. 112).

The full essay, reproduced verbatim (pp. 112–114):

> **THE PRICE OF SERVICE**
> *Amy Jetel*
>
> Imagine you're a waiter, and a big-top (a party of 7 or more) just sat down in your section. While you're trying to get their drink order, someone at the next table is tugging at your apron because their enchiladas are cold. Another table is waiting for you to get the staff together to sing "Happy Birthday"; the people in the next booth are staring at you while loudly slurping down the last of their drinks; another party has empty plates sitting in front of them; you still have to get 3 margaritas, 2 Bud Lights, 6 waters, 2 Cokes, and 2 teas for your big-top; the restaurant is out of ice; someone forgot to make more tea; the Coke syrup just ran out; and you really, really have to go to the bathroom. That's what we servers call being "in the weeds."
>
> Now imagine going through all of that and not getting paid.
>
> Servers who value their jobs wouldn't dream of asking customers why they left a lousy tip, or no tip at all. We can only speculate. Maybe they didn't expect their bill to be so high, and can't fathom paying much more. Perhaps some people start their gratuity meter at 15% and look for any reason to knock off percentage points throughout the meal. But most likely, those who regularly tip 10% or less are just uninformed about the service industry.
>
> Let me share some basic facts that every restaurant patron should know.
>
> **Fact: Tips are not "extra" income — waiters rely on them**
>
> In Texas, servers make $2.13 an hour. At the end of each shift, we claim how much money in tips we made — this is our taxable income. The government then taxes us out of our hourly wage. Since $2.13 an hour doesn't go very far after taxes — paychecks under a dollar are a common occurrence — a waiter's livelihood rides on the generosity of his or her customers. Our only source of income is tips.

— Amy Jetel, "The Price of Service" (pp. 112–113)

Continuing (p. 113):

> **Fact: Waiters don't keep your entire tip**
>
> People dine out so they don't have to cook, clean up after themselves, or even get up to refill their drinks. A restaurant is where you pay someone to do all of this for you. And the standard price of the waiter's service is at least 15% of your bill. Restaurants pay waiters an unlivable wage as an incentive for them to work harder and sell more. Counting on that 15% gratuity — the bigger the bill, the bigger the tip — a waitress will push bar drinks, appetizers, and desserts, making more money for the restaurant and, with luck, for herself.
>
> But if you tip less because you think your server is getting too much help (someone else brought chips to your table or refilled your glass), you should know that she's paying those people to help her. This is called "tipping out." For example, at my restaurant, we have to tip out 4% of our sales. So if your bill was $100, and you gave your waitress $15, she gets to keep $11. If you left 10%, or $10, she would keep $6. If you got so inebriated that you forgot to leave a tip, she would lose $4. Our customers aren't required to pay us for our service, but we still have to pay the bussers and the bar — tip or no tip.

— Amy Jetel, "The Price of Service" (p. 113)

Continuing (pp. 113–114):

> **Fact: Waiting tables requires profound patience**
>
> One night, I waited on a woman who asked for silverware as soon as she sat down. Since I was in the weeds at the time, I considered this a low-priority request — after all, she hadn't even ordered. When I returned to my section, loaded down with tea pitchers, food, and extra plates for my four other tables, she hissed, "I asked you for silverware!" I smiled and told her I would get it right away, but this didn't satisfy her. She jabbed at the heavens with open palms and snapped, "I have to wipe my hands!" Seeing the urgency of the situation, I promptly returned with her silverware — which, at my restaurant, isn't wrapped in a napkin.

— Amy Jetel, "The Price of Service" (pp. 113–114)

The essay's close (p. 114):

> The Silverware Lady has plenty of company. She shares a special place in all waiters' hearts with a host of other personalities: like Queenie, who answers the question, "Hi, how are you?" with, "Diet Coke and a bowl of queso"; or like Mute Woman, who gestures her requests with the subtle shaking of a chip-basket or empty glass; or like Mr. Condescension, who tries to make even the most self-assured waiter feel responsible for a kitchen mistake, a dirty fork, a cold nacho. With our income in the hands of these characters, we must smile, comply, and resist the urge to demand respect.
>
> So why do we do it? Well, work is never boring and access to food is a plus. But for college students, as most servers are, it's the only way to work less than 25 hours a week and still make rent. The aching feet and back, late hours, and hectic work can all be worthwhile when someone shows their appreciation, not by telling you what a great job you did, but by leaving a fat 20%. It's what waiters live for.

— Amy Jetel, "The Price of Service" (p. 114)

Trimble reports that he later asked Jetel — "now a successful Austin attorney — in fact, a name partner in her law firm" — what had prompted the essay and its form. He quotes her emailed reply in full (p. 114):

> After waiting tables for over 3 years, I was fed up with people's lack of empathy for others, their desire to get something for nothing, and their view that waiters are, by definition, less intelligent and less worthy human beings than they. Although the essay is primarily about tipping, my hope was to also give readers insight into the world of waiters so that they could have a little compassion.
>
> So I guess you could say that my mood when writing the essay was irritated, my state of being, jaded. And it felt really, really good to write it — very cathartic!
>
> I felt it absolutely necessary to add drama to the paper if I were to successfully foster a sense of compassion in my readers. I wanted them to actually feel a little stressed out by my opening scene of being in the weeds; I wanted them to personally dislike the Silverware Lady, Queenie, and Mr. Condescension. I also figured that nobody would personally identify with the "bad customer" caricatures (even if they were, in fact, bad customers!); and seeing themselves as the "good customers," perhaps they'd strive to be even better. After all, who wants to be called some nasty nickname behind their back by a disgruntled waiter? :)

— Amy Jetel, quoted p. 114

Trimble then works through the essay himself, giving a bulleted list of the elements that "helped Amy dramatize these ideas and that left us, her classmates and me, feeling exceptionally well taught — one mark of a great essay" (p. 115). The list runs eighteen items (pp. 115–116):

- The direct invitation, couched in the second-person "you" ("Imagine you're a waiter"), to enter her world and briefly experience her work night — "This opener epitomizes 'drawing the reader in.'"
- The many concrete details that actualize the scene (a song title, exact numbers and brands of beverages, and, "my favorites," "tugging at your apron" and "staring at you").
- The 92-word stretched sentence in the opening paragraph ("Another table is waiting for you...") that dramatizes, "with clause after clause after clause," a chaotic, exhausting environment — "and at below minimum wage, no less."
- The 21-word sentence about "Servers who value their jobs..." immediately lightened by the next one, a crisp 4-word sentence ("We can only speculate") whose shortness gains it extra emphasis for transitioning into the three conjectures that follow.
- The fascinating lessons in Server Lingo ("big-top," "in the weeds," "tipping out") that also further accredit the author, "who knows the score and who is generous with her knowledge."
- The eye-opening facts that prove as disturbing as they are informative ("In Texas, servers make $2.13 an hour"; "Our only source of income is tips"; "Waiters don't keep your entire tip"; etc.).
- The two one-sentence paragraphs, one of which "packs a mule kick" ("Now imagine going through all of that and not getting paid") while the other neatly lays out what's ahead and simultaneously piques curiosity ("Let me share with you some basic facts that every restaurant patron should know").
- The comic anecdote about the hissing Silverware Lady ("One night..."), which allows both Amy and the reader to have more fun with the essay, "an essay that might otherwise feel preachy — precisely what she did not want."
- The snatches of dialogue — "not much, but enough to put us there and hear some very spoiled patrons talking" ("I asked you for silverware!"; "I have to wipe my hands!"; "Diet Coke and a bowl of queso"), plus Amy's cordial "Hi, how are you?"
- The patrons who, "behaving like stereotypes, really earn their pejorative nicknames" ("The Silverware Lady," "Queenie," "Mute Woman," "Mr. Condescension").
- The 76-word sentence in which Amy brings together, "as if at one table," a host of other personalities that "share a special place in all waiters' hearts."
- The effortless bridging throughout.
- The short, inevitable question ("So why do we do it?") opening the final paragraph, which "has exactly the right, conclusive sound" and prepares readers for "her thoroughly sensible answer."
- The fluid, conversational style — 16 contractions, "all of them ever so easy on the ear," plus simple but very effective conjunctions used as transitional words ("And," "But," "So").
- The clever formatting of the three "Fact" statements, which are not only boldfaced but, for even greater impact, centered on the page, "thereby reinforcing the essay's already strong structure and ensuring that her key points don't get lost."
- The assumption, straightforwardly presented, that actually drives the essay ("But most likely, those who regularly tip 10% or less are just uninformed about the service industry").
- The pleasingly direct statement of the author's plan for us ("Let me share some basic facts that every restaurant patron should know").
- The comfortable sentence length, averaging just 19 words — an average that would have dropped to 16 without the two stretched sentences (92 and 76 words) — with Trimble adding, "But who would have wished those two magnificent sentences any shorter?"

Trimble closes the chapter: "It's a great little essay. With it, Amy changed my life. What higher praise can a reader give an author?" (p. 116).

**Terms introduced.**

- *Artful presentation* — Trimble's umbrella term for the rhetorical shaping of everything on the page (punctuation, layout, word choice, phrasing, sentence structure, paragraphing, plotline, and content itself), organized here into three components: attractive formatting, interesting phrasing, and dramatic plotting (p. 101).
- *Dramatist of ideas* — the stance Trimble urges writers to take instead of being a mere "chronicler," staging key points for maximum drama and orchestrating their sequence so the reader "feels swept along," a phrase and framework he draws from Barbara Tuchman (pp. 102–103).
- *Burying the information* — Trimble's/pros' term for running listed items into body text with commas or semicolons instead of displaying them as an indented list (p. 106).
- *MA phrasing* — Trimble's own shorthand, used with students, for "merely adequate" phrasing, i.e., writing that is technically fine but flat (pp. 108–109).
- *MO* — Trimble's editing-symbol shorthand for "missed opportunity," flagging a place where a writer failed to reach for an anecdote, dialogue, analogy, list, personal example, witticism, description, or structural surprise (p. 109).
- *Developing dramatic narrative* — Barbara Tuchman's phrase, adopted by Trimble, for prose that hooks readers' curiosity with a problem, develops it to sustain tension, and then resolves it (pp. 102, 110).

**Connections.** The chapter explicitly builds on Chapter 9: Jolene Shirley's Mencken-rebuttal essay, presented there as a model of critical rebuttal, is reused here as the model of an essay that fully becomes a story (p. 110). The chapter's discussion of concision and word choice (short words, cut wordiness) presupposes and echoes material Trimble treats at greater length elsewhere in the book on clarity and economy. The closing student essay by Amy Jetel parallels the closing use of a full student essay (Jolene Shirley's) in Chapter 9, establishing a recurring structural device — ending an instructional chapter on a complete, annotated student specimen.

#### Chapter 11 — Revising

**Claim.** The chapter is not an argument in Trimble's own voice; at one page, it consists entirely of a quoted exchange from a *Paris Review* interview with Ernest Hemingway on the labor of rewriting.

**Mechanism.** No rule, test, or procedure is stated — the chapter offers the exchange itself, unglossed by Trimble, and nothing else.

**Evidence and examples.** The full exchange, quoted verbatim (p. 117):

> Interviewer: How much rewriting do you do?
>
> Hemingway: It depends. I rewrote the ending of Farewell to Arms, the last page of it, thirty-nine times before I was satisfied.
>
> Interviewer: Was there some technical problem there? What was it that had stumped you?
>

>
> —Paris Review interview

The words are Hemingway's, not Trimble's: he is the one answering, recounting thirty-nine passes at the last page of *Farewell to Arms* and reducing the reason for all of them to four words — "Getting the words right."

#### Chapter 12 — Proofreading

**Claim.** Proofreading — specifically, verifying every quotation against its source before submitting a piece of writing — is where a writer's credibility is won or lost; Trimble treats it as a test of professionalism, not a minor housekeeping step.

**Mechanism.** The chapter states its procedure through narrative rather than as a numbered rule: check every quotation against its source, and if a first check leaves any doubt, check it again. The chapter's epigraph frames the standard —

> Don't expect your reader to accept a piece of writing that you wouldn't accept yourself.
>
> —Donald H. Ross (p. 118)

— and the anecdote that fills the rest of the chapter cashes this out as an instruction: "double-check — triple-check, if necessary — everything you quote" (p. 119), on the reasoning that transcribing a quotation correctly is "the most mechanical of tasks, requiring minimal intelligence" (p. 119), so a writer who cannot be trusted with it cannot be trusted with anything harder.

**Evidence and examples.** Trimble's one example is autobiographical, recounting a graduate seminar with Professor Maynard Mack at Berkeley (pp. 118–119):

> Years ago, back when I was a graduate student at Berkeley, I had the extraordinary good fortune of taking a seminar with Professor Maynard Mack, then on semester's leave from Yale.
>
> To my classmates and me, it felt like taking a class with Einstein, for Professor Mack was, by all counts, the most distinguished English scholar in the world. President of the Modern Language Association, chairman of Yale's English Department, preeminent authority on Shakespeare, the last word on Pope and Homer as well, general editor of Norton's acclaimed World Masterpieces series, author of the most frequently reprinted article ever penned on Hamlet — the honors ran on and on. An authentic genius, this man. Also a singularly gracious, unpretentious individual.
>
> Anyway, for this Pope seminar, which proved a real doozie — of the 20 students who started, only 7 of us finished — we had to write a major research report every other week and, occasionally, read it to the group. The only cautionary instructions Professor Mack had given us were some terse words appended to his first assignment. They were enough to stop the heart:
>
> P.S. Put it in apple-pie order, as if submitting it to a learned journal.
>
> One day, some weeks into the term, a classmate was delivering his report when Professor Mack, who normally listened in silence, softly interrupted.
>
> "Excuse me, Mr. Rierson," he said. "Is that last quotation correct?"
>
> "I believe so, sir," Rierson replied, blushing.
>
> "But you're not sure?" Mack's voice carried only the barest edge. We felt the room chill.
>
> "I'm almost positive it is, sir."
>
> "Would you kindly double-check it for us?"
>
> "Yes, sir," said Rierson, who immediately began thumbing through his volume of Pope for the passage in question. Having found it, he began silently comparing his transcription of it against the text while the rest of us held our breath.
>
> Suddenly Rierson blushed scarlet. "Uh, I made a small mistake," he said. "The word 'fearsome' should have been 'fulsome.'"
>
> "Everything else correct?" Professor Mack asked, the mild question hinting of further disasters.
>
> Rierson's eyes darted back to the text and he resumed checking. More blushes. "There are two other errors, sir," he confessed, looking like the unhappiest person on the planet.
>
> "Please tell us what they are," Professor Mack said. Whereupon Rierson proceeded to explain them, his voice cracking in embarrassment and growing so low that we had to strain to hear him.
>
> When he had finished, Professor Mack looked him dead in the eye and said: "Mr. Rierson, the most important thing a writer has going for him is his credibility. Transcribing quotations correctly is the most mechanical of tasks, requiring minimal intelligence." Pause. Then Mack's voice, normally ever so civil, began crescendoing as he resumed: "If a writer can't be trusted with so mechanical a task, what then, Mr. Rierson, can he be trusted with?" Long pause. Then: "From here on, Mr. Rierson, I want you to double-check — triple-check, if necessary — everything you quote. Let us have no more careless errors."
>
> That little incident taught me more of lasting value than many entire courses. Among its great lessons was the meaning of professionalism.

The example demonstrates the chapter's claim directly: Rierson's paper contains three transcription errors against Pope, and Mack's response — mild in tone, escalating in implication — reframes a "mechanical" slip as a credibility failure, then converts the episode into a standing instruction to double- and triple-check every quotation.

**Terms introduced.** None coined; Mack's phrase "apple-pie order" (p. 118) names the standard of polish and accuracy he expected of a submitted report — fit to hand to a learned journal.

**Connections.** Chapter 11 (Revising) precedes it. Chapter 13 (Punctuation), which opens a new part of the book ("Odds and Ends," p. 120), follows immediately.

### Part 2 — Odds & Ends

#### Chapter 13 — Punctuation

The chapter opens with two epigraphs, both attributed to their original speakers, not to Trimble:

> Punctuation, to most people, is a set of arbitrary and rather silly rules you find in printers' style books and in the back pages of school grammars. Few people realize that it is the most important single device for making things easier to read.

— Rudolf Flesch (p. 121)

> In conversation you can use timing, a look, inflection, pauses. But on the page, all you have are commas, dashes, the amount of syllables in a word. When I write, I read everything out loud to get the right rhythm.

— Fran Lebowitz (p. 121)

**Claim.** Punctuation is not an arbitrary rulebook but a working toolkit, and each mark has a distinct job that a writer can learn to feel by ear before learning it as a rule. Trimble teaches the marks one at a time — semicolons, commas, parentheses, dashes, colons, hyphens, and exclamation points — giving for each the mark's function, a governing test or rule (including stated exceptions), and worked examples, mostly drawn from professional prose, journalism, and his own students' sentences.

**Mechanism.**

##### Semicolons

Trimble describes the semicolon as an "automatic coupling device" that joins two or more related, complete sentences into a single complex thought, doing conversationally what a half-pause does in speech (p. 122). No connective word is required, though one may be added. His stated rule, boxed as a rule of thumb, is:

> If you can replace your semicolon with a period, you're OK. If not, try a comma or a dash. (p. 122)

The one stated exception is "using semicolons like super-commas to separate a series of lengthy items — especially when one or more of those items uses a comma itself" (p. 122). He lists four occasions where semicolons prove especially useful (p. 124): (1) presenting two or more sentences as parts of a single thought; (2) giving the reader a rest stop inside a sentence too long or complex to read straight through; (3) linking two or more related, parallel-structured sentences to emphasize their contrast or likeness; (4) separating items within a series, especially when commas already occur inside one or more members of that series, or when items need to be separated into classes. He also addresses a misconception directly: semicolons do not, in themselves, make prose heavier or more formal; that effect comes from surrounding bad writing habits — "pretentious jargon; galumphing sentences and paragraphs; excessive abstractness; impersonality; passive verbs; humorlessness" (pp. 125–126) — and the semicolon gets blamed by association.

##### Commas

Trimble's governing test, given as a boxed rule, is:

> Use a comma when there's a light pause AND a drop in pitch. (p. 126)

The test procedure: read the sentence aloud "in your best radio voice"; wherever you pause to stay intelligible and your pitch drops, insert a comma; if still in doubt, have a friend read it aloud (p. 126). He grounds this in a cited authority: "the primary purpose of the comma is to prevent misreading" (*Words into Type*, p. 127, italics his).

He then states several specific rules. The compound-sentence rule, boxed and described as one he has students memorize and chant:

> Separate the parts of a compound sentence with a comma before the conjunction. (p. 127)

The transitional-word rule:

> Transitional words like however, therefore, then, consequently, besides, indeed, likewise, otherwise, and nevertheless are set off by a comma on both sides — or by a semicolon and comma if they fall between complete clauses. (pp. 128–129)

The direct-address rule:

> When you address a person directly by name, set off the name with commas to prevent a miscue. (p. 130)

This rule extends to substitute names of address ("sir," "coach," "ma'am," "Sis," "Dad," "my dear") and has a stated exception: "when you have two words working in tandem" (as in "Hey Bobby"), tested by whether the pitch rises rather than drops on the name — if it rises, "kill the comma" (p. 131).

On the serial comma, Trimble takes an explicit side in what he calls a contentious style question: journalists tend to drop the comma before the final "and" in a series as superfluous, while "most usage experts and editors" defend it; he sides with the defenders "because a final comma eliminates any ambiguity that would oblige a rereading of the sentence" (p. 131). His practical instruction: "Insert that comma as a matter of course" (p. 132).

On the comma splice, he defines it as "a punctuation fault that occurs when we use just a comma to connect ('splice') two independent clauses" (p. 132), and gives four numbered repair options: (a) insert an explanatory conjunction such as *because* or *for* right after the comma; (b) replace the comma with a semicolon; (c) break the two clauses into separate sentences; (d) recast the sentence (p. 132).

##### Parentheses

Trimble frames parentheses, commas, and dashes as sister "Separator" marks, each capable of setting off the same parenthetical matter, with parentheses the one to use "most sparingly" because they are "physically intrusive," stop the forward march of prose, can make a reader "feel whispered at," and "like to breed" (p. 133). His instruction: "use them uncomfortably, and keep them brief" (p. 133).

He states a placement rule for the comma that follows a parenthetical insert:

> A parenthetical insert is part of the preceding clause. So if that clause needs a final comma, put it after the closing parenthesis. (p. 133)

Simplified: "Never put a comma right before a parenthesis. Always wait until after" (p. 133). A companion rule covers question marks and exclamation points:

> Question marks and exclamation points can go before an opening parenthesis if they end a quotation there. Otherwise, they go after the closing parenthesis. (p. 134)

A third rule covers the period, for a parenthetical remark that could stand as its own sentence or remain attached to the preceding clause:

> If you want a parenthetical remark to stand as a separate sentence, capitalize the first letter and put the period inside the closing parenthesis. (This sentence is an example.) But if you want the remark to remain with the preceding clause, put the period outside your closing parenthesis (like this). (p. 134)

##### Dashes

Trimble calls the dash "the most dramatic and spirited" of the three Separator marks, stepping in when parenthetical matter wants emphasis or when the sentence needs a sudden break (p. 135). He lists five things it does particularly well (pp. 135–137): (1) mark an interruption or break in thought; (2) serve as a conversational colon or light bridge; (3) isolate a concluding phrase for emphasis or comic effect; (4) insert a parenthetical explanation, qualification, or amplification; (5) mark a gathering-up of several ideas, often a series of subjects. He then gives four further reasons journalists rely on it (p. 137): a long column of type looks heavy without occasional dashes for visual variety; piled-up commas around modifying phrases clog the flow, which a dash relieves; dashes substitute for italics, dramatizing a word or phrase by setting it at a distance; and dashes make a livelier substitute for a colon.

He gives four cautions. First, distinguish the single dash (setting off something at a sentence's end) from the double dash (a pair wedging in parenthetical matter), and never mix the two kinds in one sentence (pp. 137–138); he also advises resting the dash "for three or more sentences after each use" to avoid it losing its effect (p. 138). Second, a fingertip test for misuse of the double dash:

> Cover all the words inside your double dashes. If what remains is coherent and grammatical, you're home free. (p. 138)

Third, "don't use a comma after a dash, even if the syntax seems to want it. The dash works alone and will silently absorb the work of the comma," which he notes is "a 20th-century simplification," since earlier prose often paired the two marks (p. 138). Fourth, the dash prints as one long line and, about 95% of the time now, "attached" — jammed against the surrounding text with no surrounding space — though some writers still prefer an "open" style with spaces on both sides; either is acceptable "so long as you're consistent" (p. 139). Fifth, he warns against the increasingly common mistake of using a hyphen where a dash belongs (p. 139).

He then distinguishes the family of horizontal marks by length and use (pp. 139–140): the **hyphen**, shortest, for compounds, disambiguating prefixes, and syllable breaks; the **en dash**, roughly the width of a capital N, used to mean "to" or "through" (dates, times, page ranges), typically mistyped as a hyphen by writers unaware of it; the **em dash** (or "1-em dash"), roughly the width of a capital M and the most common dash — what most people simply call "a dash"; the **2-em dash**, twice an em wide, used to indicate missing letters; and the **3-em dash**, triple an em (roughly six hyphens), used in bibliographies to stand in for a repeated author's name.

##### Colons

Trimble's basic rule: "The colon is roughly equivalent to that is or namely" (p. 141), used when the first clause serves as a prelude that the second clause explains, particularizes, or completes. When the colon introduces a list or series instead, "its meaning changes to the following or as follows" (p. 141). He notes some authorities object to a colon placed directly after a verb (rather than after a complete statement or the words *as follows*/*the following*), and leaves the choice to the writer, noting he does it both ways himself (pp. 141–142).

He gives three further numbered points (p. 143): (1) the colon introduces a quotation when the quote runs longer than one sentence or illustrates the point just made in the lead-in clause (a topic he says he takes up at the start of the next chapter, "Quoting"); (2) if the clause following the colon is a complete sentence, its first word may be capitalized or not depending on the emphasis wanted — he himself normally capitalizes it, "figuring the reader has a right to know that a full clause is coming"; (3) to avoid confusion, don't use more than one colon per sentence, and, for the same reason, avoid letting a semicolon follow a colon unless the sentence has a series of items that themselves require semicolons.

##### Hyphens

Trimble's subject here is the compound modifier (also called a "unit modifier" or "phrasal adjective") — a phrase temporarily functioning as an adjective, which by itself rarely needs hyphens but requires them once placed before a noun, "to show that the phrase is now functioning as a unit," preventing confusion or writerly "howlers" (p. 143). His boxed two-part test:

> Hyphenate your modifiers if you can't 1. reverse their order or 2. remove one of them without damaging the sense. (pp. 144–145)

He states two named exceptions to this test (p. 145): (1) don't hyphenate a compound modifier that starts with an *-ly* adverb (e.g., "widely known speaker"), because the adverb necessarily modifies the next word and so is "automatically compounding," making the hyphen redundant; (2) don't hyphenate a compound modifier that follows its noun rather than preceding it ("artists are well known" versus "well-known artists"), a rule he says he follows by convention though he does not know its rationale and does not like it. He adds two further points: *self* always takes a hyphen as a prefix (self-confidence, self-denial, self-esteem, self-taught, self-made, self-preservation); and "suspension" hyphens are used when the parts of compound words get separated, as in constructions pairing a partial compound with a full one (p. 145).

##### Exclamation Points

Trimble reports two authorities' cautions on the mark: New Yorker editor William Maxwell's advice "that every writer has a lifetime ration of three exclamation points," and crime novelist Elmore Leonard's stricter rule, quoted directly: "Keep your exclamation points under control. You are allowed no more than two or three per 100,000 words of prose" (p. 145). Trimble gives four reasons the mark is "generally ill-advised" (p. 146): (1) it is cheapened by writers given to cuteness, exaggeration, and melodrama, giving it "a bad odor among serious readers"; (2) many readers prefer understated humor to the "trumpeted, self-approving variety"; (3) a genuinely funny witticism doesn't need an "Applause" sign, and an unfunny one only earns resentment for demanding one; (4) omitting the mark is a form of self-protection — a deft joke earns credit for dry wit, while a misfired one leaves no proof a joke was even intended. His stated exception: "The exclamation point must be used, however, after true exclamations or commands," as in "Whew!" "Go!" "How lovely!" (p. 146).

**Evidence and examples.**

##### Semicolons

Trimble opens with a sophomore's confession of comma-avoidance: "The first semester of Freshman English I used to write only short sentences so I wouldn't have to put commas in" (p. 121). To show the semicolon's effect, he takes two short sentences —

> A beauty is a woman you notice. A charmer is one who notices you.

— and links them first with a comma and conjunction: "A beauty is a woman you notice, while a charmer is one who notices you," then with a semicolon:

> A beauty is a woman you notice; a charmer is one who notices you.

— Adlai Stevenson (p. 122)

He judges the comma-conjunction version weak because "the *while* feels too explicit," and the semicolon version "unified, uncluttered, balanced, elegant" (p. 123). Four benefits follow, each with examples. Variety needs no separate example. Compression is shown with:

> Many American lawyers would disagree; they have long prided themselves as generalists, able to perform any legal task.

— *Newsweek* (p. 123)

Tighter contrasts is shown with Alexander Pope's couplet: "True ease in writing comes from art, not chance, / As those move easiest who have learned to dance" (p. 123).

Unity is shown with a Mark Twain paragraph giving four reasons he was once unaware slavery was wrong:

> In my school-boy days I had no aversion to slavery. I was not aware that there was anything wrong about it. No one arraigned it in my hearing; the local papers said nothing against it; the local pulpit taught us that God approved it, that it was a holy thing, and that the doubter need only look in the Bible if he wished to settle his mind — and then the texts were read aloud to us to make the matter sure; if the slaves had an aversion, they were wise and said nothing. In Hannibal we seldom saw a slave misused; on the farm, never.

— Mark Twain (p. 124)

Trimble footnotes the final clause: though "on the farm, never" looks like it violates the rule against joining less-than-complete sentences with a semicolon, it is in fact "an elliptical construction, with the comma correctly used to mark the omission," understood as "on the farm we never saw a slave misused" — a syntactically complete clause (p. 124).

The four numbered occasions for semicolons each get a worked example (pp. 124–125). For presenting sentences as one thought: "Training is everything. The peach was once a bitter almond; cauliflower is nothing but cabbage with a college education" (Mark Twain). For a long or complex sentence needing a rest stop: "With all the world to choose from, [creative writers] invariably select subjects closest to their inner feelings; and when they choose subjects seemingly alien to them they invariably alter them to correspond to their personal condition, even though what emerges may seem, to the uninitiated, remote and unrelated" (Leon Edel).

For parallel, contrasting or like sentences, he gives two examples. From Pete Axthelm on football scoring proposals:

> Some [football critics] would create a sliding scale making long kicks worth more points than short ones; others would return the ball to the line of scrimmage instead of the defenders' 20-yard line after missed kicks, making it riskier to attempt long ones.

— Pete Axthelm (pp. 124–125)

And from *The New York Times*, on off-year local elections:

> As in all off-year elections, many of this year's scattered races were decided on purely local issues. In Detroit, for example, the voters cast their ballots along racial lines to elect the city's first black mayor; in New York, the local Democratic organization overwhelmed three lackluster rivals to pull off Controller Abraham Beame's mayoral victory.

— *The New York Times* (p. 125)

For separating items in a series that itself contains commas, he gives three examples. A *Newsweek* roster of committee members, grouped in class pairs:

> Among its 35 or so working members are Ivan Allen Jr., the former mayor of Atlanta, and William Baker, the president of Bell Labs; Mary Wells Lawrence, of her own advertising agency, and Clare Boothe Luce; Daniel Patrick Moynihan, now ambassador to India, and Sol Linowitz, former ambassador to the Organization of American States.

— *Newsweek* (p. 125)

Eric F. Goldman's "venerable triad of rules" for history and memoir writing:

> Of course, essential to any good history or memoir is the venerable triad of rules: to respect the facts enough to keep burrowing for them, especially when they confound your preconceptions; to care enough about your subject to express judgments clearly, vigorously and if necessary, in a way that may antagonize; and withal, to exercise restraint on yourself so that what you call history or memoir is not an emanation of your own prejudices and self-interests but a work which makes an honest and sustained effort to deserve that fine old adjective, fair.

— Eric F. Goldman (p. 125)

And a *Newsweek* account of the Nixon tape recordings:

> The recordings, she said, were full of "funny things" — the President plunking his feet on the desk, where the bug was, with an impact "like a bomb"; fragments of talk drowned out by rattling china, by a marching band blaring outside, by Mr. Nixon inexplicably whistling during meetings; hopeless scrambles when four people talked at once "and you couldn't get one voice."

— *Newsweek* (p. 125)

##### Commas

Trimble's demonstration sentence for the pause-and-pitch test:

> Taking the long subway ride home to Queens Tom reviewed his hectic day. (p. 127)

He walks the reader through hearing the pause and pitch-drop after "Queens," which both prevents the misreading "Queens Tom" and marks where the introductory clause ends (p. 127).

A footnote earlier in the same discussion carries Robert Frost on why wit matters in prose: "There's got to be wit. And that's very, very much left out of a lot of this labored stuff. It doesn't sparkle at all" (Robert Frost, quoted in a footnote at p. 126).

For the compound-sentence rule, he reproduces nine real sentences with the comma missing, each one he says he "tripped over," instructing the reader to read them aloud to hear the collision (p. 128):

> Because of the late hour he expected to find no one there but Larry, who had a project due the next morning, was still at his desk.
>
> The Bible can teach you to love God and Dostoevsky can teach you to write great novels.
>
> Bob went to the ranch hoping to see Rebecca and David, thinking Kate would be there, too, arrived an hour later.
>
> She seems to be focused on money and Victor's indecisiveness about getting a new career guarantees she won't see more money from him anytime soon.
>
> The U.S. currently participates in agreements with more than 85 countries and as many as 20% of our domestic research programs have some international component.
>
> But this was a decision made by the former editor and the current editor said that he disagreed with his decision that Spellcheck undermines the responsibility of copy editors.
>
> His involvement in the confrontation displays Sammy's emerging autonomy and his decision to quit his job liberates him from the subordinate position he fills there.
>
> The question of who is a "responsible person" under §6672 is interpreted as a mixed question of law and fact and certain facts will almost invariably prove dispositive of a finding of responsibility.
>
> But Bill had been given scarcely a month to invent a new house and the plans Jim bid on, though nice-looking, were sketchy.

(p. 128)

For the transitional-word rule he gives three worked sentences. The problem case:

> You'll agree however that Wagner's music is better than it sounds. (p. 129)

The commonly miswritten fix, with only one comma: "You'll agree however, that Wagner's music is better than it sounds," which he compares to the visibly wrong "You'll agree however) that Wagner's music is better than it sounds" (p. 129). The correct version: "You'll agree, however, that Wagner's music is better than it sounds" (p. 129). A second example, testing ambiguity of which clause the transition belongs to:

> She is old enough to see her parents' faults however she is not old enough to forgive them. (p. 129)

The commonly miswritten fix — "She is old enough to see her parents' faults, however, she is not old enough to forgive them" — fails to show that *however* belongs with the second half; Trimble gives the corrected two-sentence version, "She is old enough to see her parents' faults. However, she is not old enough to forgive them," and then the semicolon version: "She is old enough to see her parents' faults; however, she is not old enough to forgive them" (pp. 129–130).

For direct address, Trimble gives five paired examples, each showing what the writer actually wrote against what he or she thought was conveyed:

> I hear Charlie. / I hear, Charlie.
>
> Most Scots are either one or the other. But you are both my friends. / Most Scots are either one or the other. But you are both, my friends.
>
> Remember Nancy. / Remember, Nancy.
>
> See that Howard, not a bother at all. / See that, Howard? Not a bother at all.
>
> Corey isn't that screwed up? / Corey, isn't that screwed up?

(p. 130)

For substitute names of address, he gives five examples to read aloud for the pitch drop: "You know, ma'am, that flight is canceled." "Yes, sir, I promise." "Sit still for me, buddy." "OK, Mom, I'll be there shortly." "What's up, y'all?" (p. 131). For the tandem-name exception, he contrasts "Hey Bobby, got a spare jersey?" (comma kept, pitch drops) with "Hey, man, got any spare change?" (p. 131).

For the serial comma, his ambiguity example:

> The motley prisoners in that cell included an unemployed actor, a murderer, a junkie, a man obsessed with flying saucers, an ex-cop with a craving for doughnuts and assorted females — all of them coexisting in surprising harmony. (p. 131)

For the comma splice, his fault example and its four repairs: "Bob wanted to leave early, he had a date that night" (fault); "Bob wanted to leave early, for he had a date that night" (a); "Bob wanted to leave early; he had a date that night" (b); "Bob wanted to leave early. He had a date that night" (c); "Since he had a date that night, Bob wanted to leave early" (d) (p. 132).

##### Parentheses

Trimble demonstrates three separators handling the same aside, all reproduced in full to show the equivalence:

> That second trip to Italy (the one in '95) cost me a lost heart, but I'm not complaining. (p. 133)
>
> That second trip to Italy, the one in '95, cost me a lost heart, but I'm not complaining. (p. 133)
>
> That second trip to Italy — the one in '95 — cost me a lost heart, but I'm not complaining. (pp. 133–134)

For overused, overweight parentheses:

> Sir Harvey's most recent book (it was originally published in 1994 in Great Britain under the title All You Ever Wanted to Know About Me But Were Afraid to Inquire) sank without a bubble. (p. 133)

For the comma-placement rule, he gives right and wrong versions of the same invented dialogue. Correct: "'OK, we're settled on All in the Family as the title,' the network executive replied, finally agreeing (though half in spite of himself), 'but I really do draw the line on calling the guy Oedipus. I think we can come up with a more American name than that.'" Incorrect, with the comma misplaced before the parenthesis: "'OK, we're settled on All in the Family as the title,' the network executive replied, finally agreeing, (though half in spite of himself) 'but I really do draw the line on calling the guy Oedipus. I think we can come up with a more American name than that.'" (p. 134).

For the question mark/exclamation point rule, three examples with hypothetical page citations: "'Was that Billy who called?' (119), she asked." "'Oh, come on, bite!' (221), the fisherman yelled, exasperated." "Did the fisherman actually yell, 'Oh, come on, bite!' (221)?" (p. 134).

##### Dashes

Before the numbered list, Trimble notes the dash's versatility with brief illustrations of its informal side-jobs: moonlighting as a colon ("We got the quickest — a border collie"), a trailing-off thought ("If I could only —"), a censor ("Oh, d—!"), and an interruption in dialogue ("Jerry, you just said —." "No, honey, you misheard me") (p. 135). His comparison sentence for standard prose: "But George, who hadn't had lessons, was as graceful as a drunk Schnauzer" (comma-handled) versus "That letter (a copy is enclosed) explains the School Board's position" (parenthesis-handled) (p. 135).

For (1) marking an interruption or break in thought:

> Life without romance — well, you might as well be in prison or a slug under the earth.

— Charlie Chaplin (p. 135)

> To be middle-aged is to be — well, what is it? It is to have hope without expectation, courage without strength, desire without the fire.

— H. L. Mencken (p. 135)

For (2) serving as a conversational colon or light bridge:

> Furthermore, recent discoveries in the physiology of the brain suggest that there may be two different kinds of intelligence — analytic, conceptual, verbal intelligence, located in the left hemisphere of the brain, and intuitive, artistic intelligence in the right hemisphere.

— *Newsweek* (p. 135)

> That's the worst of facts — they do cramp a fellow's style.

— C. S. Lewis (p. 135)

> Deadly, deflating accuracy is Sheed's game — the art of the neatly nipped hope.

— Melvin Maddocks (p. 136)

> Sorry for the exclamation mark — I know how much you loathe the overpunctuation of under-thought frivolity.

— Gale Hickman (p. 136)

For (3) isolating a concluding phrase for emphasis or comic effect:

> The Dow Jones industrial average plummeted nearly 52 points in a week — the worst break in years.

— *Time* (p. 136)

> For three days, Rose Mary Woods, 55, sat tense and bristly before an openly skeptical Judge John J. Sirica and testified that she may have erased at least some of the eighteen minutes missing from one of Mr. Nixon's secret Watergate tapes — all, of course, by "a terrible mistake."

— *Newsweek* (p. 136)

> Fashion is illusion, shimmer, magic, mirage — and money: $62.3 billion a year.

— Shana Alexander (p. 136)

> Fame creates its own standards. A guy who twitches his lips is just another guy with a lip twitch — unless he's Humphrey Bogart.

— Sammy Davis, Jr. (p. 136)

> I could never learn to like her — except on a raft at sea with no other provisions in sight.

— Mark Twain (p. 136)

For (4) inserting a parenthetical explanation, qualification, or amplification:

> Easter weekend arrived, and our cherry trees — about thirty of them — blossomed in unison.

— Peter Mayle (p. 136)

> Many of the wars fought by man — I am tempted to say most — have been fought over such abstractions. The more I puzzle over the great wars of history the more I am inclined to the view that the causes attributed to them — territory, markets, resources, the defense or perpetuation of great principles — were not the cause at all but rather explanations or excuses for certain unfathomable drives of human nature.

— J. William Fulbright (p. 136)

> IQ tests — and the academic establishment built upon the assumptions about the supreme value of conceptual intelligence — do not measure right-brain intelligence, much less such other vital performance factors as emotional understanding and patience.

— *Newsweek* (p. 137)

For (5) marking a gathering-up of several ideas, often a series of subjects:

> The art of the surprise witness, the withering cross-examination, the sudden objection phrased in arcane formulas — all seem to bespeak a profession based on elaborate training and requiring consummate skill.

— *Time* (p. 137)

For the single-versus-double-dash caution, Trimble's mixed-mark example: "Just as Rebecca wearily printed out her paper — it had taken her nearly two weeks of writing — she learned that she had missed Rupert's deadline — by an hour" (p. 138), where the first two dashes form a double dash for inserting and the third is a single dash for setting off.

For the double-dash fingertip test, his failing example: "Our purple martins arrived today — a sight we'd been anticipating all winter — I couldn't be happier," which, with the double-dashed material covered, collapses to the run-on "Our purple martins arrived today I couldn't be happier"; his repair adds a conjunction after the second dash: "Our purple martins arrived today — a sight we'd been anticipating all winter — and I couldn't be happier" (p. 138).

For the no-comma-after-a-dash caution, he builds up an example. Starting sentence: "I'd heard Vancouver was gorgeous, but nothing prepared me for this!" With a double-dash construction inserted: "I'd heard Vancouver was gorgeous — all the travel books gush over it — but nothing prepared me for this!" — the second dash absorbing the work a comma would otherwise do (pp. 138–139).

For attached versus open dash style, his two contrasting demonstration sentences: "When text is attached — as this phrase is — you have no spaces." "When text isn't attached - and you'll note this phrase isn't - you do have spaces." (p. 139). For the hyphen-as-dash mistake: "When the little hyphen is mistakenly used for a dash - as is the case here it looks weird" (p. 139).

For the hyphen itself, his examples: *self-interest* (a compound) and *pre-engineered* (a prefix disambiguated), and the syllable-break demonstration *hy-phen-a-tion* (p. 139). For the en dash, meaning "to" or "through," his six examples: "1998–1999"; "April–May 2010"; "pages 28–32"; "Matthew 3:5–6"; "Chicago–New York flight"; "8:00 a.m.–5:00 p.m." (p. 140). He also describes the manuscript notation copy editors use to mark an en dash for a typesetter — a numeral "1" written over the hyphen with an "N" under it, "like a fraction" — and gives keystrokes for producing one: Option-hyphen on a Mac, Ctrl + the numeric-keypad minus sign on a PC (p. 140). For the em dash, he notes it is conventionally typed as two consecutive hyphens on a typewriter, with no surrounding space, and gives its keystrokes: Option-Shift-hyphen on a Mac, Alt + Ctrl + the numeric-keypad minus sign on a PC; copy editors mark it with a "1" over an "M" (p. 140). For the 2-em dash, his example of a missing name: "His source was Col. B—" (p. 140).

##### Colons

For the basic "that is/namely" use, his lead example:

> Girls have an unfair advantage over men: if they can't get what they want by playing smart, they can get it by playing dumb.

— Yul Brynner (p. 141)

For a case where the writer spells out the equivalence rather than leaving it implicit:

> Nothing else is so likely to teach us what at this moment we most need to learn: namely, that more things can be actually said than we seem to believe and that so far as prose at least is concerned the best is that whose texture is firmly denotative and which can, as statement, stand firm on its own legs.

— Joseph Wood Krutch (p. 141)

Trimble observes that the colon "really isn't necessary" here — a comma before and after *namely* would work as well — but judges that Krutch's long sentence "welcome[s] more of a break than the first comma would have provided," adding, "I think he was right. It's an awkward sentence" (p. 141).

For the "as follows" use introducing a list, Woody Allen's numbered joke:

> The best methods of combatting organized crime are: 1. Telling the criminals you are not at home. 2. Calling the police whenever an unusual number of men from the Sicilian Laundry Company begin singing in your foyer. 3. Wiretapping.

— Woody Allen (p. 141)

Trimble notes some authorities dislike Allen's placement of the colon directly after the verb "are," preferring a colon after a complete statement or after the words *as follows*/*the following* (pp. 141–142).

He calls the six examples that follow a way to "teach you the use of the colon much more quickly and painlessly than a set of abstract rules will," and observes that in most of them "the colon functions as a slightly more formal version of the dash" (p. 142):

> Examining love is like examining a stocking: if you hold it up to the light and stretch it to search for snags, any snags there are may well run and ruin the stocking.

— Jo Coudert (p. 142)

> We need an alternative which is as challenging, emotionally exciting, and dramatic as war — and we have one now: rebuilding the cities, making war on our old prejudices (this war is in man's skull), and defeating poverty.

— John W. Gardner (p. 142)

> As an originator, powered by extraordinary energy of mind, Freud was capable of great forward bounds, so that he habitually extrapolated a whole system from a single item: saw the ocean in a drop of water, perceived a law of human behavior in a dropped handkerchief.

— Barbara Tuchman (p. 142)

> Fields' very appearance evoked shouts of laughter from an audience: the manorial air that was so obviously false, the too benign smile, the larcenous eye.

— Corey Ford (p. 142)

> I make a sharp distinction between two kinds of concentration: one is immediate and complete, the other is plodding and only completed by stages.

— Stephen Spender (p. 142)

> The vision behind this book is simple and horrifying: it is the vision of the humanely educated Nazi.

— Eliot Fremont-Smith (p. 142)

For the capitalization-after-colon point, Trimble gives one sentence rendered both ways to show that either is acceptable:

> Starbucks got it right: Find a good location, and customers will find you.
>
> Starbucks got it right: find a good location, and customers will find you.

(p. 143)

The printed layout is a two-column comparison with bold "Wrong"/"Right" headings (p. 143):

Each pair differs only in its hyphens, so the parallel columns put the unhyphenated string directly beside its hyphenated form and let the eye register the phrase binding into a single unit modifying the noun; hyphen counts vary with the length of the phrase — one hyphen for two-word modifiers, two for "22-year-old."

A second such table follows for longer compound modifiers, again printed as a two-column comparison, under the line "Now some longer ones, where things get rougher still:" — the "Wrong"/"Right" headings are not repeated (p. 144):

The right-hand forms chain every word of the phrase together with hyphens, so a five-word modifier such as "stickler-for-the-rules" reads as one block before the noun; the prose immediately following points at the false junctions the left column creates: "over lake," "ditch stand," "track mind," "rules attitude."

To show that missing hyphens can leave real ambiguity rather than mere awkwardness, Trimble poses four unresolvable phrases, set in two centered lines of two columns with no headings and no corrected column (p. 144):

| | |
| --- | --- |
| long dead fish | rare book store |
| light red shirt | first floor plan |

Each phrase can be parsed two ways depending on where the hyphen would go — long-dead fish or long dead-fish, rare-book store or rare book-store — so the block shows ambiguity rather than a wrong/right contrast. Trimble follows the puzzlers with three real cases from his own experience. A student's phrase, "little hands on writing experience" — was she describing her small hands, or how little actual writing experience she'd had? The Army's order for "20 foot long rods of plutonium" — did it want rods 20 feet long in an unstated quantity, or 20 rods each a foot long? A Texas state senator's "last minute objections" — offered at the last minute, or merely small ones on top of earlier objections (p. 144)?

The test itself is set off as a ruled box with a drop shadow along its right and bottom edges, closing the page in bold, its opening and closing lines running the full width of the box and its two numbered conditions indented and centered under it with "or" centered on its own line between them (p. 144):

> Hyphenate your modifiers if you can't
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;1. reverse their order
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;or
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;2. remove one of them
>
> without damaging the sense.

The layout splits the single sentence — "Hyphenate your modifiers if you can't … without damaging the sense" — around the two numbered conditions, so each condition reads as completing the same sentence. For the two-part hyphenation test, his worked pair: "a hot, muggy night" passes both parts (reversible to "a muggy, hot night"; either word removable without damaging sense) and so needs no hyphen, while "a three base hit" fails both (unreversible as "a base three hit"; "a three hit" is meaningless and "a base hit" damages the intended sense) and so requires one — "a three-base hit" (pp. 144–145).

For the *-ly* adverb exception, his three examples: "widely known speaker"; "firmly held opinions"; "sharply reduced prices" (p. 145). For the noun-following exception: "artists are well known" versus "well-known artists"; "the pages were tear stained" versus "the tear-stained pages" (p. 145). For suspension hyphens: "The high- and low-season rates differ by as much as $80 a day." "In second- and third-down situations, Murphy prefers a pass-option play." (p. 145).

##### Exclamation Points

Trimble quotes Elmore Leonard directly on his own stricter allowance: "Keep your exclamation points under control. You are allowed no more than two or three per 100,000 words of prose" (p. 145). He also cites Jim Holt's 2009 *New York Times Book Review* essay on H. W. Fowler, quoting Holt's warning about the cost of hyper-correctness generally: "Heightened self-consciousness about usage is the enemy of vigor" (p. 146), which Trimble extends to exclamation points specifically — that overcaution about the mark will, like overcaution about usage generally, "increase our sense of inhibition" and lead a writer to "shut down at least a part of our true self to avoid tongue-clucking from the more sober set" (p. 146). For the stated exception, his three examples of true exclamations or commands: "Whew!" "Go!" "How lovely!" (p. 146).

**Terms introduced.**

- *Compound modifier* (also "unit modifier" or "phrasal adjective") — a phrase temporarily functioning as an adjective, which needs hyphenation once placed before a noun so the reader can see it working as a single unit (p. 143).
- *Serial comma* — the comma placed before the final "and" or "or" in a series, which Trimble defends as removing ambiguity (pp. 131–132).
- *Comma splice* — the fault of joining two independent clauses with only a comma (p. 132).
- *Direct address* / *indirect address* — talking to a person by name ("Hey there, Holly") versus talking about them ("I knew Holly back in Berkeley"); direct address requires commas around the name (p. 130).
- *Attached* / *open* dash style — a dash set with no surrounding space ("attached," the modern majority convention) versus one set off with spaces on both sides ("open") (p. 139).
- *En dash*, *em dash* (or "1-em dash"), *2-em dash*, *3-em dash* — the family of horizontal marks beyond the hyphen, distinguished by length and function: the en dash for "to"/"through" ranges, the em dash as the common dash of everyday prose, the 2-em dash for missing letters or names, and the 3-em dash for a repeated author's name in a bibliography (pp. 139–140).
- *Suspension hyphens* — hyphens used where the parts of two compound words are separated and only one full compound is spelled out, as in "high- and low-season rates" (p. 145).
- *Screamers* — newspaper parlance for exclamation points (p. 146).

Its colon rule for introducing quotations — a comma for a short quote introduced by a phrase like "she said," a colon for a completed statement illustrated by a quote, a multi-sentence quote, or an indented block quote — is explicitly deferred to and picked up by Chapter 14, "Quoting," which Trimble names directly at the point of the rule (p. 143). The chapter's recurring "read it aloud" procedure for locating pauses and pitch drops echoes the ear-based diagnostic Trimble uses elsewhere in the book for judging sentence rhythm.

#### Chapter 14 — Quoting

The chapter opens with an epigraph attributed to its original speaker, not to Trimble:

> The first question in a manuscript reader's mind is: does the writer know his business? Little errors ... raise a doubt.

— Gorham Munson (p. 147)

**Claim.** Quoting is a set of learnable mechanical conventions — how to punctuate the lead-in to a quotation, where commas and periods fall relative to closing quotation marks, how to indent and introduce a block quote, how to punctuate dialogue, how to cite a source, and how to mark an ellipsis or an editorial insertion — and Trimble works through each convention rule by rule, with a worked example attached to nearly every rule.

**Mechanism.**

##### Punctuation Introducing Quotations

Trimble gives a comma rule and a colon rule. The comma rule: use a comma "whenever your quote is short and you're introducing it with a phrase like 'she said' or 'he replied' or '[So-and-so] observed'" (p. 147). The colon rule covers three other situations: "(1) when you've made a statement and are now supplying a quote to illustrate or prove it; (2) when your quote exceeds one sentence; or (3) when you're indenting the quote because it's hefty" (p. 147).

A third case takes no introductory punctuation at all: when a quoted phrase or clause is integrated directly into the grammar of the writer's own sentence. "No introductory punctuation is needed unless the syntax of your sentence requires it" (p. 149). He flags one specific hazard here: a lead-in ending in *that* must not be followed by a comma before the quotation begins (p. 149).

##### Punctuation at the End of Quotations

The main rule, boxed in the text:

> Commas and periods always go INSIDE closing quotation marks, even if the comma or period wasn't part of the original quotation. (p. 149)

Trimble calls this "plainly counterintuitive" and notes that British practice is the reverse — in Great Britain, "everything you see published there has the comma or period outside the marks" — but American convention holds regardless, and violating it reads as unschooled (p. 149). The corollary rule, also boxed, restores what he calls "the world of reason":

> Colons and semicolons go OUTSIDE closing quotation marks. And so do exclamation points and question marks — if they weren't part of the quotation. (p. 150)

He warns against a common evasion: placing the comma or period directly beneath the closing quotation mark as a "happy compromise." His verdict: "Resourceful, yes, but it's like observing a yellow light by stopping midway through the intersection" (p. 150).

##### Miscellaneous Small Points

To isolate a specific word or phrase from surrounding text, or a word used in a special (technical, humorous, or ironic) sense, set it off with quotation marks or italics (p. 150). Citing Ken Macrorie's *Telling Writing*: "More often than not, the words *say*, *call*, *refer to as* are followed by quoted words" — to which Trimble adds *and* the word and *the words* (p. 151). For extra emphasis, or when citing several words at once, he recommends underlining or italics instead of quotation marks, noting the two are "typographically equivalent," a holdover from typewriter days when underlining stood in for italics (p. 151).

One rule he marks for special emphasis — "probably ought to be set in bold caps" — is that a cliché must never be placed in quotation marks (p. 151). Two reasons: doing so "only compound[s] your offense by highlighting the trite expression," and it irritates readers because it implicitly claims the writer knows better while using the cliché anyway (p. 151).

##### Indented Quotations

Block quotes ("extracts") are traditionally typed single-spaced (MLA style asks for double-spacing, which Trimble calls "prodigal") and indented moderately — MLA specifies 10 spaces — from the left margin, optionally from the right margin too (p. 151). The convention: "Most professionals will indent prose quotes that exceed four lines. Verse quotes, on the other hand, normally get indented when they exceed two lines" (p. 151), since crowded lines of verse are hard to read and most poems depend on their form being visibly displayed. Quotation marks are omitted from block quotes, since the indentation itself signals quotation and marks would be redundant (p. 151).

Block quotes are normally introduced with a colon, though the lead-in can instead be woven seamlessly into the quotation, beginning in mid-sentence (p. 148, p. 151–152). Trimble names three common mistakes inexperienced writers make with block quotes (p. 152–153). Mistake #1: ending the lead-in with a period instead of a colon, which leaves the quote "hanging below it, unattached, in limbo," so the reader cannot tell whether the quote illustrates what precedes or follows it. Mistake #2: sandwiching the block quote into the middle of the writer's own sentence, which is bad for two reasons — it "almost always compels a rereading of the entire sentence," and it is usually ungrammatical since most quotes end in a period, which improperly terminates the surrounding sentence. Mistake #3, called "the commonest blunder of all," is leading into the quote "ultramechanically" (e.g., "McMurtry wrote:") and saving all commentary, if any is given at all, until after the quote — forcing a reread, or risking the reader skipping the block entirely.

Trimble's positive instruction: skilled writers "take pains to sell their oh-so-precious quote in their intro," and preview it enough that the reader has a context and the writer's slant on it before plunging into the block (p. 153).

##### Orphan Quotes

"An 'orphan' is writers' slang for a quote lacking explicit attribution. To journalists, especially, it's a cardinal taboo, and rightly so" (p. 153). Trimble gives four ways to repair an orphaned quote by supplying attribution (p. 154), each demonstrated on the same underlying sentence.

He also distinguishes the orphan quote from a related but lesser fault: "an 'orphan quote' with a 'blind quote,' which is another, less serious, taboo among journalists and other nonfiction writers." A blind quote is "attributed to some vague, conspicuously unnamed source such as 'a congressman' or 'a senior White House official.'" Blind quotes are sometimes needed to protect a source, "but orphans are ALWAYS a no-no" (p. 154).

##### Dialogue

Trimble offers a numbered "starter kit" of basics for writing dialogue (p. 155–156):

1. Give yourself permission to flout standard written English, since dialogue "follows its own rules" under what he calls Rule #1: "Authenticity before all else" (p. 155). He lists devices to use freely: sentence fragments ("frags"); short paragraphs, even one-word grafs; colloquialisms and verbal slumming (*gonna*, *wouldya* or *wouldja*, *dunno*); contractions, including offbeat ones heard in conversation; monosyllabic words (*but* instead of *however*); repeated words or phrases; interruptions; internal interruptions; internal pauses; trailing-off thoughts; and questions like "Why?" and "Really?" (p. 155).
2. Avoid semicolons and long paragraphs, "nobody talks in semicolons" (p. 155).
3. Avoid illogical speech-tag verbs — pairing a verb of facial expression with dialogue it cannot physically produce. The rule: a verb is acceptable if it can describe both a facial expression and a way of talking (like *sneer*); some, like *laugh*, are close calls (p. 155).
4. "Let your reader supply 90% of the exclamation points and italics" (p. 155).
5. In direct address, set off a person's name with a comma even where the pause is slurred over in speech (p. 155–156).
6. Draw on a lexicon of semiverbal interjections (p. 156).
7. Work in elliptical constructions and comma splices that mirror real speech (p. 156).

For models, Trimble recommends Elmore Leonard — "maybe the cleverest dialogue-writer in America" — for authentic street talk, though "definitely not for the queasy"; Michael Connelly as an equally talented technician and Trimble's "all-time favorite writer of detective fiction"; Studs Terkel's oral-history bestseller *Working* (Ballantine, 1974) as a model of real transcribed speech; and Michael Crichton's memoir *Travels* (Ballantine, 1988) as "a virtual textbook on how to write great dialogue" (p. 156–157).

##### Punctuating Run-on Quotations of Poetry

"To conserve space, verse quotations of two lines or less should be run in, in quotation marks, as part of your text," using a slash (also called a virgule) to mark the line break, with a space on each side of the slash. New lines begin with a capital letter unless the poet did otherwise (p. 157).

##### References for Quotations

"For the convenience of readers, you should specify the page reference (or line reference, if the lines are numbered) for each quotation" (p. 157). Trimble describes the traditional footnote form — the initial note gives author, title, city of publication, publisher, date, and page; subsequent notes to the same source give just author's name and page (p. 157) — then describes the shift toward "parenthetical references," brief citations in parentheses directly after the quotation, now preferred especially when most quotations come from a single work (p. 158). This has been further simplified: many writers now give just the bare page or line number in parentheses where the reference is unambiguous, using "p." and "l." (plural "pp." and "ll.") only where confusion is possible (p. 158).

When quoting multiple authors, each parenthetical citation carries the author's last name and the page number, separated by a single space, not a comma (p. 158). When quoting one author repeatedly and consecutively, the page number alone suffices until a different author is quoted. When citing more than one work by the same author, a shortened title is added to the reference (p. 158). At the essay's end, a "Works Cited" list follows standard bibliography form (p. 158).

Citing plays gets separate treatment. A Shakespeare line reference may take any of three parenthetical forms — roman act, roman scene, arabic line; roman act, arabic scene, arabic line; or all arabic — with the final number always the line, not the page, since Shakespeare's plays are in blank verse and fully line-numbered. Act is capitalized when referring to a specific Act (e.g., "Act II"); scene is not, since no ambiguity threatens it. Citations to non-Shakespearean plays are usually just Act and page (p. 158–159).

##### Punctuating Parenthetical References

Trimble states three related rules (p. 159):

> Rule #1: A parenthetical reference completes the clause containing your quote, so your final punctuation of that clause should follow the reference, not precede it.
>
> Rule #2: As for any comma or period that originally appeared at the end of your quote, simply cut it — it's now functionless and would create double-punctuation.
>
> Rule #3: But if your quote ends in a question mark or exclamation point, keep that punctuation so your quote will read intelligibly. (p. 159)

##### Ellipses

"Ellipsis points — three spaced periods ( ... ), a.k.a. 'dots' — are used to show readers where you've omitted some text in a quotation. The omission itself is called an 'ellipsis' (pl., 'ellipses')" (p. 160). Dots are unnecessary for a mere quoted clause or phrase, since the fragment is self-evidently partial; but they should be inserted whenever omitting them risks misleading the reader about the original (p. 160). He recommends restraint, since scattered dots distract readers and provoke curiosity about what's hidden (p. 160). Dots can normally be dropped when the ellipsis falls at the very start of a quotation: the writer instead builds a lead-in that flows grammatically into the quotation, so that the quotation's lowercase initial letter signals the truncation on its own. His test: "you can mentally delete the quotation marks and be left with a perfectly grammatical sentence" (p. 160).

He then walks through numbered situations (p. 160–162):

1. If a sentence ends in a quotation that itself ends in an ellipsis, the writer must still supply a final punctuation mark (typically a period) in addition to the three dots — since "the ellipsis, while written as three spaced periods, doesn't itself function as a period" — for four dots total, the fourth being the true period (p. 160–161).
2. When quoting a longish passage and cutting an unimportant middle chunk, if the sentence preceding the planned omission was itself complete (not truncated), the writer bumps the first of four dots directly against the last word of that sentence — showing the sentence really ended there — then adds a single space and three more spaced dots for the ellipsis proper. Trimble calls this "yet another way that writers have of quietly demonstrating their professionalism" (p. 161).
3. When omitting one or more whole lines from a quoted poem, the writer types "an entire line of consecutive, moderately spaced periods, as wide as the poem itself," and lets the parenthetical line-number reference at the end let readers calculate how many lines were dropped (p. 162).
4. To express a thought trailing off, as in dialogue, use just three spaced periods (not the customary four) at the sentence's end, skipping a space before the first period (p. 162).
5. For any other ellipsis situations, Trimble refers readers to *The Chicago Manual of Style*, 16th ed. (p. 162).

##### Editorial Insertions (Square Brackets)

To insert a brief note or clarification into a quotation, Trimble warns against the common mistake of using parentheses, "for how will we know the insert is yours rather than the author's own?" The instruction: use square brackets instead (p. 162).

##### Ellipses

The start-of-quote case, ellipsis dots dispensed with because the lead-in flows grammatically into the quotation:

> Jacques Barzun summed up the importance of clarity with his remark that "a written exercise is designed to be read; it is not supposed to be a challenge to clairvoyance."

— Jacques Barzun (p. 160)

Situation 1, a sentence-ending quote with a trailing ellipsis, needing the true final period as a fourth dot:

> Mme. Geoffrin remarked of her grandmother, "She spoke so pleasantly about things she did not know, that no one wished she knew more of them. . . ."

— Mme. Geoffrin (p. 161). Trimble notes that in the original a comma followed the word *them*, silently droppable since the ellipsis already signals an omission (p. 161).

The running text on this page states the terminal-ellipsis rule as inserting a space after the last word, then four evenly spaced dots, the fourth being the true period. As set on the page, though, neither this example nor the one following is spaced that way: in both, the first dot sits hard against the last letter of the word — the same distance a normal period sits after a letter — and only the remaining three dots are spaced apart from it and from each other, with the closing quotation mark then bumped tight against the fourth dot. That bumped-first-dot construction is the one the chapter's own rule 2 (below) assigns specifically to an omission following a complete sentence, on the following page.

A sentence containing an ellipsis both mid-sentence and at the end, shown to display the difference between three dots and four:

> The professor replied, with a smile, "Writing is a difficult art . . . because it involves thinking logically and interestingly, two operations that are unnatural to our minds — well, at least to my own. . . ."

(p. 161). The medial ellipsis here ("art . . . because") is spaced on the page with a gap on both sides of the three dots and between each dot, matching neither word tightly; the space between "art" and the first dot is narrower than the inter-dot spacing, roughly two-thirds of it, while the space before "because" matches the inter-dot spacing. The terminal ellipsis ("own. . . .") is set with the same bumped first dot as the Geoffrin example above.

Situation 2, a passage with three ellipses in one quotation, each illustrating a different case Trimble has just discussed: the first, at the opening quotation mark, unflagged because the lowercase "if" already tells the reader the beginning was dropped; the second, after "make-up," retaining Bradbury's own comma; the third, after "gusto," a period bumped directly against the word to show his sentence actually ended there before the omission:

> Ray Bradbury, the old science-fiction master, won himself many new fans with his collection of essays entitled *Zen in the Art of Writing*. In one of those essays, he famously remarked that "if I were asked to name the most important items in a writer's make-up, . . . I could only warn him to look to his zest, see to his gusto. . . . If you are writing without zest, without gusto, without love, you are only half a writer."

— Ray Bradbury (pp. 161–162). As set on the page, the first ellipsis has no dots at all (only the lowercased "if" signals the dropped opening); "make-up, . . . I" keeps Bradbury's comma tight against the word and takes an even, word-space gap on both sides of the three dots; "gusto. . . . If" bumps the first of its four dots directly against the *o*, at ordinary period distance, with the remaining three dots at the wider even pitch before "If." The three ellipses in this one passage thus specimen all three cases the chapter distinguishes: dropped opening, medial omission after a retained mark of punctuation, and omission after a complete sentence.

Situation 4, a trailing-off thought using three spaced periods rather than four, with no space before the first:

> "Well, whatever . . ."

As printed, this specimen carries a full word space between "whatever" and the first dot — Trimble's adjacent instruction to skip that space is not what the example itself shows — and the closing quotation mark is bumped tight against the third dot.

(p. 162).

**Terms introduced.**

- *Block quotation* / *extract* — editors' term for an indented, bulky quotation set off from the running text (p. 148).
- *Orphan* — "writers' slang for a quote lacking explicit attribution" (p. 153).
- *Blind quote* — a quote "attributed to some vague, conspicuously unnamed source such as 'a congressman' or 'a senior White House official,'" sometimes needed to protect a source and treated as a lesser fault than an orphan quote (p. 154).
- *Frags* — professionals' term for sentence fragments used in dialogue (p. 155).
- *Grafs* — writers' jargon for paragraphs, including one-word ones (p. 155).
- *Virgule* — the slash (/) used to mark a line break when running verse quotations into text (p. 157).
- *Parenthetical references* — brief source citations given in parentheses immediately after a quotation, in place of a footnote (p. 158).
- *Ellipsis* (pl. *ellipses*) — the omission itself, as distinct from the *ellipsis points* or "dots" (three spaced periods) that mark it (p. 160).

A usage footnote is also worth recording: Trimble defends using *quote* as a noun, against the 1978 *American Heritage Dictionary* Usage Panel's rejection of it as "unacceptable," siding instead with what he calls "the enlightened minority" and with Orwell's "Never use a long word where a short one will do" — while allowing that he will "occasionally use *quotation* where it won't overtax the sentence" (p. 147).

**Connections.** The chapter cross-references Chapter 13 explicitly: the direct-address comma rule for dialogue points the reader back to "my tips on direct address in the section on 'Commas'" (p. 156), and both chapters share the same underlying subject of sentence-level punctuation mechanics. Trimble also reaches back to Chapter 1, tying his advice on previewing a block quote to his opening definition of writing as "the art of creating desired effects" (p. 153).

#### Chapter 15 — Abbreviations

The chapter opens with an epigraph attributed to its original speaker, not to Trimble:

> The most essential gift for a good writer is a built-in, shockproof, shit detector.

— Ernest Hemingway (p. 163)

**Claim.** A short reference list of common abbreviations, most of them confined to footnotes, where they save space; Trimble's overriding caution is to avoid them in body text, where they risk making a writer sound pedantic or lazy and can confuse a reader unfamiliar with academic apparatus.

**Mechanism.** Trimble states the general rule up front: "Don't use them too freely in your body text — you'll look either pedantic or lazy, plus you'll risk confusing anyone unfamiliar with the apparatus of academic writing" (p. 163). For the Latin abbreviations, he supplies the full word or phrase each one derives from, and notes a historical shift in convention: "In times past, writers italicized Latin abbreviations. Current practice favors no italics" (p. 163). The list itself, alphabetical, with Trimble's guidance on each entry:

- **c., *circa***, about, approximately. Used with dates: "He was born c. 1820." Occasionally also abbreviated "ca." (p. 163)
- **cf., *confer***, compare. Use only when asking the reader to compare one thing with another, as in "Cf. Monet's sketch of the same cathedral." Trimble flags a common misuse: "Many novice writers mistakenly use 'cf.' when they mean 'see' as in 'See pp. 8–10 for a full discussion of this point'" (p. 163).
- **chap.**, chapter. (p. 163)
- **cont.**, continued. (p. 163)
- **ed.**, edited by; edition; editor. Plural "eds." (p. 163)
- **e.g., *exempli gratia***, for example. Set off with commas before and after, or a dash before and a comma after: "Conrad uses this device in some of his short stories — e.g., 'The Secret Sharer.'" (p. 163)
- **et al., *et alia***, and others: "This essay was later anthologized in Criticism, ed. Mark Schorer et al." Since "et" is a complete Latin word, no period follows it — Trimble calls the added period "a common mistake." (p. 163)
- **etc., *et cetera***, and so forth. Preceded by a comma. Trimble flags the redundancies "and etc." and "etc., etc." as both wrong. (p. 163)
- **f.**, and the following page or line; plural "ff." Example: "Her notebooks quoted on p. 23 f. and p. 60 ff. address the same issues." A space follows each "p." as well as each numeral. Trimble's guidance goes further than definition: "In the interest of exactness, it's best to avoid this lazy abbreviation; instead, try to specify the final number as well as the first," giving the repair: "Her notebooks quoted on pp. 23–24 and pp. 60–66 address the same issues." (p. 164)
- **ibid., *ibidem***, in the same place. Used in footnotes to specify the same title cited in the preceding note. If the page reference is also identical, "ibid." alone suffices; if it differs, it must be specified: "²Ibid., p. 42." (p. 164)
- **i.e., *id est***, that is. Set off by commas fore and aft, like "e.g.," or by a dash and a comma: "The titles of books, however, must be underlined, i.e., set in italics." (p. 164)
- **l.**, line; plural "ll." Trimble advises against it: since it can be confused with the Arabic numeral 1, "avoid it. You'll spare your reader sentences like 'A less complete statement of Eliot's theme appears in ll. 11–13.'" (p. 164)
- **MS**, manuscript; plural "MSS." A period after "MS" is optional, but current usage dispenses with it. (p. 164)
- **N.B., *nota bene***, note well. Example: "N.B.: this characterization was deleted in the original transcript." (p. 164)
- **op. cit., *opere citato***, in the work cited. Sometimes used in footnoting to avoid repeating a long title: having cited Eugene O'Neill's *Long Day's Journey into Night*, a writer may substitute "op. cit." and give the new page reference: "⁸O'Neill, op. cit., p. 40." Trimble recommends a clearer alternative instead — a shortened form of the title: "⁸O'Neill, *Long Day's Journey*, p. 40." (p. 164)
- **p.**, page; plural "pp." A space follows the period — "p. 21" — just as one follows the spelled-out word: "page 21." (p. 164)
- **pub.**, published by. (p. 164)
- **sic**, thus, so. Used when quoting something containing an error — a misspelling, factual inaccuracy, grammatical lapse, or nonsensical expression — that should not be misattributed to the transcriber: embed "[sic]" in square brackets right after it. Trimble glosses its function directly: "It tells us, 'This is just how I found it.'" Sic normally takes italics, and the square brackets flag it as an editorial interpolation. Example: "Bridgeport is the largest city in Conneticut [sic]." (p. 164)
- **viz., *videlicet***, namely. Set off by commas fore and aft. (p. 164)
- **vol.**, volume; plural "vols." (p. 164)
- **vs.**, *versus*, against. In court decisions, written "v." and italicized, as are the names of the two parties: "*Remy v. Thompkins*." (p. 164)

**Evidence and examples.** Trimble attaches a specimen sentence to most entries rather than defining the abbreviation and stopping. The examples carry the rule, since in every case the point is placement, punctuation, or spacing:

> "He was born c. 1820." (p. 163)

> "Cf. Monet's sketch of the same cathedral." (p. 163)

Paired against the misuse he says novice writers fall into, where "cf." is written for what should be "see": "See pp. 8–10 for a full discussion of this point" (p. 163).

> "Conrad uses this device in some of his short stories—e.g., 'The Secret Sharer.'" (p. 163)

Showing the dash-before, comma-after option he gives as the alternative to commas on both sides.

> "This essay was later anthologized in *Criticism*, ed. Mark Schorer et al." (p. 163)

Attached to the warning that "et" is a complete Latin word and takes no period, which he calls a common mistake.

> "Her notebooks quoted on p. 23 f. and p. 60 ff. address the same issues." (p. 164)

Immediately revised, because he regards the abbreviation as lazy and prefers exact ranges:

> "Her notebooks quoted on pp. 23–24 and pp. 60–66 address the same issues." (p. 164)

This is the chapter's one before/after pair, and the only entry where Trimble advises against using the abbreviation he has just explained.

> "²Ibid., p. 42." (p. 164)

> "The titles of books, however, must be underlined, i.e., set in italics." (p. 164)

> "A less complete statement of Eliot's theme appears in ll. 11–13." (p. 164)

Given as a demonstration of why to avoid the abbreviation for *line*: set in type, "ll." is easily misread as the numeral 11.

> "N.B.: this characterization was deleted in the original transcript." (p. 164)

For *op. cit.*, he works a two-stage example — first the abbreviation as conventionally used, then the shortened title he considers clearer:

> "⁸O'Neill, op. cit., p. 40." (p. 164)

> "⁸O'Neill, *Long Day's Journey*, p. 40." (p. 164)

> "Bridgeport is the largest city in Conneticut [sic]." (p. 164)

The misspelling of *Connecticut* is deliberate and is the example — it shows what *sic* is marking and where it sits relative to the error.

> "Remy v. Thompkins." (p. 164)

Showing the court-decision form, where the abbreviation shortens to "v." and both it and the party names take italics.

Entries with no example sentence are the ones needing none, where the expansion is the whole content: *chap.*, *cont.*, *ed.* (plural *eds.*), *etc.*, *l.c.*, *MS* (plural *MSS.*), *pub.*, *viz.*, *vol.* (plural *vols.*).

**Terms introduced.** None beyond the abbreviations themselves, each glossed with its full Latin or English form as listed above.

**Connections.** The chapter functions as a reference companion to Chapter 13 (Punctuation) and Chapter 14 (Quoting), both concerned with the mechanics of footnoting and citation; it precedes Chapter 16 (Tips on Usage), the next entry in the book's reference-glossary run through "Odds & Ends."

#### Chapter 16 — Tips on Usage

The chapter opens with two epigraphs, both attributed to their original speakers, not to Trimble:

> Good writers are those who keep the language efficient. That is to say, keep it accurate, keep it clear.

— Ezra Pound (p. 165)

> Let us do these things not to satisfy "rules" or to gratify the whims of a pedagogue, but rather to express ourselves clearly, precisely, logically, and directly — and to cultivate the habits of mind that produce that kind of expression.

— Theodore Bernstein (p. 165)

**Claim.** A near-alphabetical usage glossary — individual words and word pairs that writers commonly misuse, misspell, or confuse — with Trimble's ruling on each and, for most entries, an example sentence showing correct use.

**Mechanism.** No single procedure governs the chapter; each entry states its own rule. Where a distinction is commonly muddled (affect/effect, lay/lie, imply/infer), Trimble gives the discriminating test directly in the entry. The chapter closes by naming reference works to consult for usage questions it doesn't cover, and a qualified recommendation of Strunk and White's *The Elements of Style*.

**Evidence and examples.** Entries below follow the book's own order.

- **accommodate.** "Regularly misspelled 'accomodate.' This word gets my vote as the most frequently misspelled word in American English. Just remember: two e's and two m's. Two o's as well!" (p. 165)

- **affect–effect.** Trimble calls these "terrors" because they sound alike, each has both noun and verb forms, and *affect* carries two distinct verb meanings, one of which itself uses the word *effect*. He sorts out the principal meanings:

  > affect (verb) = to influence, have an effect on ("That movie deeply affected me")
  > affect (verb) = to feign ("She affected indifference, but I know she cared")
  > affect (noun) = emotional reaction ("The patient showed no affect")
  > effect (noun) = an influence or impact ("California had a big effect on me")
  > effect (verb) = to bring about ("To effect any change here, we'll need Superman") (pp. 165–166)

  He credits Dr. Mary Knatterud, "an editor friend and research associate in the Surgery Department of the University of Minnesota," with a sentence clarifying four of the five meanings at once: "The effect of the patient's affect was profound: it deeply affected her mother and effected a change in their relationship" (p. 166).

- **all right.** "All right is right; alright is wrong." Trimble predicts alright will eventually gain acceptance as Standard English, and thinks it should — "It's quicker than all right and says the same thing. Nothing would be lost except surplusage" — citing already, altogether, and although as parallel contractions no longer flagged. His practical advice, though: "I suggest you stick with all right. It will spare you being labeled a poor speller" (p. 166).

- **a lot.** Two words, not one. Many readers consider it too "colloquial" for serious prose; Trimble cites usage authority Theodore Bernstein's *The Careful Writer*, which proposes retiring the label "colloquial" in favor of "casual," and Trimble concurs: "If not a green light, he says, give these 'casualisms' ... an orange light. I agree with him." He points to the Updike passage near the end of Chapter 6 as a context where *a lot* fits invisibly and *many* or *much* would not, closing with Quentin Crisp: "Style is being yourself ... but on purpose" (p. 166).

- **and/or.** "To many readers, this is an ugly coinage associated with income-tax prose." Its only virtue is convenience, since the alternative — "X or Y, or both" — is, in Trimble's words, "itself something of an unwieldy monstrosity." His ruling: "Use it sparingly, if at all. And always ask yourself if or alone won't suffice" (p. 166).

- **assure–ensure–insure.** All three mean "to make certain or safe." Rule: "Use assure with persons, ensure with things, and insure when talking about money and guarantees (e.g., life insurance)." Example: "I assured Mary that our spending the extra $20 to insure the package would ensure its safe delivery" (p. 167).

- **between–among.** For just two persons or things, use *between*; for three or more, use *among*. Exception: "if a tight relationship is implied, use between regardless of the number": "The quarrel between Mike, Jim, and Larry still rages"; "The flights between London, Geneva, and Berlin have been canceled" (p. 167).

- **cannot.** "Cannot trumps can not." (p. 167)

- **consensus.** Means "collective opinion" or "general agreement," so it already includes the idea of opinion — making "consensus of opinion" and "the general consensus" redundant. Trimble's fix: "Simply say, for example, 'The consensus is that the bill will pass the Senate'" (p. 167).

- **criterion–criteria.** "You can have but one criterion. But you can have two or more criteria. Moral: Don't use criteria when you're speaking of just one thing." He lists allied Latin-derivative pairs following the same singular–plural pattern, introduced by "Some allied words, all Latin derivatives:" and set in two columns — not a wrong/right comparison but a single five-item list flowed down the left column and continuing in the right, each item itself a singular–plural pair joined by an en dash (p. 167):

  | left column | right column |
  | --- | --- |
  | datum–data | phenomenon–phenomena |
  | medium–media | stratum–strata |
  | memorandum–memoranda | |

  The two-column setting is space-saving for a short list; the meaningful pairing is within each item, between the Latin singular and its plural, matching the criterion–criteria pair that heads the entry.

- **different from–different than.** Since one thing differs *from* another, the default is *different from*, "except where it creates a cumbersome or wordy clause after it, in which case different than is not only acceptable but preferable": "The temptations there are different for adults than for kids" (p. 167).

- **disinterested–uninterested.** "If you are disinterested, you are unbiased or impartial. If you are uninterested, you aren't interested." Trimble calls the difference "so radical that it's worth making a point of remembering which word says which" (p. 167).

- **effect–affect.** Cross-referenced: "See the entry for affect–effect" (p. 167).

- **equally.** Use it alone — "Hugh and Stu are equally talented" — without the redundant "as": don't write "Hugh and Stu are equally as talented" (p. 167).

- **factor.** Trimble is sardonic about its overuse: "Doesn't it have a lovely scientific ring to it? Maybe that's why it appears with such depressing frequency in college writing. We ought to put a 10-year moratorium on this word. We could get along perfectly well with component, ingredient, or element" (p. 167).

- **famous–notorious.** "If people are widely known and acclaimed, they're famous. But if they're widely known because they are disreputable, they're notorious (infamous)" (p. 168).

- **first–firstly.** "In enumerating several items, say first, not firstly," because *first* "is as genuine an adverb as firstly, is a simpler form, and is easier on the ear" — and the same holds for *second, third, fourth*, etc. He allows that numerals themselves sometimes read better than the words, quoting Twain: "To be a writer, one must observe three rules: (1) write, (2) write, and (3) write." He notes the numerals are parenthesized on both sides ("Why do people so often want to use just one parenthesis?"), that a comma plus *and* precedes the final numeral (the serial comma again), and that when enumerated items are lengthy, semicolons should replace the commas for readability (p. 168).

- **imply–infer.** "If someone, such as an author, has implied something, she has hinted it or intimated it instead of saying it outright; if someone else, such as a reader, gets the hint, he has inferred it — that is, deduced the veiled point. It's analogous to the difference between giving and receiving" (p. 168).

- **irregardless.** "If you use the word, you mean regardless." Trimble hedges on calling it a nonword — "for there it is" — but argues it deserves the label since the suffix *-less* makes the prefix *ir-* "plainly redundant." His verdict: "in any case, it's 'nonstandard'" (p. 168).

- **its–it's.** "It's is the contraction of it is, whereas its is the possessive form of it." He flags this as counterintuitive, since possession is normally marked with an apostrophe; *its* instead belongs to a class of possessive pronouns (its, hers, theirs, yours, ours) that dispense with it by convention. His stakes-raising close: "the correct spelling of these two words is, to many readers, one of the bedrock credibility tests for writers" (p. 168).

- **lay–lie.** *Lay* means "to put or set down" — "I'll lay the baby in her crib," "Please lay the book on the table" — and Trimble offers a memory tip: "lay means 'to place,' which is a good memory tip, since lay and place share the same vowel sound." Its past tense is *laid*: "I laid it there only yesterday." *Lay* always takes a direct object, even in alternate senses such as "to arrange" ("I'll lay the table for dinner") or "to produce and deposit" ("Our hen laid three eggs last week"). Its declension: "lay, laid, laid, laying" (pp. 168–169). *Lie*, by contrast, never takes a direct object; it means "to recline" — "note the near rhyme" — as in "Let's lie down for a bit." Further tenses: "They lay down" (past); "They had just lain down when the phone rang" (past perfect); "They were just lying down when the phone rang" (past progressive) (p. 169).

- **like–as.** "If no verb follows your comparison, use like. But if a verb follows it, use as." Compare: "Molly giggles like her sister" versus "Molly's a gem, as I knew she would be." Exception: when *like* means *as if*, a verb may follow it — "Write like you're actually talking to that friend" (p. 169).

- **loathe–loath.** "You may loathe a bad habit, yet still be loath (unwilling) to give it up" (p. 169).

- **loose–lose.** "If a button is loose, you're apt to lose it" (p. 169).

- **neither.** Follow it with *nor*, not *or*: "Neither Bill nor his father ate the turnips with relish." Use *or* only with *either*. Verb number after a neither...nor construction agrees with the noun closest to it, illustrated with two correct sentences:

  > Neither Jack nor Susan was happy about it.
  > Neither Jack nor his roommates were happy about it. (p. 169)

- **none.** Can be either singular or plural, following the number of the noun after it:

  > None of the work was done.
  > None of the guests were here when we arrived. (p. 169)

  If no noun follows, decide by sense whether more than one is meant. To emphasize singleness, substitute *no one* or *not one*: "Not one of his shots was good" (p. 169).

- **only.** "Here's my nominee for the most commonly misplaced modifier in American English. Be sure to put it immediately adjacent to the word it actually modifies." He contrasts two placements:

  > Sam only plays golf on weekends.
  > Sam plays golf only on weekends.

  The first implies Sam does nothing all weekend but play golf; the second implies weekends are the only time he plays (pp. 169–170).

- **oral–verbal.** "If it's spoken, it's oral (e.g., 'oral contract'); if it's in words, it's verbal (e.g., 'verbal contract'). An oral reply is always verbal (unless it's wordless), but a verbal reply isn't always oral. (And do you see how parentheses breed?)" (p. 170)

- **¶ (paragraph mark).** "Although it seems illogical, the proofreader's symbol for a new paragraph is ¶, not a 'P' with an extra leg" (p. 170).

- **precede.** "Precede ('to come before') is, next to accommodate, perhaps the most commonly misspelled word in college prose. It apparently gets confused with proceed and comes out misspelled as preceed" (p. 170).

- **principal–principle.** "If you're referring to a rule or basic truth, say principle." This word functions only as a noun. Its sound-alike, *principal*, denotes "chief" and can be used as either noun or adjective: "the principal of the school," "the principal witness" (p. 170).

- **reason is because.** Redundant, since "because means 'for the reason that.' Say one or the other, not both." Both of the following are equally correct:

  > He double-parked because he was rushed.
  > The reason he double-parked is that he was rushed. (p. 170)

- **revert–regress.** Both mean "go back," so "revert back" and "regress back" are redundancies. "Simply say, for example, 'He reverted to his old ways'" (p. 170).

- **shall–will.** Earlier generations were taught a strict distinction between these words but forgot it, "apparently it was not so vital after all," and the words are now interchangeable. *Shall* "sounds slightly fussier and more bookish to the average ear," so a conversational style favors *will* — except in a first-person interrogative: "Shall we dance?" "Shall I pick her up at the station or will you?" (p. 170)

- **so.** Many people treat *so* as an intensifier synonymous with *very* ("This coffee is so hot"), but "it isn't, however, at least not in grown-up prose." Readers unconsciously expect a *that* clause to follow: "This coffee is so hot that I can't drink it" (p. 170).

- **supposed to.** The final *d* is hard to hear in conversation, "but it's there — or should be — since it's the past participle of the verb suppose." Say "I was supposed to leave today," not "I was suppose to leave today" (p. 171).

- **there is–there are.** Both are "empty phrases and should be used sparingly," since recasting a sentence to eliminate them usually makes it "more vivid, concrete, and direct." Trimble adds a caveat: "There are many exceptions, though, and this is one of them" (p. 171).

- **though.** When a sentence ends with *though*, set it off with a comma: "That was not the first time, though." Trimble likens its function to a sentence-final *however*, and offers a diagnostic: read the unpunctuated version aloud — "That was not the first time though" — and listen for "the big drop in pitch as you arrive at though"; that drop is "your proof that the word is parenthetic — and thus needs setting off" (p. 171).

- **throughout.** "Includes the idea of 'entire,' whereas through does not." Say either "Throughout the story..." or "Through the entire story...." (p. 171)

- **thus.** "Thus is an adverb." Many writers wrongly attach the regular adverbial suffix *-ly* to it: "Thusly, however, is an illiteracy. It's ugly, too" (p. 171).

- **unique.** An absolute condition, "like perfection" — a thing either is or is not the only one of its kind, so it cannot logically take degree: "You can't say, for example, 'rather unique' or 'the most unique' or 'very unique.'" When writers misuse it this way they usually mean "uncommon," "unusual," or "rare": "'A rather unique invitation,' for example, should read 'a rather unusual invitation'" (p. 171).

- **used to.** Another silent-*d* case, the past participle of *use* ("to be accustomed to"). Say "I used to work there," not "I use to work there" (p. 171).

- **whether.** Use it alone; don't tack on the redundant "or not," as in "He doesn't know whether or not to go." The "or not" is needed only to convey the sense of *regardless of whether*: "We're going biking today whether it rains or not" (p. 171).

The chapter closes with a further epigraph, attributed to its original speaker:

> I would be most content if my children grew up to be the kind of people who think decorating consists mostly of building enough bookshelves.

— Anna Quindlen (p. 172)

Trimble frames the glossary above as partial — "just a handful of the many usage questions that arise in our writing lives" — and recommends buying at least two of seven reference texts, "which you'll find both authoritative and indispensable" (p. 172):

1. Theodore Bernstein, *The Careful Writer: A Modern Guide to English Usage* (New York: Atheneum, 1965)
2. H. W. Fowler, *A Dictionary of Modern English Usage: The Classic First Edition*, ed. David Crystal (New York: Oxford U.P., 2009)
3. Bryan Garner, *Garner's Modern American Usage*, 3rd ed. (New York: Oxford U.P., 2009)
4. Merriam-Webster, *Merriam-Webster's Dictionary of English Usage* (Springfield, MA: Merriam-Webster, Inc., 1994)
5. Marjorie Skillin, ed., *Words into Type*, 3rd ed. (Englewood Cliffs: Prentice-Hall, 1974)
6. Andrea Sutliffe, ed., *The New York Public Library Writer's Guide to Style and Usage* (New York: HarperCollins, 1994)
7. University of Chicago Press, *The Chicago Manual of Style*, 16th ed. (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2010)

He adds a final, qualified recommendation for Strunk and White's *The Elements of Style*: "At just 105 pages, it can't pretend to be comprehensive, and it's often dated, over-prescriptive, and misinformed, yet its succinctness and general utility have made it a bible to legions of writers," who "affectionately refer to it as 'Strunk & White'" — "perhaps the most popular style manual in history." But he qualifies the recommendation further: "it turns out to be far more flawed than most readers would imagine." He cites, on the book's 50th anniversary in April 2009, a reassessment in *The Chronicle of Higher Education* by Professor Geoffrey Pullum, "Head of Linguistics and English Language at the University of Edinburgh and co-author of *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*" (Cambridge U.P., 2002), titled "50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice," which Trimble calls "a riveting read" and "freely available online," recommending it "to anyone inclined to deify Strunk & White." His conclusion: "You won't want to throw out your copy of their manual, but you will want to read it more critically — and you'll be a better writer for doing so" (p. 172, citing chronicle.com/article/50-Years-of-Stupid-Grammar/25497).

**Terms introduced.** None beyond the usage entries themselves, each glossed in place above.

**Connections.** Continues the reference-glossary run begun in Chapter 15 (Abbreviations), within the book's closing "Odds & Ends" part. Several entries cross-reference earlier chapters directly: *a lot* points back to the Updike passage near the end of Chapter 6; *e.g.* and comma-setoff conventions echo the comma rules of Chapter 13 (Punctuation); the discussion of Strunk and White closes out the book's running engagement with other usage and style authorities cited throughout.

#### Chapter 17 — Epilogue

The chapter opens with two epigraphs, both attributed to their original speakers, not to Trimble:

> A writer's problem does not change. He himself changes and the world he lives in changes but his problem remains the same. It is always how to write truly and having found what is true, to project it in such a way that it becomes part of the experience of the person who reads it.

— Ernest Hemingway (p. 173)

> A writer provides his reader with a role model of both the courage to experience without dimming or repressing this or that facet of self and the courage to share this experiencing with others.

— Sidney Jourard (p. 173)

**Claim.** Closing the book, Trimble likens himself to a pilot who bails out just as the flight gets rough, then restates his hope for the book as a whole: that it has given the reader real insight into how skilled writers think, and the motivation to write that way personally. He frames writing well as hard work that can also be pleasant work, because it is a form of creative challenge, self-knowledge, and connection with others.

**Mechanism.** The epilogue is not instructional; it states no procedure, rule, or test. It closes the book through a single extended image rather than a checklist.

**Evidence and examples.** Trimble develops one anecdote as his closing figure:

> Some time ago I ran across a cartoon depicting a scene aboard a commercial airliner. As the startled passengers look on, the whole flight crew, outfitted in parachutes, strolls down the aisle towards the rear escape hatch. Leading them out is the pilot, his hands nonchalantly tucked in his pockets and a casual whistle on his guiltily upturned face.
>
> I confess I see myself in that pilot. Here I am, cravenly parachuting out of harm's way on the wings of a final paragraph, and there you are, abandoned to your own devices just when the trip has started getting bumpy. I hope, though, that this book, brief as it is, has given you new insight into how skilled writers think, plus the itch to go out and write like them yourself. Writing well is hard work, but it can be pleasant hard work if we view it for what it really is — a challenge to our creativity, an opportunity to know our own mind, and a chance to share our thoughts and feelings with others. That's certainly what this book has meant for me. Thanks for listening. (p. 173)

The cartoon of the parachuting flight crew stands for Trimble himself ending the book at the point the reader must go on writing alone; the passage then turns from that image directly to what he hopes the book accomplished and what writing well can feel like once the difficulty is accepted as part of the work.

**Terms introduced.** None.

**Connections.** Closes the book as a whole rather than any single preceding chapter, restating in miniature the aims stated at the outset in "A Word About These 'Conversations'" — demystifying how skilled writers think and making the reader want to write like them.

## Cross-cutting synthesis

### The reader in the room

Chapter 1 states the book's axiom, and every later chapter is an application of it. The novice writes badly not from ignorance of rules but from an unexamined egocentrism — the page never talks back, so the novice never learns that anyone is on the other side of it. The veteran has internalized a reader and shapes each sentence around that reader's likely reactions. Trimble's term is **other-oriented**, and he treats the shift toward it as the single teachable thing that separates the two.

The rest of the book redistributes this one idea across scales. Chapter 3's opener has to win a reader who will quit in three or four sentences. Chapter 4's signposting and bridge sentences exist because a reader cannot see the writer's outline. Chapter 5's closer matters because it is what the reader retains. Chapter 6's compression is justified by the reader's time, chapter 7's conversational register by the reader's patience with a false voice, chapter 12's proofreading by the reader's trust.

The framing is consistently one of courtesy rather than of correctness. A rule earns its place by serving a reader, which is what makes chapter 8's demolition of the "Seven Nevers" consistent with chapter 13's insistence on punctuation rules rather than a contradiction of it. A convention that helps a reader parse a sentence is real; a taboo that only signals propriety is not.

### The ear as the instrument

Trimble's recurring test is not a rule but a physical act: read it aloud. It appears as the operative procedure in chapters 2, 7, 13 and 16, and rhythm is discussed as a working concern in six chapters.

Chapter 13 makes the strongest version of the claim, teaching each punctuation mark as something a writer can hear before knowing the rule for it — the reader's voice pausing and dropping tells you where the mark goes, and the rule is a description of what the ear already does. Chapter 16 applies the same test to usage. Chapter 7 makes it a diagnostic for the false formal voice: a sentence you would not say aloud is a sentence you have written for the wrong reason.

This is why so much of the book is unteachable as a list. The tests it gives are perceptual — *does this sound like a person talking* — rather than propositional.

### Conversation as the model, and the book as its own demonstration

The subtitle is *Conversations on the Art of Writing*, and the conversational register is discussed as subject matter in thirteen of the seventeen chapters. Chapter 7 gives it a name, **General English** — a middle style between the stiff formal register and slang — and argues it is the register most expository prose should be written in.

The book then writes itself in that register, and this is not incidental. Every permission chapter 8 grants — begin a sentence with *But*, use contractions, address the reader as *you*, write in the first person, end on a preposition, split an infinitive, write a one-sentence paragraph — is visible in Trimble's own prose throughout the other sixteen chapters. Chapter 8's argument is therefore partly ostensive: the reader has already been reading the permitted constructions for seven chapters without objecting.

Chapter 10 pushes the same point into a stronger claim. Form is a creative resource rather than a container to fill, and a writer who shapes it deliberately becomes a **dramatist of ideas** rather than a chronicler. Pleasure is named as the effect prose aims for, in chapters 4, 7 and 10 alike.

### Directness, and the front door

Chapter 3 gives the pairing the book returns to: the **front-door approach** commits to the subject immediately, and the **back-door approach** stalls, generalizes, or summarizes before saying anything. Chapter 3's claim that "the quality of an opener tends to forecast what follows" makes this diagnostic rather than merely stylistic — a back-door opening usually indicates a writer who has not yet decided what the piece argues.

The **"I contend that—" test** operationalizes it: if the sentence cannot follow that phrase, it is not yet a thesis. Chapter 4 carries the same standard into the body with one main contention per paragraph, and chapter 5 into the ending, which must conclude rather than recap.

### Rules, superstitions, and the difference

The book takes three distinct attitudes toward received rules, and keeping them apart is what makes it coherent:

- **Superstitions** (ch. 8) — the Seven Nevers, which Trimble traces to a class of "literary prudes" he satirizes as TOTELS, The One True English Language Sect. Each is shown violated routinely by writers he admires. His verdict on each is permissive, with his own practice stated.
- **Conventions** (ch. 13, 14, 15) — punctuation, quotation mechanics, abbreviation. These are real, they are taught rule by rule with stated exceptions, and the justification is always that a reader needs them to parse the sentence.
- **Usage judgments** (ch. 16) — the near-alphabetical glossary, where Trimble rules on individual words and gives an example.

The distinction is the reader again. A rule that changes what a sentence means to someone reading it is binding; a rule that only marks the writer as properly brought up is not.

### Teaching by specimen

Trimble almost never states a principle without immediately showing it operating on a real passage, and the specimens come overwhelmingly from working prose rather than from constructed examples — Mencken, Hemingway, Orwell, journalism, letters, and his own students' sentences.

Chapter 9 is this method at full length and undiluted: one Mencken essay, "Literature and the Schoolma'm" from *Prejudices, Fifth Series* (1926), read sentence by sentence for nearly thirteen pages. Trimble chooses it because it mixes "truths, half-truths, and fallacies" with real stylistic brilliance, which forces a reader to evaluate the argument and the style separately instead of letting the prose carry the reasoning. The chapter is a demonstration that admiration and assent are different responses.

Chapter 10's closing analysis of a student essay works the same way from the opposite direction — a piece that succeeds, with an enumerated account of what makes it succeed.

### Writing as labor

The book discusses effort directly, against the grain of its own easy voice. Difficulty is discussed in nine chapters, and chapter 11 makes the point by giving over its single page entirely to Hemingway, in a *Paris Review* exchange about how much rewriting he did.

Placing that exchange as a standalone chapter rather than as an epigraph is itself an argument. The chapter has no Trimble in it at all; the position is that on this subject a working novelist's answer settles the matter and commentary would weaken it.

### Where the book contradicts itself

On printed page 161 the Geoffrin ellipsis example is set differently from what the adjacent instruction prescribes — the instruction calls for a space before the first of four dots, and the example as printed does not have one. Whether this is a typesetting slip or a deliberate exception, the text does not say.

## Scope and limitations

**What the book covers.** Expository and persuasive prose at essay length — the thinking that precedes it, the shape of the piece, sentence-level style, and the mechanical conventions of punctuation, quotation and usage. The unit of work throughout is the essay with a thesis.

**What it explicitly declines to cover.** The front matter states the boundary: the book is not a textbook, and it deliberately leaves out the apparatus that textbooks on writing conventionally carry. It does not teach grammar from the ground up, does not teach research methodology, and treats documentation only as far as quoting requires. Fiction and poetry are quoted as specimens but their techniques are not taught.

**What the advice assumes about its reader.** A student writer, in an American university, writing graded essays for an instructor. The examples come from Trimble's own teaching — the Mencken chapter reports nearly three decades of use in an Advanced Expository Writing seminar at the University of Texas at Austin — and the anxieties the book addresses are a student's: fear of sounding unserious, the pull toward a falsely formal register, uncertainty about whether a rule is real. A professional writer would recognize the craft advice while finding the framing pitched below them.

**Its era, and its layers of era.** The third edition is dated 2011, but the book first appeared in 1975 and the revisions have not displaced its foundations. The specimens skew heavily to mid-twentieth-century American journalism and letters, and the central worked example is an essay from 1926. Digital writing is essentially absent — no email, no web writing, no screen reading, no collaborative drafting, and none of the citation apparatus that software now handles. A reader looking for guidance on writing for a screen will not find it addressed.

**The Mencken chapter carries a period.** Trimble selects the essay because it mixes "truths, half-truths, and fallacies," and the chapter's instructional value depends on the reader disagreeing with parts of it. Mencken's views and idiom are of the 1920s. The chapter is built to be argued with rather than absorbed, which is worth knowing before reading it as a model.

**The nature of its evidence.** Craft experience and connoisseurship, not empirical study of readers. Claims about what a reader will notice, tire of, or quit reading come from Trimble's teaching and his own reading, and the specimens are chosen to illustrate the book's positions rather than drawn as a representative sample. The strongest form of evidence the book offers is the demonstration — the passage in front of you, working or failing.

**Where the book is prescriptive without saying so.** Chapter 7's "General English" is presented as a middle style available to any writer, but it is a specific register with a specific ancestry in American literary journalism. Chapter 8's permissions are argued from the practice of writers Trimble admires, which is an appeal to a canon rather than to a rule. Both are positions rather than neutral instruction, and the book is more openly opinionated here than elsewhere.

**Where the book overlaps itself.** Chapters 6 and 7 both treat sentence-level economy; chapters 3, 4 and 5 all apply the same thesis discipline at different positions in a piece; chapters 13, 14 and 16 all rule on conventions and occasionally on the same construction from different angles. The chapter treatments above cross-reference these rather than repeating them.

## Topic mapping

Sections touched, stated neutrally:

- Expository and persuasive writing craft, at essay length
- The writer–reader relationship; audience awareness as a teachable stance
- Composing process, invention, and getting a first draft down
- Essay architecture — openings, development, closings
- Thesis formulation and testing
- Paragraph construction, signposting, transitions
- Diction, economy, active and passive constructions, figurative language
- Register and voice; formal versus conversational style
- Received usage prohibitions and their status
- Critical reading and rhetorical analysis of a specimen text
- Form as rhetorical resource; prose that aims at pleasure
- Revision and proofreading
- Punctuation, mark by mark
- Quotation mechanics, block quotes, dialogue, ellipses, editorial insertion
- Abbreviations and scholarly apparatus
- Usage glossary
