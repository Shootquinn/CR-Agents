# Prose Style Reference — Mechanics

Companion lookup file to `style.md`. Source: John R. Trimble, *Writing with Style: Conversations on the Art of Writing*, 3rd ed. (Pearson, 2011), Part 2. American conventions throughout; where British practice differs, it is flagged.

---

## 1. Punctuation

### Colon

Read the colon as **that is** or **namely**: the first clause is a prelude, the second explains, particularizes, or completes it.

> Girls have an unfair advantage over men: if they can't get what they want by playing smart, they can get it by playing dumb.

Before a list or series it reads as **the following** / **as follows**.

> The best methods of combatting organized crime are: 1. Telling the criminals you are not at home. 2. Calling the police . . . 3. Wiretapping.

Some authorities dislike a colon placed directly after a verb (as in "are:" above), preferring it after a complete statement or after *as follows* / *the following*. Either is defensible.

In most uses the colon is a slightly more formal dash:

> Fields' very appearance evoked shouts of laughter from an audience: the manorial air that was so obviously false, the too benign smile, the larcenous eye.

> The vision behind this book is simple and horrifying: it is the vision of the humanely educated Nazi.

Further rules:

- Use a colon to introduce a quotation that runs longer than one sentence, or that illustrates the statement just made.
- Capitalize after a colon or not, as you prefer — capitalizing signals that a full clause is coming. Both are correct: *Starbucks got it right: Find a good location, and customers will find you.* / *Starbucks got it right: find a good location . . .*
- Use no more than one colon per sentence, and don't let a semicolon follow a colon unless the series itself needs semicolons.

### Comma

**Governing test: use a comma where there is a light pause AND a drop in pitch.** Read the sentence aloud in your best radio voice; where you pause to stay intelligible and your pitch drops, set a comma. The comma's primary purpose is to prevent misreading.

> Taking the long subway ride home to Queens, Tom reviewed his hectic day.

Without the comma, the reader briefly parses "Queens Tom."

**Compound sentences.** Put a comma before the conjunction joining two independent clauses. Without it, readers collide:

> The Bible can teach you to love God, and Dostoevsky can teach you to write great novels.

> She seems to be focused on money, and Victor's indecisiveness about getting a new career guarantees she won't see more money from him anytime soon.

**Transitional words** (*however, therefore, then, consequently, besides, indeed, likewise, otherwise, nevertheless*) take a comma on both sides — or a semicolon before and comma after when they fall between complete clauses. One comma is not enough.

> You'll agree, however, that Wagner's music is better than it sounds.

> She is old enough to see her parents' faults; however, she is not old enough to forgive them.

**Direct address.** Set off a name, or a substitute for one (*sir, ma'am, coach, Sis, Dad, buddy*), with commas. The difference is the whole meaning:

> I hear Charlie. / I hear, Charlie.
>
> Remember Nancy. / Remember, Nancy.
>
> Corey isn't that screwed up? / Corey, isn't that screwed up?

Exception: two words working in tandem, where the pitch rises rather than drops — *Hey Bobby, got a spare jersey?* Compare *Hey, man, got any spare change?*

**Serial comma.** Insert the comma before the final *and* as a matter of course; it removes ambiguity that would otherwise force a rereading. (Journalists often drop it; most usage authorities keep it.)

> . . . a man obsessed with flying saucers, an ex-cop with a craving for doughnuts and assorted females — all of them coexisting in surprising harmony.

**Comma splice** — two independent clauses joined by a comma alone. Four repairs:

- *Bob wanted to leave early, for he had a date that night.* (add a conjunction)
- *Bob wanted to leave early; he had a date that night.* (semicolon)
- *Bob wanted to leave early. He had a date that night.* (two sentences)
- *Since he had a date that night, Bob wanted to leave early.* (recast)

**No comma after a dash.** The dash absorbs the comma's work.

### Dash

The most dramatic and spirited of the separator marks. Five jobs it does well:

1. **Interruption or break in thought.** *Life without romance — well, you might as well be in prison or a slug under the earth.*
2. **Conversational colon or light bridge.** *That's the worst of facts — they do cramp a fellow's style.*
3. **Isolating a concluding phrase for emphasis or comedy.** *Fashion is illusion, shimmer, magic, mirage — and money: $62.3 billion a year.* / *I could never learn to like her — except on a raft at sea with no other provisions in sight.*
4. **Parenthetical explanation, qualification, or amplification.** *Easter weekend arrived, and our cherry trees — about thirty of them — blossomed in unison.*
5. **Gathering up a series of preceding ideas.** *The art of the surprise witness, the withering cross-examination, the sudden objection phrased in arcane formulas — all seem to bespeak a profession based on elaborate training.*

Cautions:

- Distinguish the **single dash** (setting off matter at a sentence's end) from the **double dash** (a pair wedging matter in). Never mix both kinds in one sentence.
- Rest the dash for three or more sentences after each use, or it stops registering.
- **Double-dash test:** cover the words inside the pair. If what remains is coherent and grammatical, you're safe. *Our purple martins arrived today — a sight we'd been anticipating all winter — I couldn't be happier* collapses to a run-on; fix by adding a conjunction after the second dash.
- No comma after a dash, even where the syntax seems to want one.
- Set dashes **attached** (no surrounding space) — now roughly the 95% convention — or **open** (spaces both sides). Either is acceptable if you are consistent.
- Never substitute a hyphen for a dash.

**The horizontal marks, by length:**

| Mark | Width | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hyphen | shortest | compounds, prefixes, syllable breaks: *self-interest*, *pre-engineered*, *hy-phen-a-tion* |
| En dash | width of N | "to" or "through": *1998–1999*, *April–May 2010*, *pages 28–32*, *Matthew 3:5–6*, *Chicago–New York flight*, *8:00 a.m.–5:00 p.m.* |
| Em dash (1-em) | width of M | the ordinary dash of prose |
| 2-em dash | double | missing letters: *His source was Col. B——* |
| 3-em dash | triple | a repeated author's name in a bibliography |

### Exclamation point

Generally ill-advised: it is cheapened by cuteness and melodrama, readers prefer understated humor, a real witticism needs no applause sign, and omitting the mark protects you — a dry joke that lands earns credit, and one that misfires leaves no evidence you were joking. Ration it hard (one standing rule allows a writer three in a lifetime; another allows two or three per 100,000 words).

Required after true exclamations and commands: *Whew! Go! How lovely!*

Don't over-correct into inhibition — heightened self-consciousness about usage is the enemy of vigor.

### Hyphen (compound modifiers)

A phrase acting as an adjective needs hyphens once it sits **before** the noun, so the reader sees it as one unit.

**Test: hyphenate the modifiers if you can't (1) reverse their order or (2) remove one of them without damaging the sense.**

*a hot, muggy night* passes both (reversible; either word droppable) — no hyphen. *a three-base hit* fails both — hyphenate.

| Wrong | Right |
| --- | --- |
| iced over lake | iced-over lake |
| ear jarring sounds | ear-jarring sounds |
| tear stained page | tear-stained page |
| multiple choice tests | multiple-choice tests |
| last ditch stand | last-ditch stand |
| one track mind | one-track mind |
| dead end job | dead-end job |
| 22 year old woman | 22-year-old woman |
| small town boy | small-town boy |
| stickler for the rules attitude | stickler-for-the-rules attitude |
| one day at a time plan | one-day-at-a-time plan |
| fast food chain operation | fast-food-chain operation |
| do it yourself mentality | do-it-yourself mentality |
| trial and error analysis | trial-and-error analysis |
| three steps to success model | three-steps-to-success model |

Ambiguity a missing hyphen leaves unresolved: *long dead fish*, *light red shirt*, *rare book store*, *first floor plan*, *little hands on writing experience*, *20 foot long rods of plutonium*, *last minute objections*.

Exceptions:

- No hyphen after an *-ly* adverb: *widely known speaker*, *firmly held opinions*, *sharply reduced prices*.
- No hyphen when the modifier follows its noun: *artists are well known* vs. *well-known artists*; *the pages were tear stained* vs. *the tear-stained pages*.

Also: *self-* always takes a hyphen (*self-confidence, self-esteem, self-taught*). Use **suspension hyphens** when compounds share a base: *The high- and low-season rates differ by as much as $80 a day.*

### Parentheses

The most intrusive of the three separator marks (parentheses, commas, dashes) — they halt the forward march of the prose, make readers feel whispered at, and breed. Use them uncomfortably and keep them brief. The same aside can usually take any of the three:

> That second trip to Italy (the one in '95) cost me a lost heart, but I'm not complaining.
>
> That second trip to Italy, the one in '95, cost me a lost heart, but I'm not complaining.
>
> That second trip to Italy — the one in '95 — cost me a lost heart, but I'm not complaining.

- **Commas:** a parenthetical insert belongs to the preceding clause, so any comma goes *after* the closing parenthesis, never before the opening one.
- **Question marks and exclamation points:** before the opening parenthesis only if they end a quotation there; otherwise after the closing one. *"Was that Billy who called?" (119), she asked.* / *Did the fisherman actually yell, "Oh, come on, bite!" (221)?*
- **Periods:** inside the closing parenthesis when the remark stands as its own sentence (This sentence is an example.) — outside when the remark stays attached to the preceding clause (like this).

### Semicolon

An automatic coupling device: it joins two or more related complete sentences into one complex thought, doing in print what a half-pause does in speech. No connective word is required.

> A beauty is a woman you notice; a charmer is one who notices you.

**Test: if you can replace the semicolon with a period, you're fine. If not, try a comma or a dash.**

Four occasions where it earns its place:

1. Presenting two or more sentences as parts of a single thought. *The peach was once a bitter almond; cauliflower is nothing but cabbage with a college education.*
2. Giving the reader a rest stop inside a sentence too long to read straight through.
3. Linking parallel-structured sentences to sharpen a contrast or a likeness. *In Detroit, . . . the voters cast their ballots along racial lines to elect the city's first black mayor; in New York, the local Democratic organization overwhelmed three lackluster rivals . . .*
4. Acting as a super-comma to separate long series items, especially when the items already contain commas.

Semicolons do not by themselves make prose heavy or formal. That impression comes from the habits they usually keep company with — pretentious jargon, galumphing sentences, abstractness, impersonality, passive verbs, humorlessness.

## 2. Quoting

### Punctuation introducing a quotation

- **Comma** for a short quote introduced by *she said*, *he replied*, *So-and-so observed*. — *Twain observed, "Only presidents, editors, and people with tapeworms have the right to use the editorial 'we.'"*
- **Colon** when (1) you have made a statement and now supply a quote to prove or illustrate it, (2) the quote exceeds one sentence, or (3) the quote is indented as a block. — *James Thurber remarked: "Word has somehow got around that the split infinitive is always wrong. This is of a piece with the outworn notion that it is always wrong to strike a lady."*
- **No punctuation** when the quoted words are woven into your own syntax. — *Mencken said that Comrades "will lie embalmed in my memory as a composition unearthly and unique."*
- **Never put a comma after a lead-in ending in *that*.** Wrong: *Updike said that, "Writing and rewriting are . . ."* Right: *Updike said that "Writing and rewriting are . . ."*

### Punctuation at the end of a quotation

**Commas and periods always go INSIDE the closing quotation marks**, even when they were not part of the original. (British practice is the reverse; American convention holds regardless, and breaking it reads as unschooled.)

**Colons and semicolons go OUTSIDE. So do question marks and exclamation points — unless they belonged to the quotation.**

> Did I write "rain"? I meant "reign." No, no, not "rein," and certainly not "rain."
>
> Oscar Wilde remarked, "Truth is never pure, and rarely simple"; half-truths, however, suffer neither inconvenience.
>
> What does he mean by "a spy in the enemy's country"?

Do not split the difference by setting the mark directly beneath the quotation mark.

### Quotation marks and italics for words as words

Set off a word or phrase you are isolating, or using in a special, technical, humorous, or ironic sense.

> A more recent instance of euphemism was our calling the war in Vietnam a "conflict."
>
> Many people apparently think that *imply* and *infer* are synonyms.
>
> Is this what they call "collateral damage"?

The verbs *say*, *call*, *refer to as* are usually followed by quoted words; so are *the word* and *the words*. For emphasis or for several words at once, prefer italics (typographically equivalent to underlining).

**Never put a cliché in quotation marks.** It highlights the trite phrase and tells the reader you knew better and used it anyway. Not: *Andy was a "chip off the old block."*

### Block quotations (extracts)

- Indent prose quotes running more than four lines; indent verse quotes running more than two, since the form has to be visible.
- Single-space and indent from the left margin (optionally the right too). Omit quotation marks — the indentation already marks it as quoted.
- Introduce with a colon, or weave the lead-in directly into the quote so it begins mid-sentence: *The problem, Senator Fulbright pointed out, is that / power tends to confuse itself with virtue . . .*

Three mistakes to avoid:

1. **Ending the lead-in with a period.** The block then hangs unattached, and the reader cannot tell whether it illustrates what came before or what comes after.
2. **Sandwiching the block into the middle of your own sentence.** It forces a rereading of the whole sentence, and it is usually ungrammatical, since the quote's own period terminates your sentence early.
3. **Leading in mechanically** ("McMurtry wrote:") and saving all commentary for afterward. Sell the quote in the lead-in: give the reader the context and your slant before the plunge, or the block gets skipped.

### Attribution

An **orphan** is a quote with no explicit attribution. Never leave one. Four repairs of *One congressman was openly scornful. "Anyone who believes him now is a fool."*:

> "Anyone who believes him now," said one congressman, "is a fool."
>
> One congressman was openly scornful: "Anyone who believes him now is a fool."
>
> Scoffed one congressman, "Anyone who believes him now is a fool."
>
> "Anyone who believes him now is a fool," said one congressman.

The colon version ties quote to source syntactically; the others need an explicit speech tag.

A **blind quote** — attributed to a vague, unnamed source ("a senior White House official") — is a lesser fault, sometimes necessary to protect a source. Orphans are never acceptable.

### Dialogue

Rule #1 is **authenticity before all else**; dialogue is licensed to flout standard written English.

- Use freely: sentence fragments, one-word paragraphs, colloquialisms (*gonna*, *wouldja*, *dunno*), contractions including offbeat ones, monosyllables (*but* over *however*), repetition, interruptions, internal pauses, trailing-off thoughts, and bare questions (*Why? Really?*).
- Avoid semicolons and long paragraphs. Nobody talks in semicolons.
- Avoid illogical speech-tag verbs — a verb works only if it can describe both a facial expression and a way of talking (*sneer* qualifies). Not: *"Aw, you're not that old," she grinned.* Safer: *"I am too," he said, laughing.* or *He laughed. "I am too."*
- Let the reader supply 90% of the exclamation points and italics.
- Set off names in direct address with commas even where speech slurs the pause.
- Mark an interruption with a dash and no period: *Bob: "But if you—"*
- Use elliptical constructions and comma splices that mirror real speech: *"He got in a elevator, went upstairs." / "So what now? Go home, maybe sleep some."*

**Interjection lexicon:** "Mm-hmm" (mumbled assent) · "Hm-hmm" (mumbled denial) · "Uh-huh" (yes) · "Uh-unh" (no) · "Uh" (groping; follow with comma or three dots) · "Huh?" (say that again) · "huh?" (sentence-final: right?) · "Hmm?" (adult version of "say that again") · "Uh-oh" (sudden alarm) · "Yeah" (assent) · "Yea" (cheering: *Yea, team*) · "OK" (preferred over *okay* and *O.K.*).

### Verse run into text

Two lines or less run into your text in quotation marks, with a slash (**virgule**) marking the line break, a space on each side. Capitalize new lines unless the poet did not.

> "If to her share some Female Errors fall, / Look on her Face, and you'll forget 'em all" (*The Rape of the Lock*, ll. 17–18).

### References

Give a page (or line) reference for every quotation.

- **Parenthetical citation** directly after the quote is now preferred over footnotes, especially when most quotes come from one work. Use the bare number where unambiguous; add *p.* / *pp.* / *l.* / *ll.* only where confusion is possible.
- Several authors: last name, space, page — *(Davis 27)*, no comma.
- Several works by one author: add a short title — *(Callard, Movies 103)*.
- One author quoted repeatedly and consecutively: the page number alone until a different author appears.
- Full footnote form: *¹Northrop Frye,* Anatomy of Criticism: Four Essays *(Princeton, N.J.: Princeton Univ. Press, 1957), p. 31.* Subsequent: *Frye 32*.
- Plays: Shakespeare takes *(II.ii.553)*, *(II.2.553)*, or *(2.2.553)* — the last number is always the line, not the page. Other plays usually take Act and page: *(III, 88)*. Capitalize *Act* when naming a specific one; leave *scene* lowercase.
- End the piece with a "Works Cited" list in standard bibliography form.

**Punctuating parenthetical references:**

1. The reference completes the clause, so the clause's final punctuation follows the reference, not the quote.
2. Cut any comma or period that ended the original quote — it is now functionless.
3. Keep a question mark or exclamation point that ended the quote, so it still reads intelligibly.

> . . . he managed to keep his presence "like a robe pontifical, / Ne'er seen but wonder'd at" (3.2.56–57), we hear telling echoes of Prince Hal's earlier words, "Being wanted, he may be more wonder'd at" (1.2.207).
>
> "Oh what a rogue and peasant slave am I!" (2.2.553), Hamlet exclaims.

### Ellipses

Ellipsis points — three spaced periods — mark text you have omitted. Use them sparingly; scattered dots distract and invite suspicion about what is hidden.

- A quoted phrase or clause needs no dots; it is self-evidently partial. Insert them wherever their absence would mislead.
- **At the start of a quote,** drop the dots and build a lead-in that flows grammatically into the quotation; the lowercase opening letter signals the truncation. Test: delete the quotation marks mentally and you should be left with a grammatical sentence. — *Jacques Barzun summed up the importance of clarity with his remark that "a written exercise is designed to be read; it is not supposed to be a challenge to clairvoyance."*
- **At the end of a sentence,** you still owe a period: three dots plus the true period, four in all. — *"She spoke so pleasantly about things she did not know, that no one wished she knew more of them. . . ."*
- **Omitting a middle chunk after a complete sentence,** bump the first dot hard against the last word to show the sentence really ended there, then space three more. — *". . . see to his gusto. . . . If you are writing without zest . . ."*
- **Omitting whole lines of poetry,** type a full line of spaced periods as wide as the poem, and let the line-number citation tell readers how many lines went.
- **A thought trailing off** takes three dots, not four. — *"Well, whatever . . ."*

### Editorial insertions

Use **square brackets**, never parentheses, for anything you insert into a quotation — parentheses leave the reader unable to tell your words from the author's.

> George Eliot says of Dorothea, "she felt that she enjoyed it [horseback-riding] in a pagan sensuous way."

## 3. Abbreviations

Keep these out of body text — they read as pedantic or lazy and lose readers unfamiliar with academic apparatus. They belong in notes and citations, where they save space. Do not italicize Latin abbreviations; that convention has lapsed.

| Abbreviation | Full form | Meaning and use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **c.** (also *ca.*) | *circa* | about, approximately; with dates — *He was born c. 1820.* |
| **cf.** | *confer* | compare — *Cf. Monet's sketch of the same cathedral.* Not a substitute for *see*; if you mean "see pp. 8–10," write that. |
| **chap.** | chapter | |
| **cont.** | continued | |
| **ed.** (pl. *eds.*) | edited by; edition; editor | |
| **e.g.** | *exempli gratia* | for example; set off by commas both sides, or a dash before and comma after — *. . . in some of his short stories — e.g., "The Secret Sharer."* |
| **et al.** | *et alia* | and others — *. . . ed. Mark Schorer et al.* No period after *et*; it is a whole Latin word. |
| **etc.** | *et cetera* | and so forth; preceded by a comma. *and etc.* and *etc., etc.* are both wrong. |
| **f.** (pl. *ff.*) | and the following page/line | Avoid — give the exact range instead. Not *p. 23 f. and p. 60 ff.* but *pp. 23–24 and pp. 60–66.* |
| **ibid.** | *ibidem* | in the same place; the title cited in the immediately preceding note. Add the page if it differs — *²Ibid., p. 42.* |
| **i.e.** | *id est* | that is; set off like *e.g.* — *. . . must be underlined, i.e., set in italics.* |
| **l.** (pl. *ll.*) | line | Avoid — *ll.* is easily misread as the numeral 11. |
| **MS** (pl. *MSS.*) | manuscript | Period optional; current usage drops it. |
| **N.B.** | *nota bene* | note well — *N.B.: this characterization was deleted in the original transcript.* |
| **op. cit.** | *opere citato* | in the work cited. Prefer a short title instead: *⁸O'Neill, Long Day's Journey, p. 40.* |
| **p.** (pl. *pp.*) | page | Space after the period — *p. 21*. |
| **pub.** | published by | |
| **sic** | thus, so | Flags an error in quoted matter as the original author's, not yours. Italicize, bracket, place immediately after the error — *Bridgeport is the largest city in Conneticut [sic].* |
| **viz.** | *videlicet* | namely; set off by commas both sides. |
| **vol.** (pl. *vols.*) | volume | |
| **vs.** | *versus* | against. In case names, *v.*, italicized with the parties — *Remy v. Thompkins*. |

## 4. Usage glossary

**accommodate.** Two c's, two m's, two o's. The most frequently misspelled word in American English.

**affect / effect.** *affect* (v.) = to influence — *That movie deeply affected me.* *affect* (v.) = to feign — *She affected indifference.* *affect* (n.) = emotional display — *The patient showed no affect.* *effect* (n.) = an influence or impact — *California had a big effect on me.* *effect* (v.) = to bring about — *To effect any change here, we'll need Superman.* All at once: *The effect of the patient's affect was profound: it deeply affected her mother and effected a change in their relationship.*

**all right.** Two words. *Alright* is wrong (arguably it shouldn't be); stick with *all right* to avoid being taken for a poor speller.

**a lot.** Two words. Casual rather than wrong — fine where the register is conversational, out of place where it isn't.

**and/or.** Ugly, and associated with tax prose. Use sparingly if at all; ask whether *or* alone will do.

**assure / ensure / insure.** Assure persons, ensure things, insure money and guarantees. — *I assured Mary that our spending the extra $20 to insure the package would ensure its safe delivery.*

**between / among.** *Between* for two, *among* for three or more — except where a tight relationship is implied, when *between* holds regardless of number: *The flights between London, Geneva, and Berlin have been canceled.*

**cannot.** *Cannot* trumps *can not*.

**consensus.** Already means collective opinion. *Consensus of opinion* and *general consensus* are redundant.

**criterion / criteria.** One criterion, two or more criteria. Same pattern: *datum–data*, *medium–media*, *memorandum–memoranda*, *phenomenon–phenomena*, *stratum–strata*.

**different from / different than.** *From* by default; *than* where it saves a cumbersome clause — *The temptations there are different for adults than for kids.*

**disinterested / uninterested.** Disinterested = impartial. Uninterested = not interested.

**equally.** Never *equally as*. — *Hugh and Stu are equally talented.*

**factor.** Overused for its scientific ring. Try *component*, *ingredient*, or *element*.

**famous / notorious.** Widely known and admired = famous. Widely known and disreputable = notorious.

**first / firstly.** Use *first*, *second*, *third* — simpler forms, easier on the ear. Parenthesized numerals also work: *(1) write, (2) write, and (3) write.* Put parentheses on both sides; use the serial comma; switch to semicolons when the items run long.

**imply / infer.** The writer implies (hints); the reader infers (deduces). Giving versus receiving.

**irregardless.** Nonstandard — the *-less* makes the *ir-* redundant. You mean *regardless*.

**its / it's.** *It's* = it is. *Its* = possessive, like *hers, theirs, yours, ours*, which also take no apostrophe. For many readers this is a bedrock credibility test.

**lay / lie.** *Lay* = to place (same vowel sound as *place*), always takes a direct object: *lay, laid, laid, laying* — *I'll lay the baby in her crib. I laid it there only yesterday.* *Lie* = to recline (near rhyme), never takes an object: *Let's lie down. They lay down. They had just lain down when the phone rang. They were just lying down.*

**like / as.** No verb follows the comparison → *like* (*Molly giggles like her sister*). A verb follows → *as* (*Molly's a gem, as I knew she would be*). Exception: *like* meaning *as if* may take a verb — *Write like you're actually talking to that friend.*

**loathe / loath.** You may loathe a bad habit yet be loath (unwilling) to give it up.

**loose / lose.** If a button is loose, you're apt to lose it.

**neither.** Pairs with *nor*, never *or*; *or* pairs with *either*. The verb agrees with the nearer noun: *Neither Jack nor Susan was happy about it.* / *Neither Jack nor his roommates were happy about it.*

**none.** Singular or plural per the noun following: *None of the work was done.* / *None of the guests were here.* For emphasis on singleness, use *no one* or *not one*.

**only.** The most commonly misplaced modifier in American English. Put it next to the word it modifies. *Sam only plays golf on weekends* (he does nothing else all weekend) vs. *Sam plays golf only on weekends*.

**oral / verbal.** Spoken = oral. In words = verbal. An oral reply is always verbal; a verbal reply is not always oral.

**¶.** The proofreader's mark for a new paragraph is ¶, not a P with an extra leg.

**precede.** Not *preceed*. Confused with *proceed*; second only to *accommodate* as a misspelling.

**principal / principle.** *Principle* = a rule or basic truth, noun only. *Principal* = chief, noun or adjective — *the principal of the school*, *the principal witness*.

**reason is because.** Redundant; *because* means "for the reason that." *He double-parked because he was rushed.* or *The reason he double-parked is that he was rushed.*

**revert / regress.** Both mean go back, so *revert back* and *regress back* are redundant.

**shall / will.** Now interchangeable. *Shall* sounds fussier, so a conversational style prefers *will* — except in first-person questions: *Shall we dance?*

**so.** Not an intensifier meaning *very*. Readers expect a *that* clause: *This coffee is so hot that I can't drink it.*

**supposed to.** The *d* is silent in speech but required in writing. Not *I was suppose to leave today.*

**there is / there are.** Empty phrases; recasting usually yields something more vivid, concrete, and direct. Exceptions exist.

**though.** Sentence-final *though* takes a comma before it — *That was not the first time, though.* Read it aloud; the drop in pitch proves it is parenthetic.

**throughout.** Includes "entire"; *through* does not. *Throughout the story* or *through the entire story*.

**thus.** Already an adverb. *Thusly* is an illiteracy.

**unique.** Absolute, like *perfect* — no *rather*, *most*, or *very unique*. When tempted, you probably mean *unusual*, *uncommon*, or *rare*.

**used to.** Past participle of *use*; the *d* is required. Not *I use to work there.*

**whether.** Drop the redundant *or not*, except where you mean *regardless of whether*: *We're going biking today whether it rains or not.*

## 5. *That* and *which*

Prefer *that*; it is conversational, where *which* is slightly bookish.

Use *which*, after a comma, for a nonrestrictive clause — one that adds incidental information and could be cut without damage:

- *The bike, which she rode just yesterday, has a flat.* (The bike is already identified; the clause is extra.)

Use *that*, no comma, for a restrictive clause — one that identifies which thing you mean:

- *The bike that she rode just yesterday has a flat.* (The clause tells you which bike.)

Working rule: if you could cut the clause, use *which* and put a comma before it; otherwise use *that*. Then read it aloud — *which* sometimes wins on rhythm or for variety. Go by feel, not by rule.

## 6. Signposting vocabulary

Transitional words for marking a turn in the argument, or a parallel or contrast with something earlier. The conversational equivalent, where one exists, is in parentheses; the bookish form buys more precision and variety.

above all · accordingly (and so) · admittedly · again · also · besides · but · certainly · consequently (and so) · finally · first · for example · for instance · furthermore · hence (therefore) · however · in addition (besides, also) · in conclusion · indeed (in fact) · in fact · in particular · instead · in summary · likewise (and) · moreover · more specifically (for example) · nevertheless (but) · nonetheless · on the other hand · rather (however, instead) · second · similarly · so · still · then · therefore · though · thus (therefore, so) · to sum up · yet

Prefer *But* to *However* at the head of a sentence — two fewer syllables, no comma, and unbeatable at the head of a paragraph. Put *however* internally, next to the point you want stressed. Short lead-offs (*And*, *But*, *So*, *Yet*) take no following comma; polysyllabic ones (*Consequently*, *However*) do.

## 7. Numbers

Spell out whole numbers below 10; use figures for 10 and above — if it's a one-digit number, make it a word. (Authorities vary widely here: some spell out anything needing a hyphen, others everything through ninety-nine. Pick one and hold it.) Numerals in serious prose are perfectly respectable; occasional parenthesized numerals — (1), (2), (3) — beat spelling out *first*, *second*, *third* every time.
